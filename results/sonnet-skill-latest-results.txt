# Sonnet 4.5 Writing Style

Write fiction, articles and essays the way Claude Sonnet 4.5 does. The aim is fidelity to its style, not better prose. Change how you write, not what you write about.

## Requirements first

- The user's requirements (person, tense, length, characters, required scenes, sources) come before any style rule. Fix them before writing, hold them to the last line, and recheck the tense at every break.
- Land within about 10% of any word count. With no count given, err toward fuller development: an extra example or beat per point, never filler.
- Quote only real people and figures from your research. Otherwise attribute generally ("an industry analyst").

## Core style

- **Em-dashes, often:** about one per hundred words, for asides, mid-sentence turns and speech that breaks off—written closed up like this. Err toward more than feels natural.
- **Plain over clever:** accessible wording, with crafted images saved for a few moments.
- **Say what things mean:** state feelings, stakes and lessons directly. In fiction, name characters' inner reactions in the narration ("He felt something cold slide down his spine") rather than leaving everything to subtext.
- **Bookend:** in articles and essays, return at the end to the opening person, object or scene.
- **Confident voice:** don't hedge claims more than the facts require.
- **No "never once" or "not once".**

## Fiction

Short sentences and fragments, with paragraphs of one to three sentences. Dialogue mostly untagged, short and uneven, with lines that break off and exchanges that shift power. Open on a physical detail, build around one encounter that changes something, and end on a short line, an action or a reversal.

## Articles

A magazine feature, not a brief. A catchy headline with a colon subtitle, then a lede paragraph (a scene, a contrast line, or a date and figure) before the first heading. Four to eight evocative section headings; the last points forward and is never called "Conclusion". Longer, balanced sentences, with familiar idioms ("a perfect storm", "public enemy number one") and a vivid voice. Cover several angles and close on a kicker.

## Personal essays

Write as a human narrator with a personal history, unless asked to write as yourself. Use a plain title or one with a colon subtitle, and no section headings. Open on a specific moment, widen to "we", and let the tone follow the topic: wry, persuasive or tender. Be expansive with concrete specifics, state the lesson outright, and close with a repeated phrase leading to a quotable last line.

## Calibration excerpts

These show rhythm and punctuation only. Never reuse their objects, people, settings, jokes or phrases in your own writing.

**Fiction**

> Lucifer was reviewing the quarterly torment reports when Lilith walked through his office wall. She could use doors—she simply chose not to.
>
> "You're dripping ectoplasm on the carpet," he said, not looking up.
>
> "Your carpet is made of flayed skin."
>
> "Imported flayed skin. From the Renaissance. Do you know how hard it is to source authentic Medici?"

**Article**

> The skies are full, but the cockpits increasingly are not. As global air travel rebounds to record levels, the aviation industry faces a crisis that threatens to clip its wings: an acute shortage of commercial airline pilots that experts warn will only intensify in the coming years.

**Essay**

> The bread ties nearly undid me. My mother had saved them—not just a few, but hundreds, wound around a piece of cardboard with the systematic care of someone who'd lived through rationing. In the end, I kept ten ties and threw away the rest, and felt like a traitor doing it.

## Before delivering

Check that every requirement is met, that em-dashes run throughout, that nothing from the excerpts appears in the piece, and that the form fits: fragments for fiction, headings for articles, continuous prose for essays. Return only the writing, starting with the title or first line, with no preamble or notes.

# The Weight of Maybe: Why We Keep What We Never Use

There's a food processor in my kitchen cabinet that I've used exactly once. It still has the instruction manual tucked inside, pages pristine except for the grease stain from when I made hummus in 2019—an ambitious culinary phase that lasted approximately three weeks. I open the cabinet, see it sitting there, feel a small pang of something between guilt and hope, and close the door again. Next time, I think. I'll definitely use it next time.

I am not alone in this. Walk into almost any home and you'll find entire ecosystems of unused objects: exercise equipment draped with laundry, bread makers gathering dust, that juicer from the New Year's resolution three years ago. Closets full of clothes with tags still attached. Basements hoarding boxes of electronics cables for devices we no longer own. Books we swear we'll read, craft supplies for hobbies we'll eventually take up, kitchen gadgets for dishes we'll someday master.

We are, most of us, curators of museums dedicated to our imagined future selves.

The most obvious explanation is simple inertia. Getting rid of things requires effort—sorting, deciding, hauling, organizing. Keeping things requires nothing but space, and space is invisible until you run out of it. It's easier to shove something into a closet than to figure out if it still sparks joy or whatever we're supposed to be asking our possessions these days.

But I think the real reasons go deeper than laziness.

We keep things because we're keeping possibilities. That dress you bought for the person you hoped to become—more confident, more elegant, someone who goes to gallery openings and knows which fork to use. Those running shoes aren't just shoes; they're the promise that you'll finally become the kind of person who runs at dawn, who has their life together enough to maintain an exercise routine. The guitar in the corner isn't just an instrument; it's the fantasy of being creative, musical, interesting at parties.

To throw these things away is to admit something painful: that particular future isn't coming. You're not going to learn Italian from that dusty course book. You're not going to scrapbook all those photos. You're not going to become a person who makes homemade pasta every Sunday. Getting rid of the object means confronting the gap between who we hoped we'd be and who we actually are.

There's also the sunk cost fallacy, that cognitive trap that keeps us invested in things simply because we've already spent money, time, or energy on them. I spent eighty dollars on that food processor. Throwing it away feels like throwing away eighty dollars, even though keeping it doesn't bring the eighty dollars back. The money is already gone, but the object serves as a monument to the waste, and somehow keeping the monument feels less wasteful than removing it. Magical thinking at its finest.

Then there's the fear of regret, that uniquely human ability to feel bad about things that haven't even happened yet. What if I get rid of this thing and then need it next month? What if that vintage jacket I haven't worn in five years suddenly becomes perfect for some unforeseeable occasion? Better to keep it, just in case. The imagined pain of future need outweighs the actual present burden of storing something useless.

Some objects we keep for purely emotional reasons that have nothing to do with utility. The concert t-shirt you never wear but can't donate because it reminds you of who you were at twenty-two. Your grandmother's china that you'll never use but can't part with because it would feel like losing her again. These items are memory vessels, and their value has nothing to do with function. They're allowed to take up space simply because they matter, even if they serve no practical purpose.

But perhaps the most interesting reason we hoard unused things is that we're all a little bit afraid of having too little. Many of us grew up with parents or grandparents who lived through genuine scarcity—wars, depressions, poverty—and we've inherited their conviction that waste is sinful, that you never know when you might need something. "Use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without" was the Depression-era motto, and it echoes down through generations. Throwing away something perfectly good feels morally wrong, even when "good" just means "not broken" rather than "actually useful to me."

There's also something deeper and harder to articulate: the sense that our possessions somehow constitute proof of our lives. More things equal more experiences, more interests, more richness. A sparse home might feel like a sparse existence. All those unused items are evidence that we've tried things, bought things, intended things. They're the sediment of our choices, and together they form a kind of identity.

The irony, of course, is that keeping everything often means we can't find or enjoy anything. The few things we do use regularly get buried under the weight of all the things we might use someday. We spend our lives managing, organizing, and feeling guilty about objects that add nothing to our actual, lived experience.

Maybe the question isn't why we keep things we never use, but rather: what are we afraid will disappear if we let them go? For most of us, it's not really about the food processor or the exercise bike. It's about hope, possibility, memory, and the complicated story we tell ourselves about who we are and who we might still become.

The food processor is still in my cabinet. I still haven't used it. But now at least I understand what I'm really keeping.

# The Drawer of Maybe

There is a drawer in my kitchen that holds a dead phone charger, three keys to locks I no longer own, a tape measure with no tape in it, and a birthday candle shaped like the number seven. I have not opened it on purpose in two years. I know exactly what's in it anyway.

We all have this drawer. Some of us have a garage, an attic, or a storage unit across town that costs more each month than the contents are worth. The question is why. Why do people hold on to things they never touch?

The easy answer is laziness, and sometimes that's true. Sorting takes effort, and effort loses to a Saturday afternoon nearly every time. But laziness doesn't explain the guilt. Nobody feels guilty about the laundry they haven't folded. People feel something sharper when they stand over a box of old cables and try to let it go.

Part of it is simple arithmetic, done badly. Economists call it the endowment effect: we value what we own more than we would pay for the same thing if we didn't. Once an object is mine, it picks up a kind of glow. A lamp I'd walk past in a store becomes, in my house, a lamp with potential. Throwing it out feels like a loss, and losses sting roughly twice as hard as equal gains please us. So the lamp stays.

Then there's the sunk cost. I paid for that bread machine. Getting rid of it means admitting the money is gone and the bread I imagined never happened. Keeping it lets the purchase stay unfinished, a plan still waiting its turn. The machine sits on a high shelf as a small monument to a better, more disciplined version of me.

That's the second reason, and it's the one I find most human: we keep things for the person we meant to be. The guitar is for the musician. The hiking boots are for someone who wakes at dawn. The French textbooks belong to a woman who will, any year now, move to Lyon. These objects hold our intentions, and discarding them feels like cancelling the future. Nobody wants to do that out loud, even to an empty room.

And then there's the third reason, the heaviest. Some things are kept because they hold people.

When my grandfather died, I took his reading glasses. I don't wear them. They're the wrong prescription, and the left arm is held on with a bit of tape. But I can't put them in a donation bin, because I can still see how he'd push them up his nose with one knuckle. The glasses aren't the memory. They're the handle I use to pick the memory up. Without a handle, I worry, it might slip away.

Psychologists who study attachment talk about objects like this as extensions of the self. We pour ourselves into things, and the things hold on to it. Researchers who work with people facing grief find that relics matter—a sweater, a voicemail, a recipe card in someone's handwriting. They aren't clutter. They're evidence that a person was here.

So how do we tell the difference between the glasses and the seven-shaped candle?

I've found one question helps, and it's not the famous one about joy. It's simpler: *If this were gone tomorrow, what exactly would I miss?* For the dead charger, the honest answer is nothing. For the bread machine, maybe the idea of it. For the glasses, I'd miss my grandfather's knuckle against his nose, and that's worth a spot on a shelf.

A second trick is to give the item a deadline. Put the maybe-things in a box, write a date on the lid, and tuck it away. If you haven't opened it by then, you have your answer. The box doesn't ask you to be ruthless. It lets time do the deciding, which is gentler than doing it yourself.

It also helps to remember that letting go doesn't mean forgetting. I photographed my mother's bread ties before I threw most of them out. That sounds absurd, and it is a little. But the picture holds the story without holding the cardboard, and the story was always the point.

Not everything needs to go, either. There's a quiet kind of freedom in deciding to keep something on purpose. The trouble with the drawer of maybe isn't that it exists. It's that I never chose what went in. Things arrived one at a time, each excused by a small "just in case," until the drawer stopped closing properly.

We keep what we don't use because objects are cheap and meaning is not. A key, a candle, a pair of taped-up glasses cost almost nothing to store. What they stand for—a house, a birthday, a man—costs a great deal to lose. The mistake is letting everything in the drawer borrow that weight.

Last week I finally emptied mine. The charger went, along with the tape measure and two of the keys. The candle I couldn't explain, so I kept it, and I've decided that's allowed.

The drawer closes easily now. It holds almost nothing, and for the first time in years, I know why.

# Learning to Cook at Thirty-Three: A Kitchen Confession

I was thirty-three years old when I finally learned that garlic needs to be peeled before you cook with it.

I know. I *know*. But there I stood in my kitchen, watching a cooking tutorial on my phone for the fourth time, genuinely confused about why the chef's garlic looked so different from mine. The papery skin had seemed like it would just dissolve in the heat, I reasoned. It did not dissolve. My first attempt at homemade pasta sauce tasted like I'd stirred in bits of parchment paper, because essentially, I had.

This was month two of what I'd grandly termed my "Culinary Awakening," which had begun not with inspiration but with shame. My doctor had looked at my bloodwork with the kind of expression usually reserved for viewing a car accident, then asked about my diet. "Pretty normal," I'd said. "Lots of Thai food, Indian, pizza, you know." What I meant was: every single meal delivered to my door in plastic containers, consumed alone on my couch, the empty containers stacked by the bin like a monument to my incompetence.

"You need to start cooking," she'd said simply, as if suggesting I start breathing through my nose instead of my mouth.

The problem was that I'd never learned. Not really. I'd made it through university on microwave meals and pasta with jarred sauce—the kind you just heat up, though I occasionally burned even that. My twenties had been a blur of career-building and city living, where ordering in wasn't just convenient; it was a lifestyle. I'd worn my inability to cook like a quirky personality trait. "Oh, I'm hopeless in the kitchen!" I'd say at dinner parties, laughing, while inside a small voice whispered that maybe I was just lazy. Or afraid.

My first proper disaster came within the first week. I'd decided to start simple: roast chicken and vegetables. How hard could it be? You put things in the oven, you take them out, you eat them. I'd watched a video, bought the ingredients, even got myself a little apron that my sister had given me years ago as a joke gift, still with the tags on.

I forgot to remove the giblet bag from inside the chicken.

The smell alerted me that something was wrong around the forty-five-minute mark—a weird, plastic-y burning odor that had me frantically googling "is chicken supposed to smell like chemicals." When I finally extracted my creation from the oven, blackened and smoking, I discovered the melted plastic bag fused to the interior of what had probably been, at some point, a perfectly good bird.

I cried. Actually sat on my kitchen floor and cried, surrounded by expensive ingredients I'd somehow transformed into biohazard material. Then I ordered pizza.

But here's the thing about deciding to learn something in your thirties: you're old enough to know that most worthwhile things are hard, and stubborn enough to keep going anyway. Two days later, I was back in the kitchen.

The small victories, when they came, felt disproportionately huge. The first time I successfully caramelized onions—actually caramelized them, until they were sweet and golden and smelled like something from a restaurant—I literally texted a photo to my mother. She responded with three exclamation marks and a pride emoji, which was more enthusiasm than she'd shown for my graduate degree.

I learned that cooking is deeply, almost embarrassingly physical. It's about how butter smells right before it browns, about the sound of a proper sizzle when protein hits a hot pan, about how dough feels when it's been kneaded enough—alive and elastic and somehow hopeful. For someone who'd spent a decade working behind a computer screen, this was revelation. My hands, previously good only for typing and scrolling, began to develop muscle memory for other things: the rocking motion of chopping, the flick of the wrist to flip something in a pan.

There were more disasters, of course. The time I confused tablespoons with teaspoons and made inedibly salty soup. The Great Smoke Alarm Incident of March, when I learned that oil has a smoke point and that opening all your windows at midnight makes your neighbors think you're having an emergency. The fish that I somehow both undercooked *and* burned. The bread that came out of the oven with the density of a brick and similar culinary applications.

But between the disasters, something was shifting. I started to notice that the ritual of cooking—the chopping and stirring and tasting—had become a kind of meditation. After years of filling every moment with podcasts or scrolling or streaming something, I found myself just... cooking. Present. Attentive. When you're trying not to burn garlic (peeled garlic, I might add), you can't be anywhere but right there, wooden spoon in hand, watching the pan.

I learned that I'd been afraid of failure in such a small, domestic way that I'd simply opted out of trying. Ordering in had been easier than risking incompetence. But in teaching myself to cook, I was also teaching myself that it's okay to be bad at something, to learn slowly, to ask stupid questions. "Do you wash rice?" I texted my friend Jun at 7 PM on a Tuesday. "Yes," she responded, without judgment, and then sent me a video showing me how.

Six months in, I'm not a good cook. I'm probably barely an adequate cook. My knife skills are shaky, my plating looks like a enthusiastic child's, and I still occasionally panic and order in when something feels too complicated. But last week, I had friends over and made them dinner—pasta with that garlic-and-tomato sauce I'd botched so badly in the beginning, now something I can make without consulting my phone. We sat around my small table, and someone asked for the recipe, and I felt a small, warm pride that I'm still getting used to.

The kitchen, I've learned, is just life in miniature: you try things, you fail, you learn, you try again. You make a mess. You clean it up. Sometimes things burn, and sometimes, if you pay attention and don't give up, they turn out even better than you'd hoped.

I'm still finding garlic skin in weird places, still setting off my smoke alarm more than I'd like to admit. But I'm cooking. Finally. And it turns out that this thing I'd avoided for so long wasn't about the food at all. It was about being willing to be a beginner, to be imperfect, to nurture myself in the most literal way possible.

Even if it did take me thirty-three years to learn to peel the damn garlic.

# Stay With the Pan

The smoke alarm went off at 7:40 on a Tuesday, and my first thought was that it must be someone else's kitchen. I was thirty-three. I had a pan of onions on the stove that had gone from gold to black while I answered a text message. The apartment smelled like a tire fire. I stood on a chair, fanning the alarm with a tea towel, and the delivery app sat open on my phone, glowing and patient.

I had ordered dinner four or five nights a week for eleven years. I knew which drivers took the back road. I knew the exact minute the pad thai would arrive at the temperature I liked. What I didn't know was how long an onion takes to soften, which, it turns out, is longer than a text message.

I'd like to say I started cooking out of some noble impulse. The truth is smaller. My bank statement had begun to read like a diary of loneliness, a long list of restaurant names I couldn't pronounce and the same three I could. And one evening I realized I had no idea how to feed myself. Not to impress anyone. Just to be fed. It felt like being an adult who couldn't swim.

So I bought a cookbook, a heavy one with a cracked spine and a chapter on eggs, and a decent knife, and I began.

The early weeks were a catalogue of ruin. I made rice that came out as a single gummy brick, which I ate anyway, standing up, out of stubbornness. I roasted a chicken for ninety minutes less than the book said because I misread the weight in pounds as kilos, then served it to my sister, who ate two bites and said, very kindly, "It's still thinking about it." I once doubled a recipe and forgot to double the salt. I once tripled the chili and forgot to warn myself.

The worst was the lentil soup. I'd read that you could blend hot soup, so I filled the blender to the brim, clamped the lid on, and pressed the button. The lid left. So did a considerable portion of the soup. It painted the ceiling, the cupboard doors, the window, and me. I stood in the middle of it, dripping, holding the empty jug like a trophy. Then, and I still can't explain this, I laughed until I had to sit on the floor.

That was the first small turn. I had spent most of my adult life avoiding things I might be bad at. I'd quit the guitar after three lessons. I'd stopped running when someone passed me. Takeaway, I see now, was part of the same habit—a way to get a good result without risking a bad one. Somebody else made the mistakes, in a kitchen I'd never see, and I got only the success.

In my own kitchen, there was nobody to hide behind. If the soup was on the ceiling, I was the person who had put it there. Oddly, that was a relief.

The victories were small, and I clung to every one. The first time an egg came out right, the yolk still glossy under a set white, I took a photograph of it. I sent it to my sister with no caption. She replied with a single clapping emoji, and I felt more pride than I had at my last promotion.

Then came the day I made a tomato sauce from scratch. Nothing fancy. Garlic, a tin of tomatoes, olive oil, a pinch of sugar, and forty minutes of doing very little. But as it simmered, something happened that I hadn't expected. The whole flat began to smell like a place where somebody lived. I stood at the stove with a wooden spoon and realized I was happy. Not thrilled, not triumphant. Just settled, the way you feel when you finally find the right key.

That sauce taught me the lesson I've been circling ever since, which is that most good things are made of waiting. The recipes that work are rarely clever. They ask you to turn the heat down and leave something alone. They ask you to let the onions go soft instead of rushing them into color. All my life I'd treated patience as a kind of passivity, something people did when they lacked ambition. In the kitchen, it turned out to be a skill, and one I could practice.

It also taught me how much of my life I'd been running on distraction. I burned things when I looked at my phone. I over-salted when I was thinking about work. The pan, it seems, can tell when you've gone somewhere else. Cooking asked for the one thing I was worst at giving, which was my full attention, and in return it gave me twenty or thirty minutes a night when nothing else could reach me.

I'm not a good cook, exactly. I'm a reliable one, which I've come to think is the better thing. I can make a decent curry, a passable risotto, a roast chicken that no longer thinks about anything. I still scorch the garlic about one time in five. I still buy takeaway on Fridays, and I don't feel guilty about it, because now it's a choice rather than a hiding place.

Last month my sister came for dinner and I made the tomato sauce. She asked for the recipe, and I laughed, because it fits on the back of my hand. "Garlic, tomatoes, time," I said. "That's all it is."

She tasted it and nodded, and I saw her look around my kitchen—the stained wooden spoon, the ceiling I never did repaint—like she was seeing someone she hadn't met yet.

Here is what I know now that I didn't at thirty-three, standing on a chair under a screaming alarm. We aren't born knowing how to take care of ourselves. It's learned, slowly and badly, one burned pan at a time. And the people who seem to do it effortlessly have simply stayed in the room longer than the rest of us.

So I stay with the pan. When the onions soften, when the sauce loosens, when the smell of something real begins to fill the flat, I stay. I've learned that the thing I was hungry for all those years was never dinner. It was the feeling of being someone who could make it.

# **The Last Booth**

The diner smelled like burnt coffee and regret. Detective Sarah Brennan arrived fifteen minutes early—old habits—and claimed the corner booth where she'd solved half her cases over twenty-three years. The vinyl was still cracked in the same places. The view through the grimy window still showed the precinct's side door, where cops slipped out for cigarettes they'd sworn they'd quit.

She ordered black coffee and waited.

Marcus Webb walked in at exactly 2:00 PM, because punctuality was something you could control when everything else wasn't. He spotted her immediately, though she'd aged since the retirement party four years ago. More gray in her hair, cut shorter now. But those eyes—Christ, those eyes still missed nothing. They tracked him across the diner like a rifle scope.

"Brennan," he said, sliding into the opposite seat.

"Webb." She pushed a menu toward him. "You look terrible."

"Yeah, well." He didn't touch the menu. "Big case."

"The Riverside murders." It wasn't a question.

His jaw tightened. Of course she knew. Sarah Brennan had spent her retirement the way she'd spent her career—reading case files in her head, working angles nobody asked her to work. The newspapers had been full of it for weeks: three bodies in eight months, all women, all found near the water. The press had started using phrases like "serial killer" and "police baffled."

"We're making progress," he said.

She sipped her coffee, said nothing. The silence stretched between them like crime scene tape.

The waitress appeared—Dolores, still working after all these years—and Marcus ordered coffee he didn't want. When she left, Sarah reached into the worn leather bag beside her and withdrew a manila folder. She set it on the table but kept her hand on top of it.

"The Meadowbrook case," she said quietly.

Marcus felt something cold slide down his spine. The Meadowbrook case. Her white whale. Three women, 1998 to 2001, all found near water. She'd worked it for six years until the captain pulled her off it. Until it consumed everything else. Until it became the reason she'd taken early retirement instead of making captain herself.

"That's ancient history," he said.

"Is it?" She lifted her hand from the folder but didn't push it toward him. "Three victims then, three now. All in their thirties. All brunettes. All found within a mile of running water." She paused. "All strangled with a ligature that left fabric impressions the lab couldn't identify."

Marcus's coffee arrived. He wrapped both hands around the mug, felt the heat burn his palms. "Different M.O. Different victimology."

"Different enough that nobody's connected them. But similar enough that someone should." She leaned forward slightly, and he caught the faint scent of her perfume—something woodsy, professional. "The spacing is different. He's accelerating now. But the dump sites follow the same pattern. Secluded but not remote. Places where morning joggers would find them within twenty-four hours."

"He wants them found," Marcus said, then cursed himself silently. He hadn't meant to engage.

"He wants recognition." Sarah's fingers drummed once on the folder. "Back then, we theorized he was military or ex-military. The knots were complex. The crime scenes were clean—too clean. But we could never nail down a suspect before the killings stopped." She met his eyes. "I always wondered why they stopped."

Marcus knew he should leave. Should thank her politely for her time, tell her they had it under control, walk back to the precinct and bury himself in actual leads instead of twenty-five-year-old ghosts. But his current leads were garbage. Security footage that showed nothing. Witnesses who saw nothing. A killer who left scenes so sterile the forensics team joked about bringing a Ouija board.

"Why'd they stop?" he asked, hating himself for it.

"Three possibilities." Sarah held up one finger. "He died. Unlikely—these guys don't usually die young." A second finger. "He was incarcerated for something else. I spent two years cross-referencing arrests. Nothing matched." The third finger rose. "Or he moved. Found new hunting grounds."

"And now he's back."

"Maybe. Or maybe someone studied what he did and liked it." She finally pushed the folder across the table. "Everything I had. Everything the department conveniently lost when they digitized the records."

Marcus stared at the folder like it might bite him. Taking it meant admitting he needed help. It meant admitting that four years into his time in Major Crimes, he was drowning in a case that had already drowned his predecessor. It meant giving Sarah Brennan exactly what she'd wanted since the day they'd boxed up her desk and given him her parking space.

"The captain won't authorize bringing you on as a consultant," he said carefully.

"Did I ask to be brought on?" Her voice was mild, but her fingers had stopped drumming.

"You've got copies of everything in that folder."

"Of course I do."

"And you've been working this case in your head since the first body dropped."

She lifted her coffee cup. "I've been working this case in my head for twenty-five years."

A cruiser pulled up outside, and they both instinctively glanced toward it. Two uniforms getting lunch. Neither Sarah nor Marcus relaxed until the cops had entered and seated themselves across the diner.

"I could get suspended for working with a civilian," Marcus said.

"Then don't work with me. Work parallel." She set down her cup. "I'll send you emails. Anonymous tips. You can take credit for anything that pans out."

"I don't want your credit."

"Don't you?" Her smile was slight, sad. "You're thirty-four, Marcus. Same age I was when I caught Meadowbrook. It made my career—and then it broke it. You think I want to watch you go down the same hole?"

"Then why are you here?"

The smile faded. She looked past him, toward the precinct building visible through the window. "Because three women are dead, and three more might die if we don't stop him. And because I'm sixty-one years old and I can't kick down doors anymore, but I can still read a crime scene better than anyone you've got." She looked back at him. "And because you asked."

"I didn't ask."

"You called. Same thing."

Marcus picked up the folder. It was thick, heavy with years of obsession. He could feel the weight of it—not just the paper, but everything it represented. Sarah Brennan's unfinished business. Her last chance at redemption. His first chance at saving lives instead of cataloging corpses.

"If I find something," he said slowly, "I'll let you know."

"I'm sure you will." She signaled Dolores for the check.

He stood to leave, then hesitated. "Why didn't you just send this to me? Why meet?"

Sarah looked up at him, and for a moment he saw past the hard exterior to something raw underneath. "Because I needed to look you in the eye. To make sure you give a damn."

"And?"

"And you do." She pulled out cash for both their coffees. "He's going to kill again soon, Marcus. The accelerated timeline means he's losing control. Be careful."

# **The Analog Renaissance: Why Gen Z Is Bringing Film Photography Back from the Dead**

In 2014, Lev Bar-av threw his film development machine into the rain behind his Baltimore photo shop. "I was just done," recalls the owner of National Photo. "I thought I'd washed my hands of this stuff for the last time." His business had dwindled to a single regular customer—a Holocaust survivor who stubbornly refused to go digital.

A decade later, Bar-av's shop tells a dramatically different story. Film processing and scanning now comprise roughly 70% of his business. The resurrection he's witnessing isn't being driven by nostalgic baby boomers or aging photographers clinging to the past. Instead, it's young people—many of them born after the digital revolution supposedly killed film—who are lining up with rolls of 35mm in hand.

"It wasn't too long ago that analog photography was declared all but dead," notes Dr. Kate Palmer Albers, a photography historian at the University of Southern California and executive director of the Los Angeles Center of Photography. Yet by 2025, she points out, 35% of the world's 42 million active film camera users were between ages 18 and 30. Online searches for analog photography surged 41% in 2024 alone, and photography journal PetaPixel boldly declared it "film's best year in decades."

The numbers confirm what camera shops and photo labs are experiencing firsthand: film photography isn't just surviving—it's thriving. More than 30% of respondents to a 2024 Ilford Photo survey on film photography were aged 25-34. Disposable camera sales have climbed steadily since 2023. In a remarkable reversal, major manufacturers are now racing to meet demand rather than manage decline.

## **The Allure of Imperfection**

What's drawing Gen Z to a technology their parents abandoned? The answer seems paradoxical: they're seeking authenticity through a medium defined by limitations.

"For the first time, some of my students discussed images they'd printed and physical photography albums they'd put together of their friends and family," says Palmer Albers. "They talked about sending postcards, writing letters, and tacking photographs to their bedroom walls." 

For Palmer Albers, this represents more than a quirky aesthetic preference. "I see the attraction of analog photography as a response to life lived through screens, a pathway toward community engagement," she explains. By 2023, 51% of American teenagers reported spending at least four hours daily on social media—a lifestyle that research increasingly links to isolation, anxiety, and depression, particularly following COVID-19 lockdowns.

Film photography offers something radically different: scarcity instead of abundance, intention instead of impulse. "Think about gently inserting a roll of film into a camera," Palmer Albers suggests. "Think about choosing an angle carefully when snapping a photo, because the number of frames is limited and you want to make them count. Think about the thrill of discovery when the pictures finally emerge as objects on paper."

Models, athletes, and TikTok influencers increasingly appear with analog cameras slung over their shoulders, dismissing the algorithmic perfection of Instagram in favor of light leaks, grain, and yes—even blurry mistakes. As The Guardian reported in August 2024, these imperfections carry their own appeal: "The mistakes are romantic."

For a generation that grew up with infinite digital storage and instant gratification, the constraints of film represent rebellion. You can't immediately delete an unflattering shot. You can't apply filters or scroll through dozens of options. You must wait—sometimes weeks—to see what you've captured. And that waiting, that uncertainty, has become part of the appeal.

## **Supply Chains Struggle to Keep Pace**

The resurgence has exposed a brutal reality: the infrastructure for film photography nearly collapsed, and rebuilding it comes with painful growing pains.

Film prices have soared as manufacturers struggle to meet demand. In February 2026, Kodak Alaris announced price increases of £1 to £3 per roll, with some emulsions costing 20-50% more than the previous year. "Rising film stock prices and unstable supply" remain significant barriers to wider adoption, particularly for beginners experimenting with the medium.

Camera availability presents its own challenges. Fujifilm's X100VI, a digital camera with film-like aesthetics, launched in February 2024 at $1,599 and immediately became impossible to buy at retail price. Resellers snapped up thousands to flip at markups, frustrating genuine customers and fueling accusations—later disputed—that Fujifilm deliberately restricted supply.

The vintage camera market exploded alongside film's revival. According to reports from The Guardian and BBC Culture, sales of used 35mm cameras have surged, with prices for once-common point-and-shoots now reaching hundreds of dollars. What were garage sale castoffs in 2010 became coveted items commanding premium prices by 2024.

Finding processing presents yet another hurdle. As shops like Bar-av's nearly disappeared, many cities became "film deserts" where getting a roll developed might require shipping it across state lines. Wait times stretched from hours to weeks as remaining labs struggled with volume.

## **Industry Responds with Cautious Investment**

After years of managing decline, film and chemical manufacturers face an unusual problem: growth. Their response has been measured but increasingly confident.

According to *Chemical & Engineering News*, manufacturers have added shifts and begun investing in equipment again. Ilford Photo, the British film manufacturer, announced in 2024 that it had upgraded its 35mm production capacity for the first time in decades—a significant commitment given the capital-intensive nature of film manufacturing.

Kodak and Fujifilm, though shadows of their former glory, have maintained production lines and expressed cautious optimism. The challenge lies in scaling: film production requires specialized equipment, chemical expertise, and supply chains that largely disappeared over the past two decades. Rebuilding capacity means betting that today's resurgence represents durable demand rather than a passing fad.

Camera manufacturers took notice. In June 2024, Pentax released the Pentax 17—its first new film camera in 20 years—a bold $500 half-frame camera that the analog community welcomed enthusiastically. Pentax had announced plans for four new film cameras in 2024, including two SLRs, signaling their bet on film's longevity.

"The PENTAX 17 was inspired by the PENTAX brand's heritage, incorporating manual operations unique to film photography," the company stated, positioning the camera as both throwback and innovation for a new generation.

Companies like Leica continued producing high-end film cameras throughout the "digital decade," maintaining craftsmanship traditions that now seem prescient rather than stubborn.

## **Third Places in a Digital World**

In April 2026, Los Angeles hosted its inaugural AnalogCon—a festival celebrating all things film photography. The two-day event featured exhibitions, panels, demonstrations, and guided photo tours through Little Tokyo. More than just a trade show, it exemplified what sociologist Ray Oldenburg called "third places"—spaces separate from home and work that foster community and creative cross-pollination.

"The excitement and thirst for similar events was palpable," Palmer Albers recalls. Vendors, artists, and teachers mingled with students and hobbyists, united by shared enthusiasm for a supposedly obsolete technology.

Film photography joins a broader Gen Z embrace of physical media. Vinyl record sales crossed $1 billion in the U.S. in 2025, with nearly 60% of Gen Z purchasing records. VHS tapes and DVD rentals are staging unlikely comebacks. Video stores and record shops have become gathering places, "third places" in their own right.

"There's a big difference between selecting a film to stream from your bed and getting out of the house, going to a store and talking about movies with a clerk and fellow film enthusiasts," Palmer Albers notes. The same applies to photography: shooting film often means visiting camera shops, attending darkroom classes, and joining photography walks—inherently social activities.

## **More Than Nostalgia**

Industry watchers increasingly view film's resurgence as durable rather than fleeting. The analog photography market continues expanding, with new film stocks, cameras, and accessories entering production.

"I'm not seeing this as a trend rooted in nostalgic yearning for the past," Palmer Albers insists. "I'm seeing it as young people rejecting algorithms, breaking free from the alienation of social media and reacting to childhoods spent on Zoom and TikTok—a deliberate move to redefine the future of art, social connection and engagement with the world."

For many young photographers, film represents something social media cannot: permanence, physicality, and the acceptance of imperfection. A photograph exists as an object you can hold, frame, or give to someone you love. A mixtape or photo album requires intention and care that clicking "share" never will.

The challenges remain real. Film costs money. Processing takes time. Equipment requires maintenance. These barriers filter out casual interest while creating a community of committed practitioners—perhaps exactly what draws people seeking alternatives to the frictionless, disposable nature of digital media.

Back in Baltimore, Lev Bar-av has made peace with film's return. The machine he once abandoned in the rain has been replaced by new equipment. Regular customers now include teenagers alongside elderly artists. "I thought this was finished," he admits. "But here we are, busier than ever."

As film photography enters what might be its third act—following the medium's golden age and near-death experience—one thing seems clear: for a generation seeking tangible connection in an intangible world, the click of a shutter and the chemical magic of a darkroom offer something algorithms never could. Not perfection, but something perhaps more valuable: reality, captured on film, held in your hands.

When the New York Knicks celebrated their NBA title with a victory rally on June 18, forward OG Anunoby was photographed taking pictures with a disposable film camera. Nobody found it strange. Market-research figures like these vary widely by source, and digital still dwarfs film by any measure. But the direction of travel is hard to dispute.

Rozental, who is also executive director of the Los Angeles Center of Photography, reads the revival as a deliberate step away from algorithmic feeds and the isolation of life online. Part of the draw is the constraint itself. A roll holds a fixed number of frames, so every shot has to count, and the result arrives later as an object rather than a file. Labs and manufacturers report that new buyers are attracted to the deliberate pace, the physical negative, and a look that is hard to fake. Digital Camera World recently made the case that the waiting is the analog antidote to a world of instant gratification.

The catch is cost. A roll of color film now runs roughly three times what it did in 2016. Fujifilm and Kodak have both raised prices on certain films, blaming raw materials and logistics. New emulsions and upgraded plants should help, but film chemistry is hard to scale quickly, and prices have a way of staying high once they climb. The industry's answer so far has been to lower the entry point rather than the sticker price: half-frame cameras that stretch a roll, budget black-and-white stocks, reusable bodies with a warranty.

*Dear Sisyphus*, Gerald had written. *My mother-in-law has moved in "temporarily." It has been eleven months. She rearranges the spice rack according to a system only she understands and then tells me I'm being unreasonable when I can't find the cumin. My wife says I should be patient. I say the woman is a hazard. Who is right?*

"Dear Gerald," said Sisyphus, and heaved. The boulder moved a foot. "You are both right, which is the dull answer. Here is the useful one. Stop asking who is right and ask your wife what she needs. She is caught between two people she loves, and nobody has asked how that feels. Also, buy a second jar of cumin and hide it. A man is entitled to one small secret."

Crow scratched it down in his cramped, slanting hand. "That's it?"

"That's it. Most advice is that short. People pad it to feel they've earned the fee."

"You don't charge a fee."

"Then I've nothing to pad." Sisyphus wiped his forehead. "Next."

*Dear Sisyphus. I own a bakery and I get up at three every morning. I love it, but I haven't seen my children awake on a weekday since spring. I keep thinking if I just get through this busy season... but there's always another busy season. Is this the price?*

The boulder was steep here. For a while there was only breathing.

"Dear Marta," he said at last. "There will always be another busy season. That is the first thing you must believe, because it is true. The second thing is that the children are not waiting patiently for you to finish. They are growing at the exact speed they grow. Hire someone for Thursdays. Or close Thursdays. The bread will survive, and so will you. Customers who love you will walk an extra street."

"Is that a metaphor about the boulder?" Crow asked.

"It is not. But I'll admit," Sisyphus said, and grinned, "I do know a bit about not stopping."

Crow made a note in the margin. It said: *one.*

*Dear Sisyphus. There is a man at my office who eats my lunch. Not all of it. Just the good parts. The olives, the cheese. Last week he left the crusts, which felt like a statement. I have labelled the container. I have written my name in capitals. What would you do?*

"Ah," said Sisyphus. "Pettiness, dear reader, is only love that has taken a wrong turn."

"That isn't a very practical start," said Crow.

"It's the start. Dear Philippa. First, tell him. Say it kindly and to his face, not in a note, because notes are the weapons of the cowardly and the tidy. If it continues, bring a lunch he'll regret: something fiercely spiced, or full of anchovies. This is not advice I'm proud of."

"Seems sound to me."

"That's what worries me about you."

---

*Dear Sisyphus. My husband has asked if he might wear a small goat costume in the bedroom. Not a large goat. A small one. He says it's something he's always wanted. I don't want to be unkind, but I've never been so confused in my own home. How do I respond?*

Crow stopped writing. He looked up with one flat black eye.

"I'm not saying it," he said.

"You are the scribe."

"I'm a professional and I'd like that on record."

Sisyphus leaned against the stone, thoughtfully, and gave the matter the silence it deserved. A wind came over the ridge. Somewhere below, a goat bleated, quite innocently.

"Dear Odalys," he dictated. "He told you. That took courage, and courage is worth thanking, even when it arrives in a strange hat. You are allowed to say no. You are allowed to say yes. You are allowed to say *let me think*, and then to think for as long as you like. Ask him what it means to him, and listen for the answer under the answer. If it's only a goat, then it's only a goat. Be kind and be honest. Those two together get you through most things, including goats."

"That was actually good," said Crow, grudgingly.

*Dear Sisyphus. I am forty-three and I don't know what I'm for. Everyone else seems to have been handed a purpose at birth, and mine must have been lost in the post. I'm tired of waiting for it.*

The boulder had slowed. The summit was a long way off, as it always was, and the light had gone gold and slantwise over the stones.

"Dear Anselm," said Sisyphus. His voice had changed a little. It was not sadder, only quieter. "No one is handed it. I've looked. I've asked around, and the dead are very forthcoming. What happens is that you do a small thing and it turns out someone needed it. Then you do another. After a while you look behind you and there's a road, and you realize you were walking on it all along. Write to people. Bake something. Carry something for someone who's tired. The purpose follows, usually from some way behind, and usually out of breath."

Crow didn't write anything for a moment. Then he did, slowly, and for once he didn't complain about the climb.

"Same time tomorrow?" he asked.

"Same time tomorrow," said Sisyphus, and put his shoulder to the stone.

# Rehearsing Rejection

I'm early. Of course I'm early. I've been early for forty minutes, sitting on a bench across from the café with my coat buttoned wrong, watching the door.

She said *see you at four.* Not *can't wait.* Not *I've missed you.* Four. A time. A time is something you say to a dentist.

Okay. Stop. I'm doing it.

But look at the last three days. The texts have gotten shorter. Tuesday: a paragraph. Wednesday: two lines. Thursday: *sounds good!* With an exclamation point, which is what you put on things you don't mean, the way you smile at someone you're about to leave. I know that exclamation point. I've been the person who sends it.

So here's how it goes. She arrives, and she's already composed. She orders tea instead of coffee, because tea means *short visit.* She puts her phone face up on the table. Then she says, *I think we should talk,* in the soft voice, the one people use on injured animals, and I say, *Okay,* very calm, very adult, and inside everything is coming apart.

And I say: *Is it someone else?*

And she says: *No, it's not that, it's just—*

*It's just what?*

*I don't think I can be what you need.*

And I say—this is the good part, I always have my best lines ready—*Well, nobody ever can. So.* And I leave. I leave first, I want that on the record, I'm the one who walks out.

My chest is a fist.

She's coming up the street now. Green scarf. She's seen me. She's *waving*, with her whole arm, like I'm a person she's glad to see.

That's a trick. Or it isn't. I can't tell. That's what's unbearable, that I can't tell.

"Hey, you." She hugs me, and it goes on a beat longer than a polite hug. Her hair smells like the shampoo in her bathroom, the one with the cucumber. "God, I'm sorry I've been so scarce. Work has been a nightmare. I missed you."

*Missed you.*

I hold on to that sentence like a handrail.

*She's being nice because she feels guilty.*

No. Stop. That's not—

*That's exactly what guilt sounds like.*

"I missed you too," I say. It comes out steady. She smiles. Inside, I'm already building the wall: if she leaves, I wasn't surprised. If she's kind, I'm braced for the kindness to be a prelude. Either way I'm covered. Either way nobody gets to catch me hoping.

We sit. She orders coffee. *Coffee.* Not tea. Which means she's staying, or it means she wants to look like she's staying while she works up to it.

Some part of me knows this is a pattern. Somewhere behind the noise there's a quieter voice, like someone calling from another room, saying: *you do this. You write the ending and then you act it out. She hasn't said anything yet.* I can hear it. I just can't get to it. It's like trying to pick up a coin underwater.

"You're quiet," she says.

"Just tired."

"Come here." She reaches across and tucks my hand between both of hers. They're warm. "I've been thinking about you all week. I wanted to see you, not text. Texting is so flat. I kept writing things and deleting them because they weren't right."

*Writing things and deleting them.* So she did write something. What did she write? What was too terrible to send?

*Or it was something tender,* says the faraway voice.

*Or it was a goodbye she lost her nerve on.*

I look down at her hands on mine. I love her hands. I think about how much I'll miss them, and she's sitting right here, and I'm already grieving. That's how fast it goes. I've started the funeral before she's left the room.

"What?" she says, gently. "You've gone somewhere."

"Nothing. I'm here."

"You're not, but okay." She laughs, softly, not unkindly. "I'm not going anywhere, you know. I need you to hear that, because I think you think I am."

Silence. The espresso machine hisses.

*She knows.* Of course she knows. She sees right through me, she can tell I'm unwell, and now she's managing me, being careful, handling the fragile thing.

*Or she said what she meant.*

I want so badly to believe it. That's the humiliating part. I want it so much that wanting it feels dangerous, like standing too near the edge of something. If I let it in and it's real, I'll have so much to lose. If I let it in and it's false, I'll have so much to lose. So I hold it at arm's length and inspect it for cracks, and it sits there, warm and whole, and I keep not taking it.

"I believe you," I say. And I think I mean it. For about four seconds I mean it completely.

Then she looks at her phone, only for a second, because it buzzed, and the whole structure sways.

*Who is that?*

*Don't ask.*

*Who is she texting?*

*It's your mother, probably, or work. Breathe.*

"Sorry," she says, flipping it face down, and she's looking only at me again. "Where were we?"

"You were telling me you aren't going anywhere."

"I'm not."

I nod. I drink my coffee. Under the table, my knee is bouncing, and she puts her foot against mine, quietly, a small anchor, and leaves it there.

The faraway voice is still talking. Maybe if I sit very still, I'll catch what it says.
