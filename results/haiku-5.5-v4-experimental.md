## 1. The Azra Gambit

The transfer vessel Persephone's Lantern had been built for diplomats, which meant it was built for comfort rather than speed, and Arthur Neegan had spent the first eleven hours of the journey trying not to think about how much of that comfort was paid for by the ore pits he had left behind. Through the curved viewport, Mars was a rust-coloured disc going slowly pale against the stars. Somewhere beneath that dust, four hundred and twelve contract labourers were digging out the stuff that had made his little plot of ground worth more than his entire life's earnings.

He had been polite to the men who came for him. He had made them tea, which they had declined, and he had let them wait while he packed a single bag, which they had not expected. The insignia on their collars had been the twin arcs of the Antares Diplomatic Corps, and the Corps, as every schoolchild on Mars knew, was diplomatic in the same way a cat was a dog.

The cabin door slid open with a sigh of hydraulics. A young attaché in grey stood aside.

"Mr. Neegan. The Consul will see you now."

The room beyond was smaller than Arthur had expected and far more expensive. Walnut panels, real walnut, the kind that had not grown on any world he had ever set foot upon. A single lamp burned over a table set for two. A man sat on the far side of it, silver-haired and wearing a jacket cut so precisely that Arthur suspected the tailor had been obliged to measure the Consul's soul as well as his shoulders.

"Sit, please," said the Consul. "You're bracing as though I might shoot you. I assure you, the weapons in this room are for decoration."

"I wasn't aware there were any weapons in this room."

"Precisely." The man smiled. "I am Teodor Marrin. I represent interests that I am not permitted to name in full, though you may find my name uncomfortably familiar by the time we finish. Have some wine. It's from Ceres, and it's terrible, but it's the only thing the Corps imports without a tariff."

Arthur sat. He did not drink. "I'm told I've been summoned."

"Invited. There's a difference, though I admit the difference is mostly in the paperwork." Marrin poured for himself. "You've been busy, Mr. Neegan. Your survey of Sector Nine turned up an Azra vein of extraordinary density. Your company's claim office filed the report on the same afternoon. Within six days, three other claim offices had requested copies. Within nine, a freight broker in the Belt tried to buy your lease through an intermediary whose intermediary turned out to be a dead man. You have made a great many people very interested in you."

"I'm a mining engineer. I dig holes. Interest is a side effect."

"Azra is not a side effect of anything, Mr. Neegan. It is the principal event." Marrin turned his glass slowly. "Do you know what it does?"

"It accelerates radioactive decay. It makes certain processes efficient enough to power a drive that doesn't need a fuel mass. And it makes a warhead the size of a fist that can turn a city into a rumour."

"Yes. The first part is what interests the navies. The second part is what interests the people who pay the navies." Marrin leaned back. "The sun is four billion years old, Mr. Neegan, and it has never once been rushed. Azra is the first substance humanity has found that will hurry things. You are sitting on a hill of it, and the hill has become the centre of the solar system's attention."

The lamp hummed. Outside, a line of cargo tugs drifted past the viewport, their running lights blinking in patient sequence.

"What do you want from me?" Arthur asked.

"Only the thing you already have." Marrin set down his glass. "Your claim is registered under a Martian charter, which means it is registered under the law of a colony that has not been independent for very long. The Corps would prefer that the claim be transferred to a holding company operated from Antares Station. The holding company would lease the vein back to you as chief engineer, at a salary that I am instructed to say is generous, and which I personally think is insulting."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then nothing happens to you. Not immediately." Marrin's voice did not change. "Your claim office will receive a notice of review. Your lease will be examined under a clause in the Pioneer Act that nobody has invoked in forty years. Your neighbours will discover that their water rights depend on your signature. Your mother, if I am not mistaken, still lives on Ophir Ring Four?"

Arthur felt the blood leave his face and then, almost at once, come back hot. "That's a threat."

"It's a description. I'm trying to be honest with you, which I suspect is a novelty in this room." Marrin poured himself more wine and did not drink it. "Mr. Neegan, the men who sent me are not villains. They are merely impatient. You are a small man standing in front of an enormous door, and the door is opening whether you hold it or not. The only question is whether you walk through with a contract in your pocket."

"And if I walk through without one?"

"Then the door will still open. But you will be on the other side of it with nothing but the clothes you wear, and that would be a shame, because you have a good mind, and I would hate to see it wasted in a ditch on Ceres." Marrin's smile returned, thin as a wire. "Think about it. You have until we dock at Antares. The wine will still be terrible when you return."

Arthur stood. His legs felt unfamiliar, like someone else's borrowed for the occasion. At the door he paused.

"Consul."

"Yes?"

"The mine. Four hundred men are digging that vein right now, under contracts that aren't worth the paper they're printed on. If this goes through, do they get anything?"

Marrin studied him for a long moment, and for the first time the expression on his face was not one of calculation.

"Mr. Neegan," he said quietly, "that is the only question in this room I would not have been able to answer for you."


## 2. Darkroom Revelations

The safelight is the colour of the inside of your eyelids after you've been staring at the sun, and after three hours under it I've stopped noticing that everything in here is red. The enlarger ticks as it cools. The stop bath smells like vinegar that's given up. Elliot is at the far end of the sink with his sleeves shoved past the elbow, sliding a print through the developer with a patience I have never once possessed.

He's wearing the velvet coat again. Black, a little moth-eaten at one cuff, eleven dollars at the Goodwill on Chestnut. The lining is torn and he refuses to fix it because he says it looks like a history. Silver rings on three fingers. In this light his hair is nearly black, the bleached ends gone the colour of rust.

"You can say it," he says, without looking up. "I can hear you thinking."

"I wasn't thinking anything."

"You were thinking I've been strange all week."

I was. For fifteen weeks I've watched him in Experimental Photo, shooting everything from the floor, dropping his voice to almost nothing whenever Professor Adair asks him something he'd rather not answer. I told myself I was interested in his work. I'm a terrible liar, but I'm an excellent editor of my own motives, and I edited that story so many times it barely resembled the truth.

"Fine," I say. "You've been strange all week."

He laughs, a small surprised sound, and then he's quiet for long enough that I hear the water running in the wash tank behind him.

"I told my mum on Sunday," he says.

I put down my loupe. I know better than to rush him, but the rushing is already in my hands. I've always been the one who steers. In bars, in bed, in the seminar where I ask the question nobody else wants to ask. Here I'm learning to sit with my hands flat on the counter.

"Told her what?"

"That I don't think I'm a boy. Or that I don't think I'm only one." He pulls the print out of the developer and tilts it so the image slides. A woman's bare shoulder, a streetlight blurred into a smear of gold. "She said she already knew. She said she was waiting for me to get there on my own, which is somehow worse."

"Worse how?"

"Because she was right."

I want to reach across the sink and take his wrist. I want to tell him he's brave, and then I want to hear how brave sounds in my own mouth, and I hate that I want that. I've always been drawn to a certain kind of man. Square jaws, easy confidence, the sort who holds a door and knows exactly where he's going. Elliot has none of that. His shoulders curl forward when he's unsure. He asks permission before he touches anything, including my camera. And every time he says something true about himself, my pulse does a stupid little skip, like I've stepped onto a stair that isn't there.

"Does that scare you?" I ask.

"Yes." He doesn't hesitate. "Does me telling you scare you?"

"No." Then, because the red light is making me honest, "Yes. But not in the way you think."

"How do you think I think?"

"That I'm going to run."

He sets the print in the fixer and comes around the end of the sink. The tanks are between us now, a row of little glowing lakes. "I wasn't thinking that," he says quietly. "But I should have thought it. You don't have to stay for the thing I'm figuring out."

It's the kindest thing anyone has said to me in a year, and it makes me furious. "Don't do that," I say. "Don't pre-forgive me."

"Okay."

"Don't say okay like you're humouring me."

"I'm not humouring you, Dani." He's close enough now that I can see the faint silver scar through his left eyebrow, the one from a bike accident he once described in three sentences and never mentioned again. "I just don't know what you want me to say."

What I want is simple and it terrifies me. I want to be the one who knows what happens next, and I want him to let me, and I don't want to be the kind of person who wants that. The timer on the enlarger clicks, a sharp little percussion in the quiet, and we both jump.

"Your print," I say, and it comes out wrong, too breathy, a girl in a film trying not to look at the boy.

"My print," he agrees. Neither of us moves.

The seconds stretch. I'm aware of everything at once: the cold coming off the wet paper, the hum of the extractor fan, the way his breathing has gone shallow to match mine. I don't know what I'm doing. For once in my life I genuinely don't know, and that uncertainty feels like falling and flying at the same time.

"Elliot," I say.

"Yeah."

"I don't know what this is."

"Me neither." He smiles, a little crooked, the expression of someone standing in a room he's never been inside before. "But I'd like to find out. If that's allowed."

I think about my ex, who always knew exactly what he wanted and never once asked me. I think about my mother, who told me at fourteen that a strong woman doesn't need anyone to hold her hand. I think about the red light and how it makes everyone look like a ghost of themselves.

"It's allowed," I say. "But you go first. I'm not used to that."

He reaches out and takes my hand, the way you'd pick up something that might still be wet, and for a second neither of us says anything at all.


## 3. Stolen Moments in Roma

The Vespa came out of the Via del Corso like a wasp with a grudge, and Ben Hartley, who had been reading the paragraph on the Trevi Fountain for the third time, looked up just in time to see a helmet, a leather jacket and a pair of furious brown eyes.

He stepped sideways. The Vespa swerved. The rider swore, long and fluent and so rapid that he caught perhaps one word in five, though he was fairly sure one of them was his mother's.

"Scusi," Ben said. "Mi scusi. Sono molto, molto—" He could not find the word for sorry in the middle of a Roman street, so he settled for "Terribly sorry," which came out in English and sounded like a man apologising to a hedge.

The rider pushed up her visor. "You're in the middle of the pavement with a book, English. This is not a library."

"It's a guidebook."

"Is it a good one?"

"It's rather good on the fountain. Did you know the water comes from the Aqua Virgo, which was built by Agrippa, and that the aqueduct is still—"

"Stop. I don't care." She swung off the bike and pulled off her gloves, one finger at a time. Up close she was smaller than he had thought, with dark hair crushed flat by the helmet and a scrape across one cheekbone. "My name is Alessandra. I deliver things for a living. Today I have delivered four lasagnas, two prescriptions and a pair of shoes that someone in Trastevere is going to regret ordering. And you almost made me the fifth delivery to the hospital."

"I'm Ben. I'm on sabbatical. I'm writing about—"

"Don't tell me. You're writing a book about Rome that no one will read."

Ben opened his mouth, closed it, and then laughed, a startled, unguarded sound that surprised them both. Alessandra's eyes narrowed. She looked at him properly for the first time, the rumpled linen jacket, the carefully folded map, the expression of a man who had spent six months apologising for existing.

"You laugh like a Protestant," she said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Like you're not sure you're allowed. Come. You owe me coffee for the near-death experience."

---

The bar was a narrow room on Via dei Condotti, all chipped marble and the smell of burnt sugar. Alessandra ordered for both of them in a flurry of Italian, and when the barista slid two tiny cups across the counter Ben said, "Grazie," with such precise pronunciation that the man grinned.

"You sound like a wedding announcement," Alessandra said.

"I practised on the plane."

"Practise on me. Say something rude."

"I'm not going to say something rude in Italian."

"Coward." She knocked back her espresso in one swallow. "Then tell me what you're avoiding. Men with guidebooks always avoid something."

Ben turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "My wife left me for a man who restores violins. Quite a nice man, actually. He sent me a card at Christmas. I'm not sure what to do with that."

"Ah." Alessandra's face softened for a moment, then sharpened again. "So you came to Rome to be sad in beautiful places."

"I came to be sensible for a year. Visit the museums. Eat the food. Be a person who writes monographs."

"And instead you nearly got run over by me."

"Yes. It's been a notable first morning."

She leaned across the counter, close enough that he could see a faint line of dust along her jaw. "Come with me," she said. "Just for the afternoon. I'll show you the real Rome. The one that isn't in your book."

"I have a lunch appointment at the Pantheon."

"Cancel it."

"I can't cancel it. It's with a professor from Oxford."

"Then he can be sensible without you." She slid off the stool and grabbed his wrist, not roughly, and Ben felt the warm, unexpected pull of it all the way to his shoulder. "Ben. You have a face like a closed library. Let's see what happens if we open one window."

---

They did not, in the end, go to the Pantheon. They went to a bakery in Trastevere where Alessandra persuaded a grey-haired woman to give them the cornetto that had been set aside for a priest. They went up the steps of the Janiculum and watched the city spread out below them like a spilled box of tiles, and Alessandra pointed out every dome and bell tower and told him, with complete confidence, which of them were haunted.

"That one is haunted by a cardinal who poisoned his brother."

"Is that true?"

"Probably. Rome is a city where all the bells are a little bit guilty."

At the Campo de' Fiori she bought a single peach from a stall, then, while the vendor was turned round arguing about the price of cherries, slipped a small silver spoon into her pocket. Ben saw it. He saw the quick, unbothered flick of her wrist and the way her face was perfectly innocent when she turned back.

"You stole that," he said.

"I borrowed it. Give it back to him later, if you're so virtuous."

"I'm not going to give it back."

"Good," said Alessandra, and bit into the peach, and the juice ran down her chin, and Ben looked away so quickly he nearly walked into a pigeon.

By four o'clock they were sitting on the edge of the Spanish Steps, sharing a bottle of wine she had bought from a shop that did not seem to have a licence. Alessandra was telling him about her brother, who wanted to be a priest and kept falling in love with the widows at his parish. Ben was laughing so hard he had to put the bottle down. He was not sure when he had stopped being careful. He was not sure he wanted to start again.

"You know what you are, English?" Alessandra said, not quite looking at him.

"Tell me."

"A man who has been waiting for someone to tell him he's allowed to be ridiculous." She leaned back on her elbows and studied the sky. "I like that. It's useful."

"Useful for what?"

She did not answer. She simply reached out, took his hand and stood up, pulling him after her into the crowd.

---

Later, much later, when the light was slanting gold across the Tiber and Ben's feet hurt and his jacket had acquired a lasagna stain, Alessandra stopped under the arch of a small church near the river.

"This is where I drop you off," she said.

"Am I being dropped?"

"You're being returned." She pressed the silver spoon into his palm. He had not noticed her take it back from the coat pocket. "For the peach man. You can give it to him tomorrow with an apology in your bad Italian."

"And if I don't come back?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and her expression was unreadable, a shuttered window with a light behind it.

"Then you'll have a wonderful story," she said, "and I'll have nothing to do with it."

She kissed him, very briefly, on the corner of his mouth, and then she was gone, the Vespa roaring off between the scooters and the tourists like something let loose from a cage.

Ben stood under the arch with the spoon in his hand and the taste of wine on his lips. He could not tell whether he had been robbed or rescued.

He was fairly sure, though, that he would be back at the Campo de' Fiori tomorrow, and that the peach man would give him a look of deep suspicion, and that the spoon would be in his pocket again before the afternoon was out.


## 4. The Last Outpost

The Outpost is never truly quiet, but on a Tuesday it settles into a kind of murmur, a hum of engines in the dock below and the clink of glasses on the bar above. I wipe down the counter with a rag that was white once, and I watch the stranger the way I watch everyone who sits down here: quickly, sideways, without appearing to look.

He has the kind of face that has had a long day. Grey around the eyes. A coat so sooty at the cuffs it could have come from an engine room. And his hair, which might once have been neat, now hangs in a dozen tentacled dreads that twitch and coil whenever he shifts his weight. Something about it makes my stomach tighten. Not fear. Recognition, maybe.

"Vega whisky, neat," he says again, quieter now. "Better make that a double."

I pour it. Vega is the cheap stuff, from a distillery on the far side of the belt that nobody admits to drinking. I set the glass down in front of him and he looks at it as though it has offended him personally.

"Long shift?" I ask.

"Long life." He picks up the glass and drinks half of it in one go. Then he sets it down very carefully, the way you put down something that might break. "Is there a back room here?"

"There's a storeroom. Nobody goes in it unless they're fixing the pumps."

"Good. I might need to fix something."

I lean on the counter. Behind me, Old Haskell is arguing with a dock hand about a crate of water filters, and the jukebox has started on something with a steel guitar, and none of that matters. The stranger is looking at me as if he is trying to decide whether I am someone who can keep a secret. I hate that I am still the sort of person who wants to be.

"What's your name?" I ask.

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters to me. I'm the one pouring your drinks."

"Then call me Pell." He turns the glass on the wood. "And I'd rather you didn't write it down."

"I don't write anything down. I'm a bartender." I pick up another glass, check it for smudges, set it back. "What do you want, Pell?"

"Nothing you have."

"Everybody wants something from the Outpost. That's why they come."

"I'm not from the Outpost." He glances at the door. Then, very softly: "I'm looking for someone who used to work the Krell lanes. A woman with a scar across her palm. Her name was Nettle, once."

The glass slips out of my hand. It doesn't break. It rolls, wobbles, and I catch it against my chest, heart hammering so hard I am sure he must see it through my shirt.

"Never heard of her," I say.

"You're lying. You're very bad at it."

"And you're very rude for a man in a dirty coat."

He actually smiles, though it doesn't reach his eyes. "She owes someone a debt. A large one. The sort that ends up with people coming through the airlocks at night with paperwork and no lights on."

"Then she should pay it."

"She tried. The money went to the wrong man." He pauses, as if weighing the words. "The wrong man is dead now. Which is why I'm here, and why I'm drinking Vega, and why you should be very careful about who you give your change to."

I pull the rag off my shoulder and fold it into a square. I do this when I need my hands to be busy. "You think I'm her."

"I think you have her hands," he says. "Scar across the palm, same as the description. And the way you looked at the door just now. Like you were counting the seconds to the back exit."

Damn him. I did count. I always count.

"Pell," I say, "I don't know what you think I've done, but my shift ends at eleven. If you want to talk, you can talk after that. Not here, not with Haskell listening."

"Haskell's already listening." He nods towards the far end of the bar, where the old man has stopped arguing and is staring at us over his beer. "He knows who I am. He's got a cousin on Ceres who owes me money."

Oh, this is going to be a bad night.

"Then drink your whisky," I say, "and tell me the rest in the storeroom, and don't you dare touch the pump valves while you're in there."

---

I have not been in this storeroom for three years. It smells the same: oil, cold metal, the sweet dust of filter cartridges. Pell stands in the middle of the crates, turning slowly, one hand resting on the strap of a duffel bag that he has not yet put down.

"You don't remember me," he says.

"I remember a lot of people. Most of them were drunk."

"I was on the Ceres run, the one that went through the outer ring. You took my transfer forms and told me to come back in a month." He pauses. "You were different then. Shorter hair. Still scared."

The name clicks, slowly, like a lock that has rusted. Ghost ships. Forged manifests. A boy with a stutter who had begged me to get him out of the ring before the Krell men came for his sister. I had done my best. I had not been enough.

"Halvard," I say.

"Pell. Halvard was the boy I was before I learned to lie." He shrugs, and the dreads twitch. "The man who got me out is dead. He was going to pay the debt to the Krell family, and then someone from the Commission started asking questions about your old contract."

"I didn't sign anything with the Commission."

"You signed something with the wrong people. That's the same thing out here." He drops the duffel bag on the floor and it lands with a heavy, metallic clunk. "Look. I need a place to stay tonight. Then I need to get to the Andromeda gate before the next patrol. And I need you to decide whether you're going to help me, or call the port wardens and let them take what I'm carrying."

"What are you carrying?"

He crouches, unzips the bag, and shows me. Inside, wrapped in grey cloth, are three sealed canisters and a stack of documents with official-looking stamps. My breath stops.

"Well," I say faintly. "That's a problem for later."

"It's a problem for now," says Pell. "And you're the only one who can make it go away."

Out in the bar, the jukebox has fallen silent. Somewhere in the dock, a klaxon begins its slow, patient rising call. I look at the canisters, and the stamps, and the scar across my palm that I stopped hiding years ago, and I think about the glass that nearly slipped out of my hand.

"Sit down," I say. "Before you fall down. And tell me everything, starting with the name of the man who's coming for you."

He does not sit. He simply looks at me, very steadily, and says, "That's the thing, Nettle. He's already here."


## 5. Gone South

Okay. Fine. Here's what I know so far.

The bus from Nashville let me off on Main Street, and the driver looked at my backpack and my face and said, "You got somewhere to be, sugar?" in a voice like a warm towel. I said yes. I had not, in fact, got anywhere to be. I had got a bus ticket that my cousin Jaycee paid for, forty dollars and a hug and the whispered words don't call Mom, she'll cry on the phone and then drive herself crazy.

Two days ago I told my family I'm trans. I know I already said that, but it's the thing that keeps replaying, so I'm going to keep saying it until it stops being the loudest sound in my head. I'm trans. My mom looked at me across the kitchen table like I'd just told her I'd joined a cult. My dad put his coffee down very carefully and said, "We'll talk about this when you've calmed down." The school counsellor, Mrs. Pruitt, had already called on Monday to tell Mom I had concerning behavioural patterns. Apparently that's what they call it when you stop pretending to be someone's idea of a good girl.

So last night, after the yelling stopped and the house went dark, I climbed out of my bedroom window with my hoodie and my savings and a bag of sour gummy worms, and I walked to the highway. A guy in a pickup truck picked me up. He had a Jesus fish on the bumper and a dog named Pilot in the passenger seat. He drove me two hundred miles and didn't try anything, which was a surprise, and then he dropped me off in downtown New Orleans at three in the morning and drove away before I could say thank you.

Now it's noon. The sun's already sweaty. The street smells like river water and fried dough and something sweet that I can't name. There's a man with a trumpet on the corner playing something slow and sad, and I stand there for a minute listening, because it's the first thing in two days that hasn't made me want to throw up.

Breathe, Taylor. You're two states away. Nobody's going to drag you back today.

---

I get a coffee from a stall with a striped awning. The woman behind the counter calls me baby and doesn't ask how old I am, which I appreciate. I sit on a bench in Jackson Square and pretend to read a map I stole from the bus station. Pigeons walk around my shoes like they're checking for crumbs.

"That's a terrible map," says a voice beside me.

I jump. A girl is sitting at the other end of the bench. She's maybe my age, maybe a bit older, with a shaved head and a leather jacket that's too big for her, and she's eating a beignet with the focus of someone who hasn't eaten in a while.

"It's a map of Tennessee," I say.

"Yeah. That's the problem. Nobody goes to New Orleans with a map of Tennessee."

"I didn't go to New Orleans. New Orleans kind of happened to me."

She snorts, brushes sugar off her fingers. "I'm Dee. You look like you've been awake for a week."

"Taylor."

"Taylor. Okay. You're a runaway."

"I'm not—"

"Taylor. Your backpack has a Hello Kitty keychain on it and you're holding the map upside down. You're a runaway."

Heat floods my face. "Yeah," I say. "I guess I am."

Dee shrugs. "Welcome to the club. Membership is basically free and the dues are mostly hunger."

---

We walk together down Royal Street while Dee explains the rules, which are apparently not written anywhere but everybody knows them. Don't sleep in the park by the river after dark. Don't take rides from guys who keep their windows up. Don't let the shelter staff take your ID unless they give you a receipt. Don't trust anybody who says they're "like family", because family is the thing you ran away from in the first place.

"Did yours do something?" I ask.

Dee's face goes still for half a second, then she laughs, a bitter little sound. "Mine don't know I'm gone yet. Mine are in Memphis, and they're the kind of people who think a bed is a reward you earn by behaving."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I stole the car they were going to give me for my birthday." She grins sideways at me. "Best revenge is a Honda with a bad radio."

There's a long pause while a streetcar goes rumbling past, its bell clanging. I want to ask her what happened, really happened, but there's something in her shoulders that tells me not to push. So I ask something else.

"Do you know a place I can sleep tonight?"

"There's a church on Esplanade that does a soup kitchen and a dorm for anyone under twenty-one. You've got to be out by seven. The dorm's full of creeps, but the staff are decent. I know the guy who runs it."

"Do they ask questions?"

"They ask your name and give you a number. It's not a confession booth, Taylor." She stops suddenly and looks at me, really looks, with narrowed eyes. "They gonna try to send you back?"

"I don't know," I say honestly. "My mom's probably already filed a report. Or she's still deciding whether to be mad or sad about it. My dad's probably just pretending I'm on a sleepover."

"Your dad sounds like a real winner."

"He's not a bad guy. He just can't look at me right now."

Dee nods slowly. "Yeah. That's the worst kind."

---

The church smells of cinnamon and bleach. A woman with grey braids hands me a plastic tray of red beans and rice and a paper cup of lemonade, and I eat so fast I almost choke. Dee watches me with a slight smile.

"Slow down. Nobody's gonna take it from you."

"Feels like they might."

"Not here. Not today." She tilts her head at a boy sitting alone in the corner, hood up, scrolling on a phone with a cracked screen. "That's Marcus. He's been here three weeks. Doesn't talk much, but he's good with a deck of cards. Don't bet money with him."

Marcus looks up, meets my eyes for a second, then looks away. There's something in his face that reminds me of the bathroom mirror at home, the way I'd stare into it at midnight, trying to make my reflection match the person I knew I was.

"I'm not going to bet," I say.

"Good," says Dee. "Because I'm not paying your debts."

I laugh. It hurts, but it's a real laugh, and it feels like something cracking open in my chest.

---

That night I lie on a narrow bed in a room with eleven other girls and listen to someone snoring two beds down. My phone is at four percent. I have one unread text from Jaycee: ur ok? mom cried all morning. dad says ur a grown up now lol. love u tay pls text.

I type im ok. Then I delete it. Then I type im trans and im scared and i dont know if i did the right thing, and I look at it for a long time before I delete that too.

Dee's voice comes out of the dark, low and steady. "You awake?"

"Yeah."

"You gonna be okay?"

I stare at the ceiling, at the cracked plaster and the water stain shaped like Florida. "I don't know," I say. "I think I'm going to be different. I don't know if different is okay."

There's a long silence. Then Dee says, "Different is the only thing I've ever been, Taylor. It's not so bad once you stop apologising for it."

I roll over to face the wall and let the tears come quietly, the way you do when you don't want anyone to know. Somewhere out in the Quarter, the trumpet player is still playing, slow and sad, and I think that maybe, for tonight, the sad song is enough.


## 6. My Apocalypse Buddy

The apocalypse had been over for about forty years, though nobody had bothered to tell the pylons, and Midas had long since stopped expecting them to get the news. He stood at the top of the rubble heap with his bag of bolts over one shoulder and considered the morning. It was grey. It smelled of wet concrete and something dead behind the bins. In the distance, a rusted billboard still advertised a brand of cola that had been bankrupt since before he was born.

"Right," he said. "The mission."

"Hrrrk," said Chewy.

Chewy was a stuffed koala, roughly the size of a loaf of bread, with one glass eye, one button eye, and a voice box that had been dropped in a river during the Fall. He sat on Midas's shoulder with his claws hooked into the strap of the bag, and every so often his head would swivel with a faint mechanical whine, as if he were running a diagnostic on the world and finding it wanting.

"The mission," Midas repeated, "is to fetch the second battery from the Old Co-op. The one with the solar panel that didn't explode last time. We get the battery, we get back to the Hub before dark, and we have a warm tin of beans and an argument about whether beans count as a vegetable."

"Hrrrk. Hrrrk."

"I know you think beans are a vegetable. You always think that. You said it the last time and I was too polite to correct you."

Chewy's head swivelled towards the billboard and gave a long, pained wheeze.

"I didn't say they weren't food," Midas said. "I said they were not a vegetable. Different thing entirely. Like a sock is not a hat, even if you put it on your head in the cold."

---

The Old Co-op was four streets away, down a slope of collapsed flats where the wind moaned between the windows like a choir with a grudge. Midas picked his way down the slope with the careful, bowlegged gait of a man who had broken both ankles once and was fond of telling people so. Chewy rode on his shoulder and kept a lookout.

"Movement," whispered Chewy, in a tinny little whisper that sounded like a radio being apologetic.

Midas stopped. Ahead, between two burnt-out cars, something was shuffling. It was tall and thin and wore the remains of a high-visibility jacket, and its head was turned at a jaunty angle, like a bird listening for worms.

"Hello?" said Midas.

The thing turned its head further, which was unpleasant, and made a noise like a kettle considering its options.

"Ah," said Midas. "A bloke. A former bloke, I think. He's very interested in my hat."

The former bloke lurched forward. Midas ran. He ran with the dignity of a man who had been running from things for most of his life, and he ran straight into a lamppost, which knocked his hat off and sent his bag of bolts flying across the road like a shower of silver hail.

"Oh, come on," he gasped, scrambling up. "That was a perfectly good lamppost. It wasn't even doing anything."

Chewy had launched himself from the shoulder at the last second and landed in a crater of wet leaves, where he sat with his claws over his face, shuddering.

"Hrrrk," he said, faintly.

"Well done," said Midas. "You saved me from the bloke. Now we only have to outrun him, and I'm sure you'll think of something dignified."

---

The dignified thing turned out to be a fire escape. They clambered up it with the former bloke pursuing them at a steady, hungry lope, his jacket flapping like a flag. At the top, Midas paused to catch his breath and realised, with a sinking heart, that the door to the roof had been bolted from the inside sometime in the last decade.

"Oh, this is a classic," he muttered. "Why did nobody think of the bolts?"

"Hrrrk."

"Yes, I know. Bolts are the problem with everything."

The former bloke reached the top of the stairs and began to climb the last rung with the wet, dreadful patience of something that had all the time in the world. Midas looked at the bolt. He looked at Chewy. He looked at the bag of bolts he had collected, which had not made it into the bag at all, but was currently rattling inside his left boot.

"Chewy," he said. "I need you to be very brave and very stupid."

"Hrrrk?"

"Go down the fire escape, skip along the railing and pull the lever on that drainpipe. There, the one with the red wheel. It's a valve. It opens the old sprinkler system. The water'll get the bloke's attention, and then he'll slip on the wet stairs and fall off the side, and we'll never have to speak to him again."

Chewy considered this for a moment, then nodded with alarming solemnity and hurled himself off the parapet. He was, at that moment, the bravest and stupidest creature Midas had ever known.

The valve screamed. Water exploded from a dozen rusted nozzles, soaking the stairs, the former bloke and, somewhat regrettably, Midas. The former bloke slipped. He went over the railing with an astonished squawk and a flutter of high-visibility jacket, and landed in the car park below with a noise like a dropped sack of cabbages.

Midas sat down in the puddle. "Well," he said. "That was dignified."

---

They limped home in the late afternoon, beans in a tin, battery on a wooden trolley, hat rescued from the lamppost, Chewy wrapped in a towel and sulking. The Hub's gate rattled open as they approached, and the old robot dog who ran the defences tilted its head and said, in a voice like a dry hinge, "You are late, and you are wet."

"We had an encounter," said Midas.

"You had a lamppost," said the robot dog. "I can see the dent."

"The lamppost started it."

"Naturally." The robot dog sniffed the trolley. "Is that the battery?"

"It is. And we also got the beans."

"Beans are not a vegetable," said the robot dog flatly.

Midas turned to Chewy with a look of profound triumph. Chewy, still wrapped in his towel, raised one button eye and let out a long, satisfied sigh.

"Hrrrk," he said, and everyone agreed that the apocalypse, for all its faults, had at least produced some good arguments.


## 7. Love in the Limelight

The bell over the door of Pritchard's Books gave a feeble jangle, and the man who came through it looked like he had been chased by a pack of wolves and was quite pleased with the wolves.

"Don't say anything," he said, pressing his back against the door. "Just lock it. Please."

Nia Pritchard did not lock it. She looked up from the card catalogue she had been re-alphabetising since eleven that morning and found a tall man in a navy wool coat that cost more than her car. His hair was artfully tousled in a way that suggested a team of people had been employed to achieve it. Through the front window, a knot of photographers was milling about on the pavement, lenses pressed to the glass.

"You're Jasper Vane," she said.

"Ah." He smiled, and it was a dazzling smile, the kind that had sold a great many cinema tickets and a fair quantity of aftershave. "So you've seen the films."

"I've seen the adverts. There was one on the bus shelter in Aberystwyth. You were holding a sword and looking like you'd just been told your dog died."

"That was a very difficult day on set."

"I'm sure it was."

Jasper looked at the window, then at her, and his smile flickered. "They're not going to leave. You understand that. There's a man in a Fiat who's been circling the car park since the Llanon turn-off."

"Then you'd better browse," said Nia. "We're not a lending library, but we'll let you stand in the poetry section."

"Poetry is my weakness." He moved away from the door, drifting between the shelves with his hands in his pockets. He stopped at the new arrivals table and picked up a hardback, glanced at the cover, and put it down again with a small, theatrical sigh. "Do you not have anything on the Stanislavski method? Or any of the modern film theory? Something to flatter a man's intellect while he's hiding from the press?"

"We have a shelf on local history and a shelf on birds."

"Birds."

"Very popular in Ceredigion."

"I can imagine." He turned, eyes lighting on her with interest. "And what do you recommend for a man who has been hunted through three villages today by people with telephoto lenses?"

"A cup of tea and a long sit down in the back room. That's what I recommend for most people."

"I'm not most people."

"No," said Nia. "You're most of the people who come in here and say that."

For a moment he looked genuinely surprised. Then he laughed, a bright, delighted sound that echoed off the beams, and something in his shoulders loosened. "You're not impressed by me at all, are you?"

"I'm impressed you found the shop. Nobody finds it on purpose. The sign's been painted over twice."

"Then I'm very lucky."

"You're very lucky the photographers can't read Welsh."

He tilted his head. "Is that an insult?"

"Only if you're trying to get a rise out of me. Are you?"

"Honestly? I was going to be charming." He leaned his elbow against a shelf of Dylan Thomas and gave her the full force of it, the slow look, the warm voice dropped half an octave. "I was going to tell you that your eyes are the exact colour of the sea on a grey morning, and then I was going to ask you to have dinner with me in front of that window so the photographers could see."

Nia folded her arms. "My eyes are brown."

"Are they?"

"They're brown, Mr. Vane. Like most of the eyes in Cardigan."

"I'm wounded."

"You'll live. Do you want the tea or not?"

He stared at her, and she watched his face shift through several expressions in quick succession: charm, bafflement, the faint, wounded hurt of a man who has never once been ignored, and then something quieter that she did not quite know what to do with. He looked, for a second, like a boy who had been told the sweets were not for him.

"Tea," he said. "Yes. Please."

She went through to the back and filled the kettle, and when she came out again with two mugs he was standing by the window, watching the Fiat and the photographers with their cameras, his reflection superimposed over the street.

"They think I'm in a shop in Brighton," he said, without turning. "I told my manager I was going to a spa. He'll be very cross when he finds out I didn't book one."

"You could tell them the truth. That you were hiding in a bookshop from a pack of pensioners with long lenses."

"They're not pensioners. Two of them are students from Bangor. One of them is in a Welsh cheese jumper."

"Then he's the most dangerous of the lot."

Jasper turned and took the mug from her, and for a moment their fingers touched on the handle. He did not let go straight away. Nia felt the warmth of his skin, and something in her chest gave a small, unwelcome lurch, like a step that wasn't where she'd expected.

"Thank you," he said quietly. "Nia, is it?"

"It says so on the door."

"It says Pritchard's Books on the door. I read the name tag on your cardigan."

"You're very observant for an actor."

"I'm very observant for a man who has been looking at a particular woman all afternoon." He let go of the mug. "Don't pretend you didn't notice."

"I noticed you were trying to get a reaction out of me."

"And?"

"And it's not working, Mr. Vane. I'm sorry."

"Jasper."

"It's still not working."

He smiled, slow and crooked, and Nia realised with a jolt of irritation that the smile was real this time, and that she liked it, which was inconvenient in every possible way.

"Then I'll just have to keep trying," he said. "I'm rather stubborn when people don't fall at my feet."

"I can tell."

"Can you?"

"Yes. You've been standing there for ten minutes waiting for me to say something clever about your cheekbones."

"Your cheekbones are very fine, Nia Pritchard."

"Thank you. Drink your tea."

He drank. The shop was quiet except for the ticking of the clock and the far-off wheeze of a bus on the main road. Outside, one of the students had lowered his camera and was staring at the bookshop window with the uncertain look of someone who had seen something on a poster and couldn't quite place it.

"They're going to find me," Jasper said.

"They'll find you when you walk out of the door. Go out the back. There's a lane to the chapel. Nobody uses it in the afternoons except Mrs. Davies and her dog, and the dog doesn't care who you are."

"Will you walk me to it?"

"I'm not walking you anywhere. I've got a delivery of Welsh poetry at four and a man coming about the roof."

"Of course." He set down the empty mug and looked at her for a long, unreadable moment. "Thank you for the tea, Nia. And for not being impressed."

"You're welcome."

"I meant what I said about the eyes, you know."

"You said they were the colour of the sea."

"They're brown, I suppose." He grinned. "But I still think they're better than the sea."

She did not answer. She simply turned back to the card catalogue, and after a while she heard the back door open, and close, and the faint crunch of expensive shoes on gravel. She stood very still with a card in her hand for a long moment before she realised she had been holding it upside down, and that she was smiling, and that she was going to have to explain that to Mrs. Davies.


## 8. The Morska Conspiracy

The underhold was colder than Aya had expected, and quieter, and the silence was the first thing that frightened her. The guild's taverns had always rung with brass and bellows, with the clatter of dice and the hoarse laughter of men who had nothing left to lose. Here there was only the drip of condensation from iron pipes overhead and the faint, sweet hum of the immateria lamps that had been set into the walls like the eyes of sleeping animals.

Katra moved first, a shadow in a shadow, her pistol held low and steady. Simeon came behind her, breathing through his teeth, his lantern shuttered to a sliver.

"Too quiet," he whispered.

"I know," said Aya.

"You knew?"

"I didn't know. I felt it." She pressed her palm against the cold brass of the door frame, and the metal was humming very faintly, the way a bell does after it has been struck and the sound has gone somewhere else. "The wards are still active. Someone has been down here, and recently."

Katra's mouth tightened. "Then it's a trap."

"It's a trap," Aya agreed. "The only question is whose."

The corridor opened into the old strongroom, the one the Morska men had carved from the bedrock two centuries ago when the city had still believed in honest iron. Lamps lit the ceiling in pale rings. At the far end, behind a grille of black steel, stood the reliquary: a squat cabinet of blued metal, scythan script running in fine lines across its doors, and a single brass key set into the lock like a splinter of bone.

Between the three of them and the cabinet stood a woman.

She was seated on a high-backed chair that had clearly been brought down from somewhere grander, one leg crossed over the other, a pocket watch open in her gloved hand. Her coat was the deep green of old bottles. Her hair was iron-grey and cropped close to a skull that looked, in the lamplight, almost delicate. Behind her stood four men in leather and oilcloth, each holding a double-barrelled steam carbine with the casual ease of a farmer holding a rake.

"Good evening," said Oriel Vantesh. "You're earlier than I expected. I had assumed you'd wait for the bell."

Aya's throat was dry. She heard Simeon's breath stop.

"Madame," Katra said, very clearly, "you were supposed to be in Ashgrave Gaol."

"I was supposed to be a great many things, Miss Varga. The Constabulary have a remarkable talent for misplacing the people they arrest, usually in the direction of whoever paid them most recently." Vantesh snapped the watch shut. "Please lower your pistol. My men are nervous, and Mr. Hollis there has a tremor in his trigger finger that worries me more than it should."

Katra did not lower the pistol. Aya put a hand on her arm. "Let her," she said softly. "If we're going to die in here, we might as well do it without a bullet in the wrong place."

The pistol came down, slowly.

Vantesh's gaze moved past Katra and fixed on Aya, and Aya felt it settle on her like a cold hand. "You're the one with the summoning," Vantesh said. "The masked girl. The one who came in six months ago with a letter of introduction from a man who was, I am sorry to say, already dead. The men upstairs said you could raise things from the immateria without a circle or a candle. I didn't believe them."

"Believe what you like, Madame."

"Oh, I do. That's why you're going to take that mask off for me."

Aya's fingers rose of their own accord to the silver filigree at her temples. She hesitated. Behind her, Simeon swore under his breath.

"Go on," said Vantesh pleasantly. "Let's not be dramatic."

The mask came away. The air in the strongroom grew noticeably colder. Aya's face, pale and fine-boned and recognisable to anyone who had ever attended a ball on Heron Street, was revealed to the lamplight.

"Lady Aya Trevelyan," Vantesh said. "Daughter of Lord Ambrose Trevelyan, the Chancellor of the Assay Office. Whose wife was hanged in the square at Lantern Hill for practising immateria magick twenty years ago." She smiled faintly. "Regrettably. I was there. I remember how the rope creaked."

Aya's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Then you know why I'm here."

"I know exactly why you're here. You're here because your father would sooner see you hang than see the family name dragged through the Morska gutters, and because you are bored, my dear, and boredom is the most dangerous thing a rich girl can carry." Vantesh rose. Her bodyguards shifted. "Do you have any idea how many of my people you've already brought down? Hollis's brother. The Pell twins. A boy named Corin who was barely fourteen. All of them dead since you started summoning things in the cellars under Velland Street."

"They chose to follow me."

"They chose to follow a masked stranger who could make shadows bite. That is hardly a choice worth the name." Vantesh stepped down from the dais and began to walk, unhurried, along the line of the grille. "Now. Here is my difficulty. The Constabulary believe the guild is leaderless. This is, as it happens, nonsense. But the belief is useful to me, and I dislike wasting useful things. So I am going to offer you a bargain."

"We are listening," said Aya, before Katra could speak.

"You're a noble. You're worth a ransom to your father, if he cares enough to pay it, which I doubt. Your companions are worth nothing except as leverage against you. I could hand the three of you to the Constabulary at dawn, and the scandal would bury the Trevelyan name and feed the newspapers for a month." Vantesh paused by the reliquary, one gloved hand resting on its blued doors. "Or you can give me your summoning. Teach my people the old practice in return for your lives. You'd be my hostage, Lady Aya, not a prisoner. I'd keep you comfortable. I might even keep you useful."

"And the others?" Aya asked.

"Your friends will be made comfortable as well. Provided they remember who has the keys."

Katra's laugh was short and bitter. "She's offering you a cage with better wallpaper."

"Yes," said Aya quietly. "She is."

Her heart was hammering. She could feel the immateria beneath the stones, a faint pressure like weather building, and she could feel the scythan artifact behind the grille, a hunger wrapped in brass. Her mother had died in the square, with the rope creaking and the crowd murmuring like the sea. Her mother had never once been offered a bargain.

"You'll want proof," Aya said. "That I can do what your men say I can."

"Naturally."

"Then you'll have to let me reach for the key."

Vantesh's eyes narrowed. "Why would I permit that?"

"Because the reliquary is warded against the Constabulary, Madame, and only a summoner can break the ward without destroying the contents." Aya's voice held. "Your men can't open it. You know that. That's why you waited for us."

For a long moment the strongroom was silent except for the drip of water through the pipes overhead. Then Vantesh smiled, and the smile was colder than anything Aya had yet seen.

"Clever girl," she said. "Your mother was clever too."

She stepped back, and gestured to her men, and the carbines lifted a fraction in the lamplight, black and patient as hungry birds.

"Go on, then," said Oriel Vantesh. "Show me what the dead have taught you."


## 9. Gunpowder Rendezvous

The bar is called the Crooked Wren, and the only thing crooked about it is the floor. Spike Spiegel sits with his back to the wall and a bottle of cheap sake at his elbow, his cigarette burning down between two fingers while he watches the door out of his one good eye. The prosthetic one sits behind a pair of dark glasses. It does not see much, but it has never once been fooled by a bad entrance.

The room is full of men who have been hired for the same bounty and are pretending not to know one another. A large man in a leather duster keeps glancing at the bar. Two brothers in matching bowling shirts whisper in the corner. Somewhere near the stairs, a thin woman in a grey suit taps a spoon against her glass, slow and rhythmic, as if counting down to something.

And then the door swings open and Revy walks in.

She does not wait for the room to notice her. She never does. Black crop top, short shorts, the dragon on her right arm curling down past her wrist, two holstered pistols riding her hips like a pair of guests who have overstayed. She ducks under the low lintel without looking up, drops into the empty seat across from Spike, and slaps a folded wanted poster on the table. The paper is smudged with sweat.

"Kinda short notice for a meet, cowboy," she says.

"Kinda short notice for a bounty, Two Hands." Spike taps ash into the tray. "You take the same job I did?"

"Looks like it." She flattens the poster with one hand. The face on it is a sallow man with a crooked nose and a grin that could sell lighters to a monk. "Name's Gorin Vaux. Four million in gold. Last seen on a freighter out of Ceres, which means he's probably smuggling himself in a crate of tinned peaches."

"Probably a crate of tinned peaches with a bomb in it."

"Yeah, well. Crate's not the problem. It's who's paying." She leans back, one boot on the rung of his chair. "Some syndicate with a name like a fishing lure. They sent a woman with a grey suit and a spoon to tell me the rules. I don't like the rules."

Spike glances across the room. The woman in grey has stopped tapping. She is watching them now, very still, her spoon balanced on her knuckles like a gambler's chip.

"Yeah," he says. "I noticed the spoon."

"You notice everything, don't you?"

"Only the things that might kill me."

Revy laughs, sharp and short, and reaches over to steal the cigarette from his fingers. She takes a drag, exhales through her nose, and hands it back. Their hands touch for a second. Her skin is warm and rough from years of pistol grips, and Spike does not pull away, which he notices and she notices him noticing.

"You still don't trust anyone," she says.

"I trust the bullets in my gun."

"Good. Me too."

"And the sake." He pours her a cup. She drinks it straight, no flinch.

"You know what I think?" she says, eyes narrowing. "I think you're just tired. You came here looking for a bounty because you're broke again, and you're sick of sleeping in the crates of that rustbucket ship with the bad coffee. And you think if you get the gold, you'll finally be free."

"I'm already free."

"Liar." She pushes the cup back to him. "You're the most chained-up bastard I've ever met."

Spike says nothing. He drinks, and the sake burns the way it always does, and somewhere behind the prosthetic eye a memory flickers: a woman's voice, a train platform, the word wait. He shakes it off. He is good at shaking things off. It is the thing he is best at, after shooting.

---

The fight starts before the bottle is empty. The large man in the duster has finally decided that the bounty is his, and he stands up with a chair in one hand and a shotgun in the other, shouting something about fair shares. The brothers in bowling shirts reach for their belts. The woman in grey does not move at all.

Revy is on her feet before the chair hits the floor. Both pistols are out, one in each hand, and she fires twice without apparently aiming. The shotgun spins out of the man's grip and clatters across the boards. Spike, one step behind her, drives a boot into the nearer brother's knee and sends him crashing into a table, which collapses under him with a long wooden groan.

The second brother pulls a knife. Spike turns his body sideways and the blade whistles past his ribs, and he catches the wrist with one hand, twists it, and the knife drops with a clatter. He does not look at the brother's face. He looks at the woman in grey, who has risen from her chair at last and is walking, unhurried, towards the stairs.

"She's leaving," he says.

"Let her go," Revy snaps. "We've got better things to do."

"Like what?"

"Like finding out who she works for before the rest of them figure out we're in the same mess."

The bar is emptying fast. Men are sprinting for the door, knocking over stools. The bartender has ducked behind the counter and is shouting about damages. Spike and Revy stand in the middle of the wreckage, breathing hard, and for a moment the room is just the two of them and a slow-moving smoke haze.

Revy wipes sake off her chin with the back of her wrist. "You're bleeding," she says.

"Just a scratch."

"Yeah, sure." She steps close, grabs his jaw, turns his face towards the light. Her thumb rubs along a thin line of red beneath his cheekbone. Her eyes are very dark. "You idiot. You let him get that close."

"I was busy."

"Busy looking at me?"

"Busy looking at the spoon."

She laughs, low and rough, and her hand stays on his face for one second longer than it needs to. He can feel the heat of her palm, the press of her fingers, and every instinct he owns tells him to step back. He doesn't.

---

The motel is three blocks away, a squat two-storey building with a flickering neon sign that has lost the letter E and now reads MOTL. Room 12 smells of bleach and old cigarettes. The bed is too small. The window looks onto a car park where a single dog is sniffing around a dumpster.

Revy drops her jacket on the chair and checks the lock. Spike sits on the edge of the bed, unbuttoning his shirt with slow, careful movements, and watches her in the reflection of the dark television.

"Tell me the truth," she says, without turning round. "Did you come here for the gold?"

"I came here for a drink."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

She turns. Her face is tight, like someone bracing for a shot. "Because if you did, I'll walk out right now, and we'll both go back to pretending we don't know each other."

"And if I didn't?"

"Then you'd better stop being so bloody cool about it."

He stands. The floorboards creak under his boots. They stop an arm's length apart, and the air between them is thick and hot, the way it gets before a storm breaks over the sea.

"Revy," he says quietly. "I've already died once. I don't like being told what I want."

"Then tell me what you want."

"I want to know if you'd shoot me if I asked you to stay."

She takes a long breath. Her hand drifts to the nearest pistol on the table, not to lift it, only to rest on the grip, the way some people touch a crucifix.

"Yeah," she says. "I would. I'd shoot you right through the chest and then I'd cry about it for a week."

"Good," says Spike. "That's the first honest thing anyone has said to me in a year."

He reaches out. Her hand comes up at the same moment, and for one suspended second they stand with their fingers almost touching, the gun between them forgotten. Outside, the dog barks once and falls silent.

Somewhere down the hall, a door opens and closes. Footsteps. Slow. Deliberate. Revy's head snaps round, her other hand already moving towards the holster, and Spike's good eye narrows at the crack beneath the door, where a thin line of light is suddenly broken by a shadow.

"Well," he says softly. "Looks like the spoon came looking for us after all."


## 10. Útiseta

The first thing Astrid taught me was that you must never say the word for what you're doing until you're already doing it. We were walking up the old cart track behind the church, the snow grey and crusted under our boots, and I asked her what we were actually going to do, and she just said, "Útiseta, obviously," and kept walking.

That was in February, 1996. We were fifteen. I had been in Lappmyra for three months, and I still thought of Stockholm as the place where things happened.

Dad had taken a transfer with the forestry board, and Mum had spent the whole drive north pretending the trees were beautiful. They were, in a way. Tall and black and close together, like people leaning in to hear a secret. At night the wind moved through them with a sound like breathing. I hated it, and then, after a few weeks, I found I couldn't sleep without it.

Astrid lived at the far end of the village with her grandmother, who was very old and very quiet and never came out of the back room. Astrid said her grandmother had taught her the old ways. She said her grandmother had been taught by her own grandmother, and so on, back to a time when people in these woods had known exactly how to speak to the dead.

"You don't have to believe it," Astrid said, the first time she showed me the cards. "You just have to listen."

I didn't believe it. I listened anyway.

---

The mound was a low green hump at the edge of the birch wood, half-buried in drifted snow. There was a flat stone at its crest, carved with lines that might have been runes or might have been water stains. Astrid knelt in front of it and began to unpack her rucksack with the tidy, methodical care of someone laying out a picnic.

"Candle," she said. "Salt. Bread from my grandmother's oven. A knife for the bread. A piece of blue thread." She held it up. "You have to tie it round your wrist before you go in, and you must not take it off until you're back home. If you take it off, the thing that answers you can follow you."

"Follow me where?"

"Home, obviously." She didn't smile. "That's the rule."

I tied the thread round my wrist. My fingers were cold and clumsy. Astrid watched me, then nodded once, as if I had passed some examination I hadn't known I was sitting.

"Now the salt," she said. "A ring around the stone. You step inside the ring and you sit with your back to the mound. You speak only when you're spoken to. And you must never look at the birches behind you."

"Why not?"

"Because they'll be watching."

I laughed. It came out thin and high. "That's not real."

"Then you have nothing to be afraid of." She lit the candle with a match that took three tries to catch. The flame bent sideways in the wind and then straightened, and in that moment the wood seemed to go very quiet, as if everything in it had been listening.

---

We sat. The cold came up through the seat of my jeans and into my spine. Astrid began to hum, something low and wordless that didn't sound like any song I knew. The candle guttered. The bread lay untouched on its cloth. I counted my breaths and tried to think about Stockholm, about the bus stop outside our old flat and the girls who used to laugh at my haircut.

Nothing happened. Then, very faintly, something began to tap.

It was a small sound, like fingernails on wood. It came from somewhere beneath the mound. Astrid's humming stopped.

"Did you hear that?" I whispered.

"Shh," she said.

Tap. Tap-tap. A pause. Then, distinctly, a single knock, as if someone were asking to be let in.

My heart was going very fast. I could feel the blue thread tight against my wrist, a tiny cord of warmth. I wanted to look behind me. Astrid's warning sat in my head like a stone. I kept my eyes on the candle.

"Who's there?" Astrid said, in a voice that was not quite her own.

The knocking stopped.

After a long moment, a voice answered. It came from inside the mound, and it was soft, almost kind, and it said my name.

Not Linnea. Linnea Berg, the way the teachers said it in the register. The way nobody in Lappmyra knew it yet, except for the people who had seen my school file.

I stopped breathing.

"That's not funny," I said, too loudly.

"Don't speak," Astrid hissed. "It isn't your turn."

The voice spoke again. It said something I couldn't quite hear, a shape of words in the wind, and then it laughed. It was a quiet, pleased, unbearably human laugh.

---

I don't remember standing up. I remember the candle going out all at once, as if someone had pinched it. I remember the snow rushing up to meet my knees, and Astrid's hand grabbing my arm so hard it hurt. I remember the birches behind us, their trunks bending very slightly towards the mound, like people turning to see who had walked in late to a film.

We ran. The wood seemed to stretch around us, the track longer than it had any right to be, and the wind came after us with that terrible breathing sound, rising and falling, almost keeping pace. At the edge of the village, Astrid stopped so suddenly I ran into her. She was shaking. Her face was white under the streetlight.

"You said my name," she gasped. "Out loud. You weren't supposed to speak."

"I didn't say anything!"

"You did. I heard you. Before it started."

I looked down at my wrist. The blue thread was still there, neat and tight. It was wet. When I touched it, my fingers came away smeared with something dark and cold, like river mud.

Astrid saw it too. Her mouth opened and no words came out.

"It followed us," she whispered.

From the direction of the birch wood, very faint, I heard the soft, pleased laugh again. Then the wind dropped, all at once, and the village was silent, and every window along the road was dark except one, far off at the end of the track, where a single lamp had been left burning in an old woman's back room.

---

I have told this story many times since. The people who listen usually want to know if it was a prank, or a bad dream, or some kind of hysteria brought on by three months of long nights and too much dark. I tell them I don't know. I tell them I was fifteen and that everything was a little bit true.

What I don't tell them is that I still have the thread. It's in a drawer in my flat in Stockholm, folded very small. Sometimes, late at night, when the wind moves past the window like something breathing, I take it out and turn it over in my fingers, and I wait, very carefully, for a knock that never comes.


## 11. Therapeutic Boundaries

The first thing Dr. Rebecca Tarrant says to me, when she comes around the padded table with her clipboard tucked under one arm, is, "You're still holding your breath, Marcus," and I hate that it's true.

I let it out. It comes out in a rush, a sort of wet, bitter sigh, and I hear her smile without looking at her. I don't need to look. I know that smile. I knew it in the changing rooms in Leeds when we were nineteen and she used to steal my tape and my trainers and then give them back, grinning, saying I'd been too slow to catch her.

"Lie down on your front," she says. "Face through the hole. Arms by your sides."

I lie down on the table. The paper sheet crackles under my chest. The face cradle is cold against my forehead, a padded horseshoe of vinyl that smells of antiseptic and something faintly floral that I suspect she bought to make the room less like a clinic. Through the hole I can see the grey carpet, a crumpled tissue, the toe of my own left trainer. The right one isn't there. It's in a bag in the corner, along with my kit, my medals and the framed photograph of the team that the club sent me in an envelope three weeks after the surgeon said the word irreversible.

"Right knee first," she says. Her hands settle on my calf, warm through the thin fabric of my shorts. "Tell me if anything's sharp."

"Everything's sharp."

"Good. That means you're still in there."

---

I was a sprinter. Four hundred metres, mostly, but I could run a six-forty-five quarter in the relay if they needed me to. The injury was a torn ACL, then a meniscus, then a second tear during a rehab session when I tried to rush things because I couldn't bear to sit still. The knee gave way on a bend in front of forty people and a physio who was not, I now realise, her.

Becky had been my training partner for four years, the person who ran the second lap next to me, the one who pushed me on the hills. We were the same height, almost, and she had a better stride and a worse temper. When I got my first major sponsorship, she laughed and bought me a pint and said I'd better not get fat. When I didn't make the final of the Europeans, she sat with me on the coach and didn't say a word, and that was worse than anything anyone could have said.

Now she is qualified, registered, the owner of a clinic with a view of the canal and a logo on the window that I try not to read. She is good at this. I hate that she's good at this.

"Keep the foot relaxed," she says, and begins to bend my knee.

---

Slowly. Her left hand cups the back of my heel to support it, her right slides under the top of my thigh to hold the hamstring in a gentle, controlled arc. The joint creaks. The muscle lifts under her palm, stringing tight from the back of my knee to the base of my glute. I feel the first thin line of pain, bright and specific, the way a wire feels when you press it against a tooth.

"Tell me where," she says.

"Here." I tap my thumb against the outside of the knee. "The fibres. Like someone's pulling a thread."

"Good. That's the scar tissue. It's supposed to hurt." She eases the leg back by two centimetres, and the wire slackens. "Breathe out as I go."

I breathe out. I can feel her thumb on the inside of my thigh, feather-light, almost absent-minded. It is perfectly professional. It is also the first time anyone has touched me without wanting something from my body in five months, and I despise the small, treacherous flare of heat that starts low in my stomach and refuses to leave.

Her breath catches. It's very quiet. It's almost nothing. But I hear it.

I don't look up. I'm not sure I could bear to see her face. We have spent four years running side by side without once letting the other see us look at each other for too long, and I can feel the old ache of that refusal sitting between us, heavy and unspoken, like a third person in the room.

"Stop tensing," she says. Her voice is steady, almost cool. "You're fighting me."

"I'm not fighting you."

"You're fighting the stretch. Your whole body wants to pull away. Let it go."

I let it go, a little. The leg moves another few degrees, and the pain flares sharp and white, and I grit my teeth and hear myself make a sound I don't recognise, low in my throat.

"Okay," she says quickly, and eases off. "Okay. We'll come back to it."

---

After the knee comes the hip. She rolls me onto my side, one arm under my hip, one hand guiding the top leg forward into a figure-four. The positioning is precise: my right ankle rests across my left knee, my left foot tucked behind my right thigh, the whole structure balanced on the padded edge of the table. Her palm sits flat on my outer hip, pressing gently inward. Her other hand grips my ankle and lifts, slowly, like someone opening a stiff door.

"Now the glute," she says. "Take a breath."

The stretch is a deep, grinding ache, the kind you feel in your teeth. I am sweating. Her hair has come loose from its clip and falls across her forehead, and she pushes it back with the inside of her wrist without taking her hand off my leg, and the gesture is so ordinary and so familiar that for a moment I'm nineteen again, lying on the grass by the track, watching her laugh at the sky.

"You've still got the same scar on your thumb," I say, before I can stop myself.

She glances down at her hand. "I was cutting the bread."

"Bread is dangerous."

"So is you." She shifts her grip, and the stretch deepens, and I hiss through my teeth. "Breathe."

"I am breathing."

"You're holding it again."

I let go. The air rushes out, and her palm flattens against my hip, and I feel the muscle loosen under her hands by a fraction. My chest is rising and falling too fast. She is so close I can smell the coffee on her breath and the sharp tang of the cleaning spray on her fingers.

Neither of us says anything for a long, humming moment. The clinic is very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall, a kettle begins to scream and then stops.

"Okay," she says, and her voice has gone thin. "That's enough for today."

---

She lets me sit up slowly. My leg feels heavy and strange, like it belongs to someone else. She stands with her back to the window and writes on her clipboard, and I watch her pen move, steady and deliberate, and try to read the words upside down.

"Your range is improving," she says, not looking up. "Sixty degrees on the flexion, which is better than last week. I want you to do the bridge exercises twice a day, and the band work on Thursdays. No running. Not yet."

"Not yet," I repeat. My voice sounds raw.

"Not yet." She finally looks at me. Her eyes are very bright, very steady, and there is something in them that is not professional at all, and both of us know it. "Marcus. You're going to hate every minute of this. And I'm going to make you do it anyway."

"That's the job."

"That's the job," she agrees. A small, tight smile. "Same time next week?"

I nod. I do not trust my voice.

She opens the door. The corridor beyond is bright and smells of disinfectant and burnt toast. I swing my legs off the table and reach for my crutch. My weight comes down on the bad knee, and the wire of pain pulls tight, and I stumble. Her hand shoots out and catches my elbow, firm and warm, and holds me upright.

We stand like that for a second too long, her fingers pressed into the bend of my arm, my breath short and shallow, her face inches from mine.

"Careful," she says softly.

"I'm always careful," I say, and it sounds like a lie to both of us.

She lets go. I take the crutch and hobble down the corridor without looking back, and I can feel her eyes on my back all the way to the door, steady and unreadable, the way she used to watch me run the last lap when I was too far ahead to catch.


## 12. High Seas and Low Vices

"Another day, Lieutenant? You'll wear a hole in the Company's patience."

Merrick did not look up from the bottle. "The Company's patience is a fine thing to be wearing, Thorne. I'd not want it worn to the bone on my account."

The shop was a stifling room off Thirteen Factories, its shutters half-drawn against the glare. The floor was packed earth, the walls hung with bamboo pipes and paper lanterns gone brown with smoke. Thorne, who had been a purser on the Hyacinth until his debts caught up with him, lowered himself onto the bench opposite and wiped his forehead with a silk handkerchief.

"You look like a man who has been dragged up the Pearl River by his ankles," he said.

"Thank you. I feel a great deal worse."

"Have you eaten?"

"I had a rice gruel at dawn, which tasted of the pot it was boiled in." Merrick turned the bottle in his hands. The glass was warm. Through its amber haze the light from the street fell in a single slanting bar across the floor. "I shall be eating a great deal better by noon, God willing."

"God is not much of a shopkeeper, Merrick."

"No. But Mr. Wu is, and he has been very patient with me."

---

The bottle had cost him a silver dollar and a promise, and the promise was already overdue. Mr. Wu's back room was reached by a narrow stair at the rear of the shop, past a row of cots where men lay in the grey half-sleep of the poppy, their faces slack, their breath slow and sweet. Merrick stepped over a boy of about fifteen who had curled himself up like a cat under a torn blanket. He did not look at the boy's face. He had found that not looking made the stairs easier.

Mr. Wu was waiting at the top. He was small and neat, dressed in dove-grey silk, a pair of spectacles perched on his nose like a pair of watchful birds. He bowed slightly.

"The Lieutenant is early."

"The Lieutenant is late, Mr. Wu. He has been late since Tuesday."

"Ah. But the Lieutenant has also been generous." Wu's eyes flicked to the pocket of Merrick's coat, where the silver clinked. "And the Lieutenant has brought a friend."

Merrick turned. In the doorway stood a tall, thin Englishman in a stained linen suit, his face as yellow as old paper, his fingers trembling against the frame.

"Stephen," said Merrick. "I'd not expected you here."

"Nor I you." Stephen Ludlow licked his cracked lips. "They say you've been selling the Hyacinth's spare cannon to the Hong merchants."

"They say a great many things. Most of them are true, and all of them are bad for business." Merrick held out the bottle. "Have a drink. You look like you've been sleeping in a ditch."

"I have."

"Then you're a rare sort of gentleman, for a man of the Company."

---

The back room was small and windowless, lit by a single lamp whose oil had turned rancid. The air was thick with smoke and the cloying, sickly sweetness that clung to the skin. Merrick settled himself on a low divan with a hiss of breath and took the pipe that Wu offered, its bowl packed with black tar, its stem warm from another man's mouth.

He drew on it slowly. The smoke went down like warm honey. For a while there was nothing in the world but the lamplight and the slow, exquisite loosening of his limbs, the terrible ease creeping in through his shoulders and down his spine. The dragon, the men here called it, and the name had always amused him. It was a more honest name than most.

When he surfaced, Ludlow was sitting cross-legged on the floor with his eyes closed, murmuring something about his mother's garden in Dorset.

"Stephen," Merrick said softly. "You owe me four dollars."

"I owe you nothing."

"You owe me four dollars and a favour, and I should like them both by the end of the week."

Ludlow opened one eye. "What sort of favour?"

"The kind a gentleman does for another gentleman when the Admiralty comes sniffing." Merrick reached for his coat. "There's a Chinese merchant called Pao who handles the Malwa boats out at Whampoa. I need a letter carried to his warehouse, unsealed, by someone who can be trusted not to read it."

"And you think I'm that someone."

"I think you're the only man in Canton who's more afraid of the Admiralty than I am."

---

Outside, the afternoon heat hung over the river like a cloth. The streets were crowded with coolies carrying bales of silk, with pedlars selling fans and lichee, with sailors from the Hyacinth pushing their way towards the brothels on Hog Lane. Merrick walked with his hat pulled low and his collar buttoned, though the sweat stuck the linen to his back. He had been a lieutenant for six years. He had sat at the Captain's table, dined with the Governor, been praised in dispatches for his conduct at the Pearl Bar. Now he slunk through the alleys like a dog that had learned to steal.

The dealer waited in a half-collapsed temple near the Thirteen Factories, a thin, pockmarked man named Lo, who sat on a stone step cleaning his nails with a knife.

"The Lieutenant owes me," Lo said, without looking up.

"The Lieutenant has come to pay what he owes."

"Good. Then the Lieutenant can tell me how the Hyacinth's spare cannon fared with Hong."

Merrick's mouth went dry. "How did you know about the cannon?"

Lo shrugged. "Everyone in Canton knows about the cannon, Lieutenant. You sold them to a man who sold them to the Cohong, who sold them back to the Viceroy's men. You're very famous."

"That's a fine thing to be, in this town."

"It is the only thing in this town," said Lo. "Now. Money or favour?"

"Money," said Merrick. "Today."

Lo took the knife from his lap and laid it across his knees, very deliberately. "And tomorrow?"

Merrick said nothing. He thought of the bar of light across the shop floor, the bottle warm in his hands, the boy curled under his blanket. He thought of the Captain's face when he had asked for a leave of absence, and the long pause before the answer came.

"Tomorrow," he said at last, "I'll have something worth more than money. A letter from the Governor's office, perhaps. Or a manifest from Whampoa with the right stamps on it. You'll tell me what you need."

Lo smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile. "The Lieutenant is a poor liar. It's what I like about him."

---

By dusk the city had turned gold and then blue. Merrick walked back along the Thirteen Factories with a little silver in his pocket and a thin brown packet inside his waistcoat, its paper crackling each time he breathed. The lanterns were being lit along the river. The fishing boats slid out on the tide with their small square sails catching the last of the light.

At the corner by the Hong gate he stopped to lean against a wall, because his legs had begun to shake again, and the dragon was calling from somewhere behind his eyes. A child came down the lane with a basket of oranges, looked up at him with an expression of tired curiosity, and walked on.

Merrick watched the child go. He thought of his own mother in Bath, writing to him each week in her neat, anxious hand, asking if he had been to church. He thought of the Captain's voice, sharp and kind: You were the best of us, Merrick. Don't make me say so in the past tense.

He straightened his coat and pushed off the wall.

The Company would want its lieutenant back by morning. The dragon would want him back by midnight. And somewhere in between, there was a boy with a torn blanket and a cat's curled spine who would wake up in an hour, hungry, and reach for whatever was left.

"Tomorrow," Merrick said under his breath, to nobody at all, and walked on into the blue dark of Canton.

## 14. Lost & Found in Osaka

"Are you going to keep staring, or are you going to tell me what that is?"

Mio stopped with her hand halfway to the pocket of her cardigan. She had not realised she was staring. She had been standing outside the Akihabara-style shop on the corner of Den Den Town, the one with the wall of second-hand synthesisers and the faded poster for a 1987 keyboard tournament, and the sound had come through the open door like someone pouring warm honey over a gravel path. A guitar, but wrong. Brighter than a guitar. Each string bent and cut and wound back on itself until it sounded like a question being answered in the wrong order.

Then she realised the sound was coming from the street, not the shop.

The busker was sitting on an upturned milk crate near the entrance to the arcade, a battered sunburst Telecaster across her knees, one leg crossed over the other. She was maybe nineteen or twenty. Her hair was dyed a pale copper and tied up in a knot that looked like it had been done with a pencil. She wore a faded denim jacket with the sleeves rolled up and a pair of enormous headphones around her neck, the old kind with the coiled cable. There was a tip jar at her feet with three hundred-yen coins in it.

"It's the theme from Vanguard Ninja," Mio said, before she could stop herself. "The second opening. But you're playing the bridge from the fourth episode, the one with the chiptune breakdown. Nobody does the bridge."

The busker's fingers stopped. She looked up, and her grin was immediate and wide, like a window thrown open.

"You know the fourth episode?"

"Everyone knows the fourth episode. It's the one where the sound drops out for eleven seconds."

"And then the bass comes back underneath it." The busker tapped the Telecaster twice against her knee. "Nobody gets that. They think I'm just being annoying."

"You're not being annoying."

"Thanks." The grin widened. "I'm Saki. You're staring at my amp, which, to be fair, is a very good amp. Are you going to tell me what you're carrying, or do I have to guess?"

Mio looked down at the paper bag in her hand. She had forgotten about it. "It's a Korg. A Monologue. I got it secondhand from the shop on the third floor. The filter's cracked, but the oscillator's fine."

Saki's eyebrows went up. "A Monologue. Are you serious? That thing's basically a brick with opinions."

"It's got a good sawtooth."

"It's got an incredible sawtooth," Saki said, and then, a beat later, "Sorry. That came out weird. I'm bad at talking to people when I'm excited."

"It's fine." Mio shifted her weight. Her shoulders had come up to her ears. She could feel the familiar heat climbing her neck, the one that made her wish she could fold herself into the bag and be carried away. She said, "I'm bad at it too."

"At talking?"

"At talking to people who aren't other people who like synthesisers."

Saki laughed, a real laugh, not a polite one. "Okay. So we're both bad. That's a start." She set the Telecaster on its stand, carefully, the way you'd put down a sleeping cat. "Have you eaten?"

"I, um. No."

"Good. Do you like sushi?"

"Yes?"

"Good answer. There's a kaitenzushi two streets over, the one with the belt that goes round in a loop and the tea that tastes like it's been boiled in a bucket. I'm starving. My treat."

"You don't have to pay for me."

"I know I don't have to. I'm offering because I'd like to hear what the Monologue sounds like with the filter open, and I'd like to tell you why the bridge from episode four is the best eleven seconds of television ever made." Saki was already packing the amp into a canvas case. "Also, I've been out here since ten, and I've made nine hundred yen, and I need someone to tell me I'm not a complete failure."

Mio hesitated. The street was loud. A cart selling takoyaki was rattling past, and the arcade doors kept opening and closing, spilling blue light and the electric chatter of machines. Somewhere above them a loudspeaker was announcing a discount on keyboards.

"I've never been to a kaitenzushi alone," she said.

---

"Good. Bring the Monologue. I want to hear the sawtooth in person."

Mio reached for a plate of salmon, took it, and found that her hand was steadier than it had been all afternoon.

Mio ate her salmon, and on the belt the plates kept moving past them, and outside the windows the lights of Den Den Town came on one by one, bright and buzzing, like a sound that had been waiting for someone to tune it.


## 15. The Watch That Ran Backwards (Teen Horror)

Dylan had always thought his grandmother's watch would be the most boring thing he'd ever own, which was why he'd been so surprised to find that it wasn't a watch at all. It was a Christmas present from his mum, a cheap grey plastic thing with a fat black screen and a button on the side that said REWIND in tiny letters underneath.

"It's a smartwatch, Dyl," his mum had said, looking pleased with herself. "It tracks your heart rate and your steps and your sleep. And it has a 'time-reset' feature. The guy in the shop said it's for emergencies."

"What kind of emergencies?"

"Like if you forget your homework." She winked. "You're twelve, love. You'll forget everything."

Dylan had laughed and put it on. He had not known, then, that the watch would be the first thing in his life that didn't do exactly what it said.

---

The first time he pressed the button was in Mrs. Hallworth's maths lesson, three weeks after Christmas, when he had been staring at a page of fractions and feeling sick about the test on Friday. He pressed REWIND on a whim, just to see what it would do.

The classroom flickered, like a film reel jumping. Then he was sitting at the same desk, the same pencil in his hand, the same chalk squeak from the board. The clock on the wall said 9:42. Ten seconds earlier it had said 9:43.

Dylan stared at his watch. The screen had changed. In the top corner, a small green number said 1.

He pressed it again, thinking it was a glitch. The room flickered and jumped back another ten seconds. The number changed to 2.

"Dylan Pratt, are you listening?" said Mrs. Hallworth.

"Yes, miss. Sorry, miss."

He spent the rest of the lesson pretending he understood fractions and trying not to think about what the number meant.

---

It meant he was getting older.

He realised it slowly, over the next week. The first sign was his voice. It cracked on a Tuesday in the lunch queue, a high squeak that made Ryan Ashby snort into his chocolate milk, and then deepened into something that sounded almost like a grown man's voice when he said sorry to the dinner lady. The second sign was his height. His trousers, which had been fine on Monday, were suddenly three inches too short. Mum bought him new ones and said he'd had a growth spurt, and looked at him in a funny, searching way over the top of the washing basket.

The third sign was the hair on his upper lip. He noticed it in the bathroom mirror one morning, a soft, dark shadow that hadn't been there before. He touched it with one finger and felt something strange flutter in his chest, half-proud and half-terrified.

The watch was still green. The number in the corner said 9.

He checked the internet on his phone that night, hiding under the duvet with the torch on. It told him there was a thing called accelerated puberty. It said it was rare and usually caused by hormones or illness, and that children who went through it could suddenly look years older than they were.

Dylan put the phone down and stared at the ceiling.

Nine times, he thought. Nine times back, nine times older.

He did not think he was ill. He had never felt better. That was almost the worst part.

---

People started treating him differently.

Mrs. Hallworth stopped calling him "Dylan, love" and started calling him "Mr. Pratt" in a careful, polite voice, as though he were a visitor. Ryan Ashby, who had once shoved him into a bush for a dare, now asked him for advice about girls in the playground, shuffling his feet and looking embarrassed. The girls in Year Seven, who had always ignored him, started giggling when he walked past the library, and one of them, Hannah Chen, actually asked him if he had a girlfriend.

"I don't," said Dylan, horrified.

"You should," said Hannah, and skipped off with her friends.

He went home and looked at the watch. The number said 11.

That night he had a dream about a grown man in a long coat walking down a street he didn't recognise, and in the dream, the man turned round and looked at him with his own face.

---

The disaster happened on a Saturday morning.

He had decided, somewhere in the night, that he would stop. He would stop pressing the button. He would wait until the watch wore off, or until he worked out how to fix it. He sat at the kitchen table with his cereal and his shaking hands and made a promise to himself.

Then the phone rang. It was Ryan. His voice was thick and panicked.

"Dyl, it's Mikey. He's been in the canal. He's not breathing right. Please, you've got to come."

Dylan's stomach dropped. He grabbed his coat, the watch glinting on his wrist, and ran.

He got to the canal bank at the same time as the ambulance. Mikey Ashby was lying on the towpath, pale and still, his mum kneeling beside him with her hands shaking. There were people everywhere, shouting, phones held up, a dog barking. Dylan's chest was tight and his legs were trembling.

If I could just go back, he thought. If I could go back five minutes, I could tell him not to climb on the railings. I could stop him.

His thumb found the button.

He pressed it.

The world flickered. The towpath, the ambulance, the crowd—they stuttered like a broken TV. And then Dylan was standing on the canal bank again, but the sky was wrong. It was a dull, bruised purple. The water was black and oily. The ambulance was not there. The crowd was not there. There was only a line of old, rusted railings, a dog lying dead on the path, and a figure in a long coat walking towards him, very slowly, with a face he knew.

It was his own face. Grey at the temples, lined around the mouth, eyes full of an exhausted terror.

"Don't," said the older Dylan. "Don't press it again. You'll make it worse."

"What did I do?"

"You changed the timeline, you idiot. You made Mikey climb the railings two minutes earlier, because you wanted to stop him, and then you stopped him, and then the dog ran into the canal, and then—" He pressed his hand to his face. "It's too late. You've got to reverse it. You've got to do it once more."

"But I'll get older."

"You're already older. You're going to be me in three days if you do nothing." The older Dylan gripped his arm. His hand was cold and rough. "There's a button on the other side. It's the only way to put it back. It's the only way to save Mikey."

"And what about you?"

The older man smiled, a thin, tired smile that did not reach his eyes. "I'm the one who didn't press it."

---

Dylan woke up on the towpath with his face in the cold mud.

Someone was shaking his shoulder. It was Ryan, his voice raw with relief. "Dyl! Dyl, come on. He's breathing. He's breathing!"

Dylan lifted his head. Mikey was sitting up beside his mum, coughing up canal water, his face streaked with tears and dirt. The ambulance crew were wrapping him in a silver blanket. Mrs. Ashby was sobbing into his hair.

Dylan looked at his watch.

The screen was black. Then, very slowly, a single green number flickered into view in the corner.

1.

He was twelve again. His trousers fitted. His voice was a child's voice. The hair on his lip was gone. His reflection in the dark screen was small and wide-eyed and frightened, and it was smiling back at him, as if it had known all along.

He did not know if he had saved Mikey, or if he had only borrowed a few minutes from the future and given them back with interest. He did not know if the other Dylan would ever come back, or whether the man in the long coat was still walking somewhere, waiting for him to make the right choice.

He only knew that the watch was still warm against his wrist, and that somewhere, deep in the plastic, a small green number was waiting for him to be brave enough to press it one more time.

Dylan Pratt put his hand over the button and held it there, very still, until the ambulance pulled away and the canal went quiet and the sun finally broke through the grey.


## 16. Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

She stopped just before the shooting gallery, where the boardwalk planks gave way to a fringe of sawdust and spilt lemonade. It was near dusk, and the carnival lights had come on all at once, a buzzing constellation strung between the Wonder Wheel and the rooftops of Surf Avenue. The air smelled of frying dough, hot sugar and the cold brine blowing in off the Atlantic. His locks. The words came again, unbidden, an old-fashioned turn of phrase that had lodged itself in her head since the moment she'd read his note. She pressed them down. Something else rose to replace them, something dangerously warm, and she pressed that down too, lest she start asking herself why she had come back here at all.

The shooting gallery's proprietor, a barrel-chested man in a striped vest, was rolling a cigarette with yellowed fingers. He glanced at her, then at the clock above the candy stand, then back at her. "He's late," he said, not unkindly. "Or you're early. Depends who you ask."

"I'm not waiting for anyone."

"Sure, sweetheart." He licked the paper. "Everybody on this boardwalk is waiting for somebody."

She turned away from him and walked to the rail. Below, the surf rolled up grey and heavy against the pilings. This was the place. The bench by the bumper cars, the wooden railing with the carved initials, the cedar-smelling corner where the crowd thinned out after dark. He had chosen it. She had agreed to it, in a flurry of ink and carelessness, without ever asking his name. Under the clock at the shooting gallery, Saturday, when the lights come on. That was all he had written. No signature, only a faint scent of bay rum and something sharper underneath, like matches struck in a damp room.

She had folded the note into the pocket of her good wool coat and carried it for three days. She had taken it out on the subway, on the bench at Coney Island, on the stairs of her building, and every time she read it she felt the same pull, half curiosity and half warning, like a current under calm water.

"Miss?"

She turned. A young man was standing a few feet away, hat in his hands, the brim turning slowly through his fingers. He was not the stranger. She knew that at once, with a small, sharp disappointment she did not care to examine.

"I'm sorry," he said. "You dropped this." He held out a silk glove, pale grey, the fingers still curled as if around something.

"That isn't mine."

"It was by the gallery. I thought—" He trailed off, uncertain, and then his gaze moved past her shoulder and his face changed. Something frightened, almost reverent.

She heard the footsteps before she turned. Measured, unhurried, the heels of polished shoes clicking on the boards.

He stood at the end of the railing with the carnival light behind him, so that for a moment his face was in shadow. Then he stepped forward and it was lit, and her breath caught in a way she resented. Dark hair, black as the sea at night, falling in loose curls over a high forehead. His eyes were darker still, deep and unreadable, and they held hers with a steadiness that felt almost like a hand laid flat against her chest.

"You came," he said. His voice was low, a little rough at the edges, the vowels soft and clipped in a way she could not place.

"You wrote that I should."

"I wrote that you could." He smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile, not quite. It was the smile of a man who had already decided how this would end and was merely curious about the route. "There's a difference, and I suspect you know it."

The young man with the hat had quietly retreated. The gallery proprietor had gone back to his cigarette. The boardwalk seemed to empty around them, the way a room does when two people stop speaking at once.

"You haven't told me your name," she said.

"No. I haven't." He tilted his head, looking her over slowly, from the pins in her hair down to the buttons of her coat and back again. "Does that trouble you, Miss—?"

"I don't give my name to men who won't give theirs."

"Then we're at an impasse." He leaned one forearm on the rail, close enough that she could smell the bay rum again, and the sharp undertone beneath it. "Perhaps I'll give you something else instead. Tell me, do you always come to strangers' appointments alone?"

"Do you always invite women to them under false pretences?"

"Is that what you think I've done?" He laughed softly, as if she had told him an excellent joke. "I've been very honest with you. I told you the hour, the place. I even told you to wear the grey."

"I'm wearing the grey."

"I noticed." His eyes dropped to the coat, the pale wool, then back up. "You look like a woman who's come to find out whether she's brave enough to be fooled."

Her pulse was loud in her ears. Somewhere behind them, the carousel organ began to play, thin and sweet and slightly out of tune, and the sound climbed over the surf and the frying dough and the shouts from the Cyclone.

"And are you going to fool me?" she asked.

"I'm going to show you the gallery." He nodded towards the stall, where a row of tin ducks bobbed on their wire in the coloured light. "I'm rather good at it. Ten shots, a dollar. If I win, you tell me why you really came."

"And if I win?"

He turned his head, slowly, and his gaze moved over her face with a deliberate, unhurried attention that made her skin prickle beneath her gloves. "Then I'll tell you my name," he said, very quietly, "and you'll wish you hadn't asked for it."

She should walk away. She knew it as clearly as she knew the taste of salt on the wind. Instead she heard herself say, in a voice that was almost steady, "Fine. Load the rifle."

He smiled again, and this time she could see the edge in it, bright and sharp as broken glass, and he stepped towards the stall with the easy, dangerous grace of a man who had never once in his life been told no.


## 17. Caribou Season at the Lodge

The lodge was dark when they got back from the second day, and JT didn't like that. His father had always left the porch light on, a yellow bulb in a wire cage that drew the moths in the summer and the snow in the winter, and it had been on every autumn of JT's life. Tonight the porch was black. The windows were black. The only light came from the truck's headlamps as Mike swung the wheel around and cut the engine, and in the sudden silence the ticking of the cooling metal sounded like someone counting.

"Power's out," Mike said. He sounded relieved. JT could tell he was pretending to be relieved.

"The generator's in the shed," JT said.

"I know where the generator is, JT."

They got out. The air was thick and sweet with the smell of pine and cold wet leaves and something underneath that JT could not name, something like the inside of a freezer that had been left open. Their father was already on the porch, a dark shape against the bigger dark of the trees. He did not turn around.

"Dad?"

"Go get the generator," their father said. His voice was calm and pleasant, the voice he used for customers at the hardware store. "Mike, you'll want to see to the stove. It's going to be a cold one."

"Dad, the caribou—"

"The caribou are fine." He turned, and the headlamps caught his face for a moment, and JT saw that his eyes were too bright, the way they had been in the clearing. "They're just out there, JT. Keeping us company."

JT looked past his father, out into the trees.

At first he thought it was the shadows. Then he thought it was a trick of the headlights on the snow, which had started falling in thin, slanting threads. But the shapes were too still for shadows and too tall for trees, and there were more of them than he could count. They stood at the edge of the clearing in a loose ring, heads lowered, breath steaming very faintly in the cold. Their antlers caught the light like the branches of dead trees. Nobody moved.

"How many?" JT whispered.

"Enough," said Mike, behind him, in a voice that sounded like it had been dragged over gravel.

JT went to the shed. The generator was where it always was, beside the stacked cords of firewood, but the door to the back room, the cold room where they hung the meat, was open. He had shut it himself that afternoon. He was sure of it. He could remember the bolt sliding home, the rattle of the hasp, the small satisfied click.

Inside, the first caribou hung from the rafter by its hind legs. It had been skinned and gutted, the way his father always did it, the pale meat clean and dry. But the hide was not in the bin where it should have been. It was folded on the workbench, neatly, the way his mother used to fold their good tablecloths, fur side up, the four corners tucked under. Beside it, laid out in a row, were the knife, the bone saw, and a pair of his father's gloves. The gloves were stiff with dried blood.

JT stared at them until the cold made his eyes water. Then he backed out of the room and pulled the door shut with both hands.

When he came back to the porch, the generator humming behind him and the bulb in its wire cage flickering yellow over the steps, his father was sitting on the top stair with his hands folded between his knees. He looked up and smiled.

"There you are," he said. "Good boy. Did you find the fuel?"

"Dad. Did you skin the caribou?"

His father's smile did not change. It just stayed there, very still, as if someone had painted it on. "Of course I did. Same as always."

"The hide's on the bench. It's folded."

"Well, that's where I'd leave it." He stood, slowly, one hand on the railing, and the joints in his knees made a faint popping sound. "You're tired, son. We all are. It's been a long season."

"There's blood on your gloves."

"There's always blood on my gloves, JT. That's what hunting is."

Mike came out onto the porch, carrying two cups of coffee in his bare hands, steam rising from them like smoke from a fire that had burned out a long time ago. He handed one to JT without looking at him. His face was pale and drawn, and he kept glancing past the porch rail into the trees.

"They're closer," Mike said.

JT did not look. He could feel them, though, the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. A pressure on his neck. A patience that was not quite hunger.

"Dad," Mike said. "The buck. The one from the clearing. Did you take the shot?"

Their father was quiet for a long moment. He looked at the trees. Then he looked at his own right hand, turning it over slowly in the yellow light, examining the palm as though it belonged to someone else.

"No," he said at last, and his voice was soft and wondering. "No. I don't think I did. I just wanted to see if it would let me touch it."

"Did it?"

"Oh yes." He smiled again, and this time it looked real, which was worse. "It was very warm. Like a horse. It was waiting for me to ask."

JT drank his coffee. It was cold and bitter and he could not taste it. Above the lodge the snow kept falling, silent and patient, and across the clearing the shapes stood their ground, their breath rising in a slow white cloud that drifted toward the porch and hung there, as if listening.

Later, when they had all gone inside and bolted the door and lain down in their clothes, JT heard the scrape of hooves on the porch boards. Slow, deliberate, one after another. Then a long pause, and a sound he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe, like someone breathing against a window.

In the morning, the porch was empty. The snow was undisturbed except for a single line of prints leading from the steps to the edge of the trees, and those prints were not deer tracks, and they were not boot tracks, and they stopped, very neatly, at the spot where his father had been sitting.

His father was in the kitchen, making eggs. He hummed while he cooked. He did not mention the caribou, or the hide, or the gloves. When JT asked him, he only looked up from the stove and smiled, and said, "What caribou?"


## 18. Hell is Other Demons

The morning arrived in Hell the way it always did, which is to say it didn't arrive at all, but the clocks in the Ninth Circle agreed on it anyway, and that counted for something. Lucifer Morningstar stood in the kitchen of the Palace in his dressing gown, the one with the faded embroidery of stars that Lilith had once called "tragically optimistic," and filled the kettle from a tap that ran a faintly sulphurous red for the first thirty seconds.

He waited for it to clear. It always cleared. He had never found out why.

The kettle took its time. He had long ago stopped trying to hurry things in Hell. Everything here arrived late, cost too much, and came back for a second helping whether you ordered it or not. The Palace had been designed by a committee of architects who had all been damned for shoddy workmanship, which explained the staircase that went up one floor and then turned into a pond.

He poured the tea and carried it to the window. Outside, the queue for the Fourth Circle was already snaking through the Plaza of Fair Grievances, the damned standing in their ranks with the patient despair of people who had been told the wait would be short. "You're up early," said a voice behind him.

"I'm always up early." He didn't turn round. "It's a hell of a thing, being the morning."

"You're the morning and you're also the evening, and the afternoons are a mess. You told me that in 1893."

"I was drunk in 1893."

"You're drunk in most centuries."

He turned. Lilith stood in the doorway holding a paper bag from the Earthside grocer, the one on Winter Lane that she had somehow persuaded to deliver to a sub-basement without asking a single question. "Lemons," she said, holding up the bag. "And the good bread. And a pot of that jam you pretend not to like."

"I like the jam."

"You like it when I make it." She set the bag on the counter. "I've come about the sofa."

Lucifer sighed. "The sofa has been a problem for a very long time."

"It was very reasonably priced."

"Everything you buy is reasonably priced until it starts to move at night."

He set his tea down. The thing about Lilith, in all their centuries of arguments, was that she never once came to Hell to be cruel. She came to be annoying in very specific ways, which he found more intimate than any amount of brimstone. "No." She began unpacking the bag onto the counter with a tidiness that suggested she had already decided where everything went. "I'm here because you've been quiet. Quieter than the usual quiet. "I like standing up. It's good for the spine."

"Lucifer."

He looked at the lemons. They were yellow and perfectly ordinary, as if they had been picked from a tree that had never once been near a fire. He picked one up and turned it over, feeling the cool weight of it in his palm.

"It's been a slow season," he said. "The damned are very dull lately. "That sounds exhausting."

"It is. Mostly I just sit in the chair and sign things."

"Both. We've always been good at both."

Behind them, the kettle went cold on the stove, and the tea sat in its cup growing a thin skin, and the woman in the grey coat finally reached the front of the queue, only to be told her number had expired and she would have to start again.

On the way out, Lucifer paused beside the sofa and gave it a kick. It made a noise like someone clearing their throat in a cathedral.

"Behave," said Lilith.

"Never," said the Morning Star, and held the door.


## 19. Historical Fiction: The Sand and the Sun

The arena at Capua smelled of blood and wet sand, and Marcus Vedius had long ago stopped noticing either. He noticed the sun instead. It came slanting through the gaps in the awning as the morning wore on, warm as a hand across his shoulders, and he thought, as he always did at this hour, that it was the only honest thing in the whole place.

"Gods, Vedius. Think about your breakfast like a sensible man."

Above them, through the gap in the wall, Marcus could hear the city. The clatter of carts on the Via Appia. The shouts of hawkers. A woman singing something from the East, high and mournful. Somewhere a dog barked, and a child laughed, and a bell rang for the third hour. It was the sound of a world that had not been built for him, going about its business without a backward glance.

---

He did not look at the trainer. He did not need to. The trainer's voice came from the far end of the yard, sharp and bored, calling out drills the way a man calls out the price of fish.

Somewhere in the middle, she says, "I missed you, you know. This week. I kept thinking, I'll tell her about that thing on the bus and then I'd remember you were busy."

I was not busy. I was sitting in my flat rehearsing a breakup with someone who was sitting in her flat thinking of me.

"I missed you too," I say, and I'm surprised by how true it is, how the sentence doesn't need to be argued with at all. For a moment I'm not building anything. I'm just here with a bowl of noodles and a person who has remembered how I take my tea.

Then my phone buzzes on the table. It's her, from the other side of the table, because she's sent me a photo of the view from her window three days ago and I hadn't replied. I look at it. Her thumb is on the screen, and then she puts it down and smiles at me like she's forgotten the device exists.

I look at the photo on my phone, the grey roofs and the one tree, and feel the case starting up again, a quiet clicking at the back of my skull. She sent that before she sat down. She wanted to be sure she'd been thinking of me before she saw me. Which is kind. Which means she's guilty about something. Which means something.

I know. I know what I'm doing. I can see the exhibit. I can see my own hand reaching for it.

"Thanks for the ramen," I say, and she grins and says anytime, and I put my phone face down on the table and pick up my chopsticks and try, for the next twenty minutes, to think of nothing except the warmth of the broth and the way she's laughing at something I said that I don't remember saying.

On the walk to the station, she links her arm through mine. I let her. The cold has sharpened everything. When we reach the barrier she turns and kisses me on the cheek, quick and sure.

"Text me when you're home," she says. "Please. I want to know you got back."

"Okay," I say, and I mean it, and then I spend the whole Overground ride home holding my phone in my lap, waiting for a message that says the thing I'm already writing in my head.

It comes at 9:47. Home safe? Had a really lovely time. Love you, dummy.

I read it three times. The third time, I start drafting the reply that will tell her I'm fine, and then the one that will tell her I'm not, and then, because I can't help it, the one that begins I've been thinking about us, and somewhere in the middle of that sentence I stop and put the phone down and sit very still in the dark kitchen, and tell myself, out loud, to breathe.

It doesn't work straight away. It never does. But it works a little, and that has to be enough for tonight.


## 24. Blood Debt

The temple had been dead for a hundred years, and the men who used it now had taken care to keep it that way. The roof over the eastern hall had collapsed in a fan of broken tile, and the rain came through in thin silver ropes that drummed on the flagstones and pooled in the cracks between them. Lanterns hung from the surviving rafters, each one wrapped in red cloth so the light fell brown and warm across the floor. The crowd stood in a horseshoe around the open space in the centre, shoulder to shoulder, shouting bets over the sound of the rain.

Kure Hayato stood at the north edge of the floor with his weight on his back foot. He was twenty-three and had trained since he was seven under a master who had taught him that a fight was decided before the first step. His hair was tied tight. His jacket had been stripped off and folded on a stone bench behind him. The Jigen-ryū stance was low and square, the front foot angled in, the hips set, the hands held loose at the sides, ready to snap out in the short, explosive bursts the school was famous for. Nine generations of his family had died in this posture, and he had no intention of being the tenth.

Across the floor, Zhao Bo stood at the south edge, and he was not in any stance at all.

He looked, to Hayato, like a man waiting for a bus. Shoulders loose, knees bent just enough to sink into the ground, arms hanging so relaxed they seemed to belong to someone else. His weight rocked from one foot to the other, slow and steady, a metronome nobody else could hear. He wore grey cotton trousers and a sleeveless vest, and his left forearm was wrapped in a strip of black cloth, the old Piguaquan way, to take the force of a parry without bruising the bone.

"Begin," said the old man who had been appointed referee, and then, because nobody had ever told him what else to say, he stepped back into the crowd and raised his hand.

Hayato moved first.

He did not waste the first second. He stepped forward with the front foot, a short, hard lunge, and the right fist drove out in a straight line toward Zhao Bo's chest. A blow from the Jigen school was meant to end things in a single exchange. It went fast, it went straight, and it expected the other man to flinch.

Zhao Bo did not flinch. He turned his body a few degrees to the left, the way a door swings on a hinge, and the fist passed his ribs by the width of a hand. At the same moment, his right arm came up from the hip in a flat, loose arc, not a punch, more a whip, and the back of his forearm slapped against Hayato's wrist with a sound like a wet sheet snapping on a line.

Hayato's arm flew wide. His balance tilted with it.

Zhao Bo had already stepped inside the arc. His left hand came up under Hayato's elbow and pushed, not hard, just enough to rotate the younger man's shoulder into the air. Then the right hand circled round, low, toward the back of Hayato's knee, and Hayato felt the joint buckle a fraction before he understood what had happened.

He pulled his weight back and dropped into the square stance, the old instinct taking over, and scraped his back foot across the wet flagstones to recover his centre. His knee screamed a warning. He had given up a half-second and a little of his footing, and he knew it.

Zhao Bo grinned for the first time, a quick flash of teeth, and stepped back to where he had started, his heels back on the same two stones.

"Good," he said mildly. "Again."

Hayato came again.

This time he was more careful. He kept his hips low and his guard tight and did not overreach. He circled to the left, bringing his front foot across the line of the temple floor so that the lanterns were at his back and the rain was in Zhao Bo's eyes. He pressed in with short, sharp jabs, one, two, the knuckles aimed at the centre of the chest, forcing the other man to give ground. Zhao Bo gave a step, then another, and Hayato felt the surge of the crowd behind him, the roar and the shouting, and his blood came up to meet it.

Then Zhao Bo stopped giving ground.

He dropped his weight, shifted his left foot a few inches behind his right, and let the second jab pass over his head. The fist sliced through the air where his face had been, and in the same instant Zhao Bo's hips rolled forward, a low, whipping turn that brought his right elbow up in a short arc into Hayato's ribs. Not a full blow. Enough to make the breath come out of Hayato in a sharp grunt and his guard drop by the width of a palm.

The left hand followed, a backfist, fast and light, and it cracked against Hayato's cheekbone. The crowd gasped. Hayato's head snapped to the side, and for a moment the lanterns were a smear of red and gold, and the rain was on his face, cold and loud.

He recovered by instinct, twisting his torso back toward the centre and throwing a long, straight kick at Zhao Bo's thigh. It was the one thing the Jigen school trained harder than any other, the kick that came from the hip and cracked like a whip. It landed. Zhao Bo's leg buckled, and he went down on one knee, and for one heartbeat the crowd surged forward, shouting Hayato's name.

Hayato lunged in for the finish, his right hand drawn back to strike at the exposed neck.

Zhao Bo was not there.

He had dropped and rolled, sideways, the way a cat rolls when a dog comes at it, and as he came up he was behind Hayato's left side, close enough to feel the heat of the younger man's back. His left hand clamped on Hayato's collar. His right hand struck out, not with a fist but with the flat of the palm, once, hard, against the back of Hayato's right elbow.

The joint bent the wrong way, not enough to break, but enough. Hayato's arm locked and his strike went wide, past the empty air, and his whole body came around in a clumsy half-circle under the pull of his own momentum.

Zhao Bo let go of his collar and stepped back. He did not follow up. He simply waited.

Hayato turned, breathing hard, his cheek bruised, his right arm hanging stiff. The stone floor was slippery under his feet. He could feel the grit of the rain in his teeth. He understood, with a cold, sick clarity, that the fight had not been about strength. It had been about angles. Zhao Bo had stood on the same two stones since the beginning, and every blow Hayato had thrown had been absorbed, turned, and returned along a line Hayato had not yet learned to see.

"Your school is very proud of its straight lines," Zhao Bo said quietly. He was still on his feet, his breathing barely raised. "Straight lines are fast. But a wall is also straight."

"Then come and be the wall," Hayato said, and lifted his left hand into a guard.

Zhao Bo smiled, almost sadly, and raised his own arms, the forearms loose, the wrapped one forward.

They came together in the centre of the floor. Hayato threw the left fist in a short arc and Zhao Bo caught it on his wrapped forearm, the sound a flat, heavy thud, and his right hand swept under Hayato's wrist and pressed down. Hayato's knee came up to the other man's thigh, and Zhao Bo sank under it, his hip driving into Hayato's side, and for a moment they were chest to chest, grappling, each man's hand clamped on the other's shoulder, both fighting for a centre that was rapidly disappearing.

Hayato stamped his heel down and tried to sweep. Zhao Bo lifted his leg, stepped over it, and let his own weight carry them both sideways. They fell together across the wet stone, one body rolling over the other, and came up again on opposite sides of the floor, each of them clutching their ribs and staring.

The crowd roared.

Hayato got to his feet first. He could feel the blood on his lip, hot and salt. His arm had begun to tremble with the effort of holding the guard. He stood in the square stance once more, back foot planted, hands loose, and looked across the temple at the man in grey cotton, who was watching him with a patience that felt almost like respect.

"Again," said Zhao Bo.

And Hayato, who had been taught that a fight ended with a single blow, realised for the first time in his life that he was not yet sure which one of them had the straighter line.


## 25. Breaking Formation

The first thing Seo-yeon said to me was not my name. It was the count.

"Five, six, seven, eight," she barked from the front of the mirrored studio, her heels striking the floor like a metronome that had been personally offended. "Again. Yours is late on the seven. You're arriving at the eight like you're waiting for a bus."

I was waiting for a bus. I was waiting for the bus that would take me back to Newark, to the cracked concrete courts behind Hillside Avenue where nobody had ever once called out a count, where the beat came from a Bluetooth speaker on a shopping cart and you moved however your body decided to move. I had been in Seoul for eleven days. I had been in this room for six hours a day. And my body had not yet decided to move like anyone's but its own.

"Five, six, seven, eight."

The routine was called Glass Frequency, and it was seventy-two bars long, and it had been choreographed by a man in Gangnam who had apparently never once been to a party where people danced. Every move had a place. Every place had a line. Twelve of us stood in three rows of four across the studio floor, sixteen-count phrases dropping like hammer blows, and when the left foot went out, it went out at exactly the same angle as the nine other left feet on the floor. In a popping battle, angles were a suggestion. Here they were law.

I came in on the first step of the phrase: a sharp side-step, weight dropping through the knees, arms cutting across the chest in a clean horizontal line. My hips wanted to follow the arms, to roll through the sternum the way they had done since I was twelve years old in front of the bodega on Dolan Street. Instead, they froze, and my right shoulder jammed up near my ear, and I heard the faint, unmistakable snap of a count going off the rails.

"Kiara." Seo-yeon's voice cut across the room without raising a single decibel. "Stop."

Everything stopped. Eleven pairs of eyes swivelled toward me, and I felt my face go hot.

"What was that?" she asked.

"Sorry. My hips—"

"Your hips are not in the choreography. Your hips are in your memory of a bodega." She took three steps down the line, her black practice shoes silent on the sprung floor, and stopped in front of me. She was small, perhaps five foot two, and she looked up at me as though I were a piece of furniture someone had delivered to the wrong flat. "Show me the first eight counts. Only the arms. No hips."

I did. My arms went out in a flat, crisp line. Then came the sharp drop, the isolated lift of the chest, the lock of the shoulders into position. My body felt like a set of parts assembled by someone with a manual and no sense of humour.

Seo-yeon watched without moving. Then she said, quite softly, "Better. Now do it the way you'd do it on the bodega."

I did it the way I'd do it on the bodega. The arms swung loose and wide. The hips went back and forth on the downbeat, a slow pop-and-lock, the wrists flicking on the accents, and my weight dropped low into the floor so that every movement had a little give in it, like a car tyre taking a bump. It felt like breathing. It felt like home.

Seo-yeon's expression did not change, but something behind her eyes shifted.

"That," she said, "is why they gave you the scholarship. Not the drops. Not the technique. That." She stepped back into the front of the room. "Now do it again, in formation, and the next time your hips go somewhere they're not supposed to, I will personally send you home on the first plane."

The room exhaled. Somebody near the back laughed nervously. I heard the girl beside me, a tall trainee from Busan named Ha-eun, mutter she's going to break you under her breath, and I did not turn my head.

"Five, six, seven, eight."

The problem was that the formation did not want me to be loose. It wanted each dancer to be a single shape in a larger shape, a pattern of lines that changed every four bars: a diagonal sweep to stage left, a tight wedge, a staggered V that collapsed into a full line on the eighth count. My body had only ever known one kind of geography, the open circle, where you claimed a square of concrete with your feet and dared anyone to take it. Here the floor was a grid, and every square was owned by somebody else.

On the next run I came into the diagonal and my footwork was perfect. My arms were perfect. Then the wedge shifted, and Ha-eun's elbow came up at the wrong height, and my body, which had learned to react to whatever was in front of it, flinched. I dropped my shoulder to avoid her arm. My foot landed a quarter-beat early, on the seven instead of the eight, and the whole line hitched like a train going over a broken rail.

"Stop!"

We stopped. Seo-yeon walked down the line again. This time she didn't look at my face. She looked at my feet.

"You're reacting to the body next to you," she said. "You're dancing with a stranger's elbow instead of the music. In this room the music is the stranger. Everyone else is simply furniture."

"Yes, Director."

"Do you understand what I'm saying?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then tell me why you're still not doing it."

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out. Because I did understand. I understood that the entire training system was built around erasing the things that made me who I was: the cross-body rolls I'd learned from my cousin in Elizabeth, the freestyle hit I'd thrown in at the end of my first audition without asking anyone, the way I stopped mid-phrase when a beat broke wrong and let the silence hang for a whole bar before finding the groove again. Those things were my voice. And here, voices were a liability. The company wanted eleven bodies that moved as one, that could drop into a formation so precisely you couldn't tell where one dancer ended and the next began. Their most successful group had sold forty million albums on the strength of that perfection.

And I was good. Seo-yeon had said so herself. My kinetic range was wider than anyone's in the room. My musicality was instinctive, not trained. I could feel the snare before the drummer played it. That was the reason they had brought me all the way from Newark, and that was the reason they were trying to take it apart.

"Because I don't want to be a stranger's elbow," I said finally.

The silence in the studio was enormous.

Seo-yeon's eyes narrowed. Then, to my surprise, she almost smiled.

"Nobody wants to be an elbow, Kiara," she said. "That is the first thing I learned about this business, and it is the only thing I have never been able to teach." She tapped her knuckles against the mirror, sharp and deliberate. "Here is the decision you have to make, and you have until the end of this run. You can conform. You can learn the lines so well that your body forgets it ever had another shape, and you can be one of the eleven who fly to Tokyo next spring. Or you can keep your hips in Newark, and you can go home, and nobody in this building will ever say your name again."

She turned to the front.

"Five, six, seven, eight."

The music started. The beat dropped, the synth line came in over the bass like glass shattering in slow motion, and eleven bodies moved as one.

I stood in the second row, my heart pounding, my shoulder aching, my weight shifting from foot to foot on the sprung floor. I could feel the old freestyle rising under my ribs, hot and restless, begging to be let out. I could feel the formation pulling at me, the grid of lines and angles, the clean certainty of the next eight counts.

I moved on the seven.

Not on the eight. On the seven, a tiny drop, the knees bending into the snare before it landed, the arms unfurling wide and loose, the hips rolling once, twice, through the centre of the phrase. The line hitched beside me. Ha-eun gasped. Seo-yeon's head whipped round.

For one long, terrible bar, I was alone in the middle of eleven perfect shapes, and I was not sure whether I had just ruined everything or finally found my way back to the beat.


## 26. Court of the Dreaming: Territorial Dream Dispute

A note before the scene: I've changed the premise. Max is a schoolboy, so I've written Mora's intrusion as a nightmare and left out the romantic and sexual framing around him. The comedy is all in the courtroom.

---

The Court of the Dreaming sat in the same hour it always did, which was the hour after the last candle went out and before anybody remembered they had an early start. Its walls were the grey of pencil lead, its benches were carved from something that might once have been driftwood, and its ceiling, if it had one, was lost in a soft, unhurried dark. Dream sat at the high table in his chair, the one shaped like a question nobody had yet been brave enough to ask, and watched the parties arrange themselves with the stillness of a man who had been awake since the first sleeping thing had dreamed of falling.

Matthew the raven shifted on the rail behind him. "Shall we begin?"

"We shall," said Dream. "Before we do, I would like it recorded that I do not enjoy this sort of thing."

"It's recorded," said Matthew. "It's been recorded for about three thousand years. Nobody reads it."

Dream sighed. The sigh moved through the room like a draught, and the nearest lamp flickered, a candle nobody had lit deciding to burn all the same.

On the left stood First Love. It was a small, round-cheeked thing with a shy tilt to its head and a habit of looking at the floor when it spoke, which it did now, very quietly, as though the Court might be listening. It wore a cardigan that had been knitted by someone who loved it, and it held a bunch of wildflowers in both hands, stems wrapped in damp paper.

"It was mine," it said. "The field. The wildflowers. Max lying on his back with the grass tickling his ears, and the girl from the next street coming over the hill, and the way it always ends right before anybody says anything. It took me eleven nights to get the light right. I did the clover myself."

"The clover is very good," said Dream.

First Love brightened. "Thank you."

"Don't be pleased. It's still a dispute."

On the right, Mora sat cross-legged on the bench, looking supremely unbothered. She was tall and narrow and wore a dress the colour of old cabbage, and her hair hung down past her waist in a dark, untidy curtain. Beneath it her face was pale and handsome and rather sharp, and she had the pleasant, unhurried manner of someone who had never once been asked to leave a party. When she smiled, her teeth were very small.

"Hello, Dream," she said. "Good to see you looking so well-rested."

"I have not been resting, Mora."

"No. You never do. It's a shame, really. You'd be so much more fun."

"Explain yourself," said Dream.

Mora spread her hands. "I went for a walk. The dream was there. The field was there, and the boy was lying in the grass, and I thought, what a lovely place for a nightmare. So I did a little tidying up." She looked at First Love with real affection. "I left the clover alone. I'm not a vandal."

"You put a weight on his chest."

"A small weight. He's a very light sleeper. He barely noticed."

"You stopped his breath."

"Only a little. Only long enough to make it interesting." Mora tilted her head. "Look, I'm not going to pretend I'm a nice person. I've been sitting on people's chests since the first village had a fire. But I don't take what isn't mine without a good reason, and I have a good reason."

"Which is?"

"A girl's gotta eat too, you know?"

There was a short silence in the Court. Matthew made a noise in his throat that might have been a cough. First Love looked at the floor and went slightly pinker.

"That is not a legal argument," said Dream.

"It's a practical one," said Mora. "I've been working on the same three hundred children for the last hundred years. They're getting thin. Their dreams are getting thinner, too. Nobody's having the old kind of nightmare anymore. They're having the kind where the bathroom door opens and there's nothing there, which is so tedious for everyone. I have to keep my standards up, Dream. Somebody has to."

"And so you chose a boy's wildflower field."

"I chose a boy's wildflower field because it's the only place in the whole Dreaming where nobody's been frightened for weeks." She shrugged. "It's a very sweet dream. It's almost too sweet. It needed something to set it off."

First Love looked up. "He liked it," it said quietly. "That was the point. He liked feeling like he could be brave in a field, with someone looking at him like that. It was meant to be the kind of thing you hold on to when you're older."

Mora's expression did not change, but something in the room did, and the light dimmed for a moment, as if a cloud had passed across a window that was not there. "And now he's going to remember the field as the place where he couldn't breathe," she said, gently. "Which is also the kind of thing you hold on to, when you're older. I'm not cruel. I'm just specific."

Dream leaned forward. The chair creaked. "Enough," he said. "Matthew, the record."

"Recording."

"Mora. Your defence is noted, and it is, I regret to say, the most honest thing anyone has said in this Court this year. You will return the boy's dream to its original state. You will not touch the clover. You will cease feeding on the child's breath, and you will go and find another dreamer who has the good sense to be terrified in a normal way."

"Terrified in a normal way?" Mora raised an eyebrow. "That's not a sentence. That's a lifestyle."

"It is what I have."

"Fair enough." She stood. Her shadow stood a half-second later, rather slower than she did. "And First Love?"

"Yes?" said the small figure, clutching its flowers.

"You're going to let me come back. Not to the field. Just to the edge of it. I'll be very good, and I'll only come on Thursdays, when the boy's mother makes him have a bath and he's too sleepy to notice me."

First Love opened its mouth to protest, saw the gleam in Mora's eye, and closed it again. "Only Thursdays," it said.

"Only Thursdays," Mora agreed, and bowed, very low, with a flourish that was almost graceful.

Dream watched her go. He waited until the last trace of cabbage-coloured dress had dissolved into the dark, and then he turned to Matthew with an expression that the raven would later describe, to nobody in particular, as faintly wicked.

"Matthew," he said.

"Yes, Lord?"

"Did you know that Mora has been sleeping on the boy's bedroom floor for three nights in a row, disguised as a draught?"

"I did not."

"Then perhaps it is time the boy had a little help with his nightmares." Dream steepled his fingers. "Summon the Court's pest control. Send him the one who lives in the cupboard under the stairs. Tell him the hallway smells of wildflowers."

Matthew blinked. "The cupboard spirit is terrified of cabbage."

"I know," said Dream, and for the first time that night, something almost like a smile moved across his face. "That is precisely why I chose him."

The lamp that had been burning on its own stopped flickering, and went out, and the Court of the Dreaming slipped quietly back into the dark.


## 27. Blood Moon

The inn at Ostrava stank of wet wool and burning tallow, and the wind through the shutters carried the smell of pine and snow down from the mountains. Yennefer had chosen the table in the corner for its distance from the fire and its clear view of the stairs. She drank her wine slowly. It was thin and sour, the kind of wine a traveller bought because it was cheap and dark and nobody would ever ask where it came from.

She did not look up when the door opened, but she felt the cold come in with it, and then the silence that followed, the sudden stillness of a room that has noticed something it cannot name.

He was taller than the men of the town, pale as chalk under the lamp, and dressed in a long crimson coat with a high collar. A wide-brimmed hat sat on his head at an angle that suggested he had never once been embarrassed. His eyes were hidden behind a pair of orange-tinted spectacles, though they could not hide the faint, violent glitter of something that had nothing to do with the light. He smiled at the innkeeper. The innkeeper, a stout man who had been ladling soup a moment before, dropped the ladle and fled into the kitchen.

Yennefer lifted her glass. "You're going to frighten the cook."

"The cook will recover." His voice was soft and amused, with an accent she could not place. He walked to her table without hurrying, his boots silent on the boards, and pulled out the opposite chair with a flourish. "May I?"

"You may do whatever you like. You clearly intend to."

He sat. The coat settled around him like something alive. For a moment he simply studied her, head tilted, and she had the uncomfortable impression that he was tasting her, the way a man might test the edge of a blade with his thumb.

"Lilac," he said at last. "And gooseberries. Underneath the wine. You smell like a garden that has been sealed behind glass for a very long time."

"Is that meant to be a compliment?"

"It is an observation." He leaned back. "You are a sorceress. I can feel it in the air around you, like a storm that hasn't broken. You are also very tired, very angry, and you have been trying for a long time to make yourself look like something you are not."

Her fingers tightened on the stem of her glass. The wine trembled. "And you are a vampire," she said coolly. "I can see the teeth you're not showing me. You're very obvious, you know. Most of the vampires I've met have the decency to be discreet."

"Ah. But you've met vampires." His smile widened. "Then you're not as frightened of me as you should be."

"I'm not frightened of you at all."

"No. You are curious. That's much more dangerous, for both of us." He tapped one long finger on the table, a soft, deliberate sound. "My name is Alucard. I am told I am an ancient monster, and I have never once argued with the description."

"Yennefer of Vengerberg." She did not offer her hand. "And I am told you have been hunting my kind in the north. Men with silver and sharp eyes and very bad manners."

"Your kind?"

"Sorceresses. Witches. Anyone who can make the world bend a little. The villagers have a way of blaming us for things we didn't do."

"Did you do this one?" His gaze drifted to the window, where the moon hung over the mountains, a swollen and sullen red.

"No." She turned her glass slowly on the wood. "But I know who did. And he's been drinking from the wells in Ostrava for a month."

Alucard's head turned back to her, very slowly, like a hawk's.

"Do you," he said softly. "How very interesting."

---

They did not speak for a long time after that. The fire burned down. The innkeeper crept back out of the kitchen and went round the room lighting candles with trembling hands, avoiding the corner table as if it were haunted. Yennefer ordered another cup of wine, and Alucard, who did not drink, watched her with the patient, lethal attention of a cat watching a bird that has not yet realised it is in the wrong garden.

"You are testing me," she said at last.

"Of course I am. It is the only pleasure left to a creature like me." He folded his hands on the table. His fingers were long and very white, the nails sharpened to faint points. "And you are testing me too. You wanted to see whether I would frighten you into leaving. I did not."

"Then we're both disappointed."

"Not yet." He smiled, and this time there was something almost boyish in it, a flash of wicked delight. "Tell me, Yennefer. What would you do with a man who could not die, and who wanted nothing more than to be killed by someone clever enough to deserve it?"

"I'd tell him he was a fool. And then I'd find some way to make him regret it."

"Regret it how?"

She set her glass down with a small, deliberate click. "I'd take his heart out with a spell he didn't see coming, and then I'd pour it into a bottle and sell it to a duke who collects such things. You'd be furious, I'm sure. You'd come after me with your silver and your guns, and I'd be waiting for you."

For a moment Alucard said nothing. Then he laughed, low and delighted, the sound rolling across the room like distant thunder.

"You would do it, wouldn't you?"

"In a heartbeat."

"Splendid." He leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the edge of his spectacles, turning the orange lenses to flame. "Now let me tell you what I would do, and you may decide whether you are flattered or appalled."

He lifted one hand and touched the back of hers, very lightly, on the table. His skin was cold as river stone. Yennefer did not move. She could feel the pulse in her own wrist quickening under his fingers, and she could feel, beneath the coldness, something ancient and hungry stirring in him, patient as a sleeping tide.

"I would let you try," Alucard said quietly. "I would let you hurt me. And then I would stand up, and I would show you exactly how much it did not matter."

"That's not a threat."

"No." His thumb moved across her knuckles, a slow, almost tender stroke. "It is an invitation to a very long game."

She pulled her hand away. Her heart was going too fast, and she hated it. "You're an arrogant, sadistic little parasite."

"Yes," he agreed cheerfully. "And you are the first person in five hundred years who has not been afraid of me. I find I am quite delighted."

Yennefer picked up her wine again and drank, very slowly, until the glass was empty. Then she set it down, stood, and gathered her cloak from the back of the chair.

"Where are you going?" he asked.

"To find the man who's drinking from the wells." She looked down at him, cool and unhurried, though her hands were not quite steady. "You're welcome to come, if you think you can keep up. Or you can sit here and watch the moon. I'm sure it's fascinated by you."

Alucard rose slowly to his full height, and the coat fell around him in a crimson sweep. He smiled, all teeth now, the mask of politeness gone.

"Oh, I think I can keep up," he said. "I've been chasing things through the dark for a very long time. Usually they stop running when they realise I'm enjoying myself."

"I'm not going to stop running."

"No," said Alucard, and something in his voice shifted, softened, became almost reverent. "I don't suppose you are."

He held the door for her, the way a gentleman would, and outside the blood-red moon hung over the mountains, watching them both as they stepped into the snow.


## 28. Prison Strings

The facility did not have a name, only a number stamped in brass above the gate, and the guards at the intake desk called it "the Hollow" in the tone people use for a dentist's waiting room. Jolyne Cujoh had been inside for eleven days. She had already learned that the walls hummed when the wardens were thinking, that the corridors lit up in order from the north wing when someone important was coming, and that nothing in this place was as sweet as the woman who had just walked into the common room.

Makima sits on the far side of the table with her hands folded, the way a schoolteacher sits when she has been told the children are going to be difficult and is looking forward to it. Her auburn hair falls to her shoulders in soft waves. Her yellow eyes, ringed in black, are warm and attentive and far too still. She wears the grey prison-issue cardigan like it was tailored for her.

"I'm so glad you agreed to talk," Makima says. "The guards told me you were very hard to reach."

"The guards are right." Jolyne leans back in her chair until the front legs lift off the floor, arms crossed, and watches the other woman carefully. "I don't know who you are, and I don't like people who smile at me before they introduce themselves."

Makima's smile widens, pleased. "My name is Makima. I'm with the Public Safety Division. I'm here to make sure you're being treated fairly."

"Fairly." Jolyne snorts. "I was framed for murder, lady. I'm not sure fair is on the menu."

"Then let's talk about the murder."

"Let's not."

"Oh, but I'd like to." Makima tilts her head. Her gaze drifts, very briefly, to the back of Jolyne's left hand, where a faint golden shimmer runs along the knuckles like a thread of light. "That's a Stand, isn't it? Stone Free. You can unravel your body into string."

Jolyne's shoulders go tight. The chair drops onto four legs with a sharp crack. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I simply noticed." Makima folds her hands again. "You're very careful not to let the guards see it. And you were very careful not to look at the cameras when you walked in. You know exactly where they are. That's clever."

"You talk too much for a Public Safety officer."

"I've been told so before." Makima's voice is light, amused. "But you're going to need someone who can talk, Jolyne. You're not going to get out of here on your own, and the people who put you in this cell are not going to let you out just because you're innocent."

"Oh, so you're the good guy now."

"I'm the only one who can help you."

"Then help me, Miss Public Safety." Jolyne leans forward, elbows on the table, her voice dropping. "Tell me who framed me."

For a second, Makima's smile does something small and sweet and terrible, like a cat deciding which bird to watch first. "That's the wrong question."

"Is it?"

"Yes. The right question is what you're willing to give up for the answer."

Jolyne has heard that tone before. Men in suits used it in the car park outside the courthouse, the soft, reasonable voice of someone who already knows the price. She feels the hairs rise on her arms. Her fingers twitch, and a single strand of golden string slips out from under her cuff and coils against the table like a sleeping snake. Makima notices, eyes sharpening with delight.

"Oh," she says softly. "Look at that."

"Back off," Jolyne says.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing a lot. You've been sitting there for five minutes telling me what I want to hear, and you haven't even asked about the cell I'm in, or my lawyer, or my dad. Normal people ask about their dad."

Makima's eyes flicker, and Jolyne sees the first real hint of interest, a glint like a blade catching light.

"You're very perceptive," Makima says. "Most people never get past the first minute."

"Most people don't have a Stand."

"That's true." Makima stands. The shadow she casts across the table is longer than it should be, stretching toward Jolyne's chair like a hand. "Would you like to see what I can do?"

"No."

"Shame." Makima steps closer, close enough that Jolyne can smell her perfume, something light and expensive, and beneath it something cold, like metal left in the rain. "Because I've been wanting to see yours for a very long time."

Jolyne's string whips out in a single clean line, faster than thought, looping around Makima's wrist and locking tight. "Then you're going to be disappointed."

Makima does not flinch. Her free hand comes up, almost lazily, and touches the golden thread at her wrist with two fingers, the way someone might test the temperature of bathwater.

"Interesting," she murmurs. "You can feel where I'm holding you."

And Jolyne feels it too. A pressure at her own elbow, cold and precise, as if a second string has been tied to her arm from the outside. She looks down. There is no string there. There is only a faint red mark on her skin, shaped like a loop.

"What did you do?" she demands.

"I made a small promise," Makima says pleasantly. "You're going to tell me what I want to know, Jolyne. And you're going to want to tell me. That's the part that makes it so much easier."

Jolyne's heart is pounding now. She can feel something in her chest tightening, a gentle, insistent warmth that feels almost like affection. It's wrong. It's not hers. She tries to pull away and her body does not quite obey, the way a dog hesitates before a familiar hand.

She's inside my head.

The thought comes sharp and clear, and with it a flash of memory: the way the guard at intake had smiled at her too warmly, the way her cellmate had stopped looking at her, the way the warden had said her name like it belonged to someone else. None of it had been an accident. It had been a pattern, and she had walked right into it.

"You're not getting in," Jolyne says through her teeth. "I don't care how many pretty words you use."

"Oh, Jolyne," says Makima, and her voice is soft with something that might be pity. "Everyone says that. Right up until they start to enjoy it."

Jolyne yanks her string back. It comes free with a snap that echoes off the concrete walls, and she stumbles backward, catching herself on the edge of the table. Makima's wrist has a thin red line where the thread was. She rubs it absently, watching Jolyne with a curious, delighted expression, like a collector who has finally found the rare piece she was looking for.

"You're fighting it," Makima says. "That's very admirable. Your father did that too, you know. In Egypt, I think it was. He stood up to something enormous and refused to kneel."

"Don't talk about my father."

"Why not? He's the reason you're here. Did you know that? He's the one who made the enemy angry enough to frame you."

Jolyne freezes. "What?"

"Think about it." Makima steps back, hands clasped behind her, the picture of innocence. "Who benefits if a woman with a Stand is locked away in a place like this? Who gains if she spends her life rotting in a cell, while the people who really want her power get to move freely?"

The room tilts. Jolyne's mind races back over the courtroom, the framed photographs, the witness who had been so certain, the lawyer who had told her to sign the papers without reading them. The entire case had been built like a cage, each bar fitted to the last, and she had been too busy trusting the people around her to notice the lock.

"You knew," she says hoarsely. "You knew all of it."

"I know a great many things," Makima says gently. "That's why I'm so useful. And why I'm going to be the only person in this building you can trust, if you're smart enough to let me help."

Jolyne looks at her, at the warm smile and the still yellow eyes, and something cold and clear settles in her chest, the way a stone settles at the bottom of a pool.

"No," she says. "You don't get to be my friend."

Makima's smile falters, just for a heartbeat. Then it returns, brighter than before.

"That's a shame," she says. "I was going to make it so easy for you."

The lights in the common room flicker and dim. Somewhere down the corridor a door slams, and a voice calls a name that might be Jolyne's, and in the shadows along the walls, something golden begins, very quietly, to move.


## 29. SCP-7731: "The Ink of Tomorrow"

Item #: SCP-7731
Object Class: Euclid (provisional, pending reclassification)
Special Containment Procedures: SCP-7731 consists of a standard Foundation research notebook, 96 pages, black hardcover, currently stored in a locked filing cabinet in Research Office 4-C at Site-19. Access is restricted to Dr. Helen Marsh and to personnel holding Level 3 clearance or above. All handwritten annotations on the notebook are to be photographed before being removed for analysis. Under no circumstances is SCP-7731 to be taken outside Site-19.

Description: SCP-7731 is a notebook which, on the basis of forensic examination, appears to have been written by Dr. Marsh herself. Its contents consist of entries dated between 14 March and 22 April of the current year. Entries are in Dr. Marsh's handwriting, which has been verified against forty-two sample documents. The notebook was discovered on 2 May in the drawer of Dr. Marsh's desk. Dr. Marsh has no memory of writing the entries.

---

Researcher's Account (Dr. Helen Marsh, Senior Researcher, Site-19)

I found the first note on a Tuesday. It was tucked inside my copy of the containment logs for Wing C, folded once, the paper soft at the crease. I assumed it was a scrap from my own handwriting, a reminder I'd forgotten I'd left. It took me a full minute to realise it was not a reminder at all.

"Do not go into the east corridor on Thursday. Do not let Okonkwo sign the transfer. You will want to. You will want to very badly. Do not."

The handwriting was mine. The little loop on the lowercase g, the way I cross my sevens. I checked it against three documents on my desk and it matched every stroke.

I did not go into the east corridor on Thursday. I did, however, have a long and unpleasant argument with Dr. Okonkwo about the transfer forms, which I had not planned to have. I put the note in my desk drawer and told myself I was tired.

The second note appeared the following week, in the margin of a memo I had read twice.

"They will tell you it's a psychological effect. It isn't. You're going to start doubting your own eyes. Keep writing things down. Keep the notebook in the locked drawer and never, ever tell Marcus."

Marcus is my husband. He is also, as it happens, a bit of a gossip, and I have never once told him anything that mattered.

---

By the third week the notes had stopped being polite. They came in a variety of places: under the spine of a manila folder, in the pocket of my lab coat, once taped to the inside of the coffee machine's lid. The handwriting became looser, the lines slanting downhill. Some of the sentences trailed off mid-thought. Others repeated themselves with the frantic insistence of someone who had been awake for days.

"The breach happens on 19 April at 03:12. Containment for SCP-4488 fails. The doors on Level D open themselves. It is not the thing in the glass. It is the glass itself."

I took that one to Director Halvorsen the next morning, feeling foolish. He read it twice, then looked up at me with an expression I could not read.

"Helen," he said slowly. "Where did you find this?"

"In my coat."

"You wrote it?"

"I think I did. I can't remember."

He was silent for a long moment. Then he said, very quietly, "Dr. Marsh, I'm going to ask you to take a few days' leave. Don't come into the building. I'm going to assign a team to look at the containment schedules and I'd rather you weren't in the middle of it while they do."

I did not argue. I was relieved. I went home and sat in my kitchen and did not turn the lights on until Marcus came back from his shift at the hospital.

---

Foundation Analysis (Excerpted from Incident Report 7731-B)

The following is an excerpt from the review conducted by Dr. Okonkwo and Security Chief Yardley following the discovery of SCP-7731.

Forensic analysis of the notebook and its loose notes indicates that handwriting, paper stock and ink are consistent with Dr. Marsh's personal stationery. Ink analysis, however, reveals a compound that is not commercially available and that does not appear in the Foundation's chemical registry. The compound is inert under normal conditions and fluoresces faintly under ultraviolet light, forming a repeating pattern identical to the coordinates of Containment Cell D-14.

Containment Cell D-14 houses SCP-4488, a fragment of what our records describe as "clear glass with an interior that changes when observed." Its containment protocols have been stable for eleven years.

On 19 April, at 03:12, SCP-4488's sensors recorded a fluctuation consistent with a seal failure. Automated systems corrected the fault within four seconds. Logs of the event were, for approximately six minutes, unavailable. When restored, the logs contained a timestamp for a second, unrecorded fluctuation at 03:12:04, attributed to a clock error.

Dr. Marsh was not present on site at the time of either event. Her badge records show she was at home. Her husband's shift logs at the hospital place him on duty from 22:00 to 07:00.

---

Addendum (Dr. Marsh, interview transcript, 6 May)

Q: Do you remember writing the notes?

A: No. I remember finding them. I remember the handwriting. I remember the way I cross my sevens.

Q: Is it possible you wrote them while under stress, or while sleeping?

A: I suppose it's possible. I don't sleep much.

Q: Dr. Okonkwo's report notes that the ink contains a compound we cannot identify. Do you have any explanation?

A: I've been thinking about that. The ink in the last note is greyer than the others. Like it's been diluted. I think somebody else was writing with my hand, and running out of time.

Q: Who?

A: The glass. Or the person who's been standing on the other side of it, watching us try to read.

Q: Dr. Marsh, I need to be clear. Does any part of you believe these notes are an accurate warning?

A: Yes. And that is the most frightening thing I have ever said in an interview room.

---

Final Note, Recovered 2 May (Unsigned, Handwriting Verified as Dr. Marsh)

"Stop looking for the breach. It already happened. You just haven't reached the page where it's written down.

If you're reading this in the drawer, you have one chance. Don't open the east door. Don't tell Halvorsen the truth. And don't believe the one who says they're trying to help you, because they're the one who taught you the handwriting.

I'm sorry. I'm so sorry about Marcus."

---

Addendum 29-B (Foundation Security): Dr. Marsh was discovered at 04:50 on 9 May in the east corridor of Site-19, seated on the floor outside Containment Cell D-14, holding a black notebook. She was calm and cooperative. She stated that she had come to close the door. When asked which door, she gestured at the empty wall beside the cell and said, "The one I'll open tomorrow." Medical staff report no physical injury. Dr. Marsh's handwriting, as recorded on a clipboard in her hand at the time of her discovery, is identical to that of the notebook. The final entry on the clipboard reads, in full: "Hello, Helen. Thursday."

Recommendation: Reclassify SCP-7731 as Keter pending review. Dr. Marsh is to be placed under observation. Do not allow any personnel to read SCP-7731 alone. Do not allow Dr. Marsh access to pen or paper.


## 30. SCP-8102: "The Archive Beneath the Doors"

Item #: SCP-8102
Object Class: Keter (provisional)
Special Containment Procedures: The doors comprising SCP-8102 are to be sealed with reinforced steel plating and marked with the standard Foundation hazard sign. Each identified door is to be monitored by a remote camera feed. Exploration of the Archive is permitted only under the authority of the Site Director and the Ethics Committee, and only by teams composed of at least one member of the Unusual Records Division. No team is to remain within the Archive for longer than six hours. No documents are to be removed.

Description: SCP-8102 refers to a series of identical, unmarked wooden doors appearing in abandoned buildings across at least forty-one countries. Each door, when opened, leads to an architecturally impossible space the Foundation has designated the Archive. The Archive's interior is organised in corridors that appear to fold back upon themselves, with shelving extending beyond the limits of visible space. Document collections contain materials in over three hundred languages, some of which have not been spoken for several millennia.

## 31. Offworlder

Envoy Sefi Arrand had been told, before the transfer shuttle left orbit, that the colony of Ysolde had three things to be wary of: its winds, its rivers, and its people. The briefing had been thorough about the first two. The third had been summarised in a single paragraph, using the phrase socially divergent populations four times and the word insular twice, and Sefi had read it on the way down with the sensation of a traveller reading a map of a country whose borders had moved in the night.

On the third morning, against the advice of her attaché, she left the Residency without an escort.

The street outside was narrow and steep, paved in a blue-grey stone that caught the light like the inside of a shell. The air was warm and smelled of smoke and cut herbs. Two children ran past her with a kite made of waxed paper, their feet bare and slapping the stones, and neither of them looked up. She stopped to let them go, and found she was smiling.

The market was at the bottom of the hill, in a square ringed by arcades. Stalls under striped awnings sold fish packed in ice and glass jars of something amber and fragrant. A woman with silver-streaked hair bargained over a basket of pale green fruit in a voice that carried across the square like a bell. Sefi listened to the rhythm of the words before she could understand them, and realised that the rhythm was the first thing she recognised.

The briefings had called the Ysolde-born the Rooted, with a faint note of disapproval, as though they had chosen to stay put out of stubbornness. Here, nobody seemed to think of themselves as rooted at all. They simply were. Their faces were broad and long-jawed, their hands large and spatulate, their skin a few shades darker than the Consortium colonists. Some of them, Sefi noted, had a fine layer of silvery down along the forearms, like a fox's winter coat. None of them looked at her with fear. A few looked at her with curiosity. One, an old man selling knives, looked at her with what she could only describe as pity, and then turned back to his whetstone.

She bought a small paper cone of roasted seeds from a girl who could not have been more than eight. The girl counted the change very carefully, and then, without being asked, added an extra seed to the cone.

---

Sefi was in the middle of the square, trying to work out whether she had been cheated or honoured, when a man in grey approached her with the careful stride of someone who had been sent.

"Envoy Arrand." He bowed without quite lowering his eyes. "The Residency has been looking for you."

"I'm aware. I'm also very much enjoying the seeds."

"You are not, I think, permitted to walk the market alone."

"I'm not permitted to do many things. I've been told that a great deal already. Have you been sent to escort me back, or to make sure I know I've been disobedient?"

"Both, I'm afraid," said the man in grey. He had a long, patient face and an accent that was almost, but not quite, Consortium-born. "Command is displeased. They're requesting you attend the Minister at the Hall of Records this afternoon."

"Ah." She tipped the seeds into her palm. "The Minister of Science."

"Yes."

"Who has not, I'm told, performed a scientific act in eleven years."

The man in grey did not smile. "The Minister is a very capable administrator."

"I'm sure he is. Is that the phrase they use for a political appointment?"

---

The Hall of Records stood at the top of the hill, a squat, white building with narrow windows and a roof of tarnished copper. The Minister's office occupied the upper floor, and it had been furnished with a care that Sefi found slightly insulting: rugs from the inner provinces, a desk of dark wood carved with river-leaves, and a window looking north over the whole of the city, so that anyone standing in front of the Minister would have the entire colony spread out behind them like a debt.

Minister Corvan Tsale was a man in his late fifties, lean and sunburnt, with silver hair cut close to the skull and a pair of pale grey eyes that had clearly been sizing up people for most of his life. He did not stand when she entered. He gestured at a chair.

"Envoy Arrand. You have a very interesting habit of disappearing."

"I've been told that I'm too curious."

"You've been told correctly." He poured two cups of tea from a copper pot. The smell was sharp and green. "Sit. Drink. We have a great deal to discuss, and I'd like you to have something in your hands while I explain why you are wrong."

She sat. She took the cup. She did not drink. "Which part am I wrong about?"

"The part where you believe what you saw in the market means anything." He leaned back, fingers laced over his stomach. "You think the Rooted are a people who have been left behind. You think they're poor, and unhappy, and hungry for the Consortium's help. You went down the hill this morning and you saw a few children flying kites, and you decided the briefings had exaggerated. They do that, you know. The briefings. They are written by people who have never had to put their feet in the mud."

"And you have?"

"I was born in the mud, Envoy. My mother sold fish in that square. I know exactly how far the Consortium's kindness goes, and I know exactly how many of its promises get paid in full."

Sefi set down the cup. "Then perhaps you should tell me what the Consortium is actually afraid of. Because it isn't the Rooted, is it? Not really. The Rooted have been here for three generations and haven't built a single weapon."

For the first time, Tsale's expression shifted. It was very small, a flicker at the corner of his mouth, the kind of movement a man makes when a card he had expected to hold is turned face up on the table by someone else.

"No," he said. "It is not the Rooted. It is what they might become, if they were given the right reasons to stop being patient."

"And what reason would that be?"

"That the Consortium has decided their children are no longer worth the trouble of being sent to school on Earth." He lifted his own cup and drank, unhurried. "You are a diplomat, Envoy. You know how these things are settled. A treaty, a ceremony, a little grace from the stronger party. And the Rooted would be grateful, and the Consortium would be grateful, and the question of who gets to decide what a human being looks like would quietly disappear from every report that crosses your desk."

Sefi felt the cold settle low in her stomach. She thought of the old man with the knives, his pity, his turning back to the whetstone. She thought of the girl with the seeds, adding the extra one without being asked.

"You're saying the Consortium intends to make them an underclass," she said.

"I'm saying the Consortium has been very generous about the terms of the next treaty. And that the Minister of Science has been asked to sign a report confirming that the Rooted are a biological hazard to the colony's stability." Tsale's gaze was steady. "I am not going to sign it. And I am not going to be the one who refuses in a way that gets me hanged. I need someone from outside to see the report before it reaches Command. Someone who will not be able to pretend she hasn't read it."

"Why me?"

"Because you went down the hill without an escort." He smiled, very thinly now. "And because you bought the seeds."

---

Outside the window, the light had begun to turn copper over the city, and the rooftops were burning gold along their ridges. Somewhere far below, in the square, a bell rang for the change of the hour.

Sefi rose, slowly, and set the untouched tea on the desk between them.

"You're taking a very great risk," she said.

"Yes." Tsale did not get up. "The question you should be asking is whether you have the stomach to take one too."

She looked at him for a long moment, at the lean, sunburnt face and the pale eyes that had seen the mud and survived it, and at the carved river-leaves on the dark wood between them, beautiful and deliberate and meant to impress.

"Show me the report," said Envoy Sefi Arrand. "And then tell me how many of your own people have been sent to the Hall this year to sign things they didn't want to sign."

Tsale's smile went out like a lamp. He reached into the drawer of his desk and drew out a single sealed folder, marked with the Consortium's seal, and laid it on the wood between them without a word.


## 32. Polymerase Chain Reaction

The lab was three rooms in the back of a dead hardware store on Carver Street, and Ines Okafor had spent four months making it breathe. The first thing you learned in the Cinder was that nothing came new. Every centrifuge had been cannibalised from a dental surgery or a dairy. The thermocycler was a Frankenstein of a laser printer's heating element and a car battery charger, and it wheezed every time the settlement's diesel generator coughed. Ines loved it the way you love a dog that bites.

She stood at the bench in a sweat-stained shirt with her hair tied up in a bandana and a pair of safety glasses fogged from the heat. On the bench in front of her sat a row of plastic tubes, each one filled with a faintly blue liquid, each one labelled in her cramped, looping hand. CHK-F4. CHK-F5. Then, at the end of the row, a single tube with a strip of red tape: BRIDGE.

The red tape meant it was the one that mattered.

Outside, the afternoon wind pushed grit against the shutters. Somewhere across the settlement a goat was complaining. Ines pulled her gloves on, tight, and looked at the tube for a long moment before she picked it up.

---

The problem was in the code. It had always been in the code. The Brayhollow Corporation had sold the original seed stock to every agricultural cooperative in the Midwest twenty-two years ago, and every seed in every packet had been engineered with a single built-in instruction: after one generation, the trait stopped. The wheat would germinate, grow tall and golden, and then produce a crop that looked perfect and carried nothing forward. The second generation was sterile in every way that mattered. Corporate customers paid for a new packet every spring. It was a beautiful piece of design, and it had killed three civilisations in the time it took the grain silos to empty.

Nobody had made the sterility reversible. The Brayhollow lawyers had made sure of that. The patents ran to four hundred pages, and every one of them promised that the lock could not be picked.

Ines had spent eleven months picking it.

She had found the fault line in a sample of Brayhollow's own documentation, a footnote about a deprecated promoter sequence that had been patched out of the production line in year six. The sequence still existed, in the tatty old backup drives scavenged from the regional extension office. It was degraded, fragmented, riddled with transcription errors. It was also the only key she had.

She ran her finger down the screen of the laptop, reading the gel results again, though she knew them already. Three weeks of work. Eleven failed clones, two that had survived for a day before the cell walls collapsed, one that had lived long enough to be photographed and then died in the night, its colonies shrivelled to brown dust. And now this. The fourteenth attempt.

The tube in her hand contained the bridge: a short length of synthetic DNA that stitched the old promoter to a fresh wheat cassette. It was the most dangerous thing she had ever made, and she had made it out of rubbish.

---

"You're doing it again," said Teo from the doorway. He was twelve, skinny as a rake, and had been running messages for the settlement since before his voice broke. He carried a plastic bucket full of water and a stack of mouldy paperbacks he had traded for a jar of honey. "The thing where you hold your breath."

"I'm not holding my breath."

"You're holding it. I can see your shoulders."

Ines let it out. "Thanks, Teo. Put the water on the shelf."

"Is it going to work?"

"Probably not."

"Then why are you doing it?"

She looked at him over the rim of her glasses. He was so earnest, so thin, the bones of his wrists showing like the bones of a bird. In the spring he had been sick for three weeks with a bloody cough. The settlement's doctor had said the lungs were weak from the long winter, and Ines had known, without saying anything, that the cause was the bread. The bread was made from Brayhollow seed. It had been grown and harvested and milled, and it had given everyone in Cinder a stomach full of something that tasted of flour and hunger and nothing else.

"Because the next crop is going to fail," she said. "Because the silo's half empty, and the last packet of seed we bought from the Ridge traders was the sterile kind, same as the rest. And because if I don't try, Teo, someone else will try something dumber, and that's worse."

Teo nodded slowly, as if this were a reasonable answer to an unreasonable question. He set the bucket down on the shelf and looked at the tube in her hand.

"Is it the red one?"

"It's the red one."

"Does it hurt?"

"Not yet," said Ines. "That's the trouble with it."

---

The contract had arrived at dusk, carried by a boy from the river camps in a sealed envelope with no return address. Ines had read it twice in the dim light of the generator shed, then folded it into the pocket of her jacket and walked straight to the bench without eating. It was written in careful, cultured English. It offered her five hundred litres of clean diesel, a working solar array and a sealed dormitory in the city of Port Helio, for a single cassette of wheat. Non-sterile. Viable for a minimum of five generations. The buyer's name was withheld. The signature was a stylised black drop of ink that Ines did not recognise.

Five generations. That was the number that kept snagging in her head like a fishhook. Five generations of wheat that could be planted, harvested, saved and planted again, without a corporation's permission, without a spring trip to the Ridge, without an empty silo and a child coughing blood into a rag. It was the thing she had been trying to make since the day she had first held a scrap of Brayhollow's documentation in her hands.

It was also the thing that Brayhollow would kill a city to stop.

She did not know who the buyer was. She suspected she would find out soon enough.

---

The bridge went into the fourteenth clone at half past eleven, under a single hot lamp, with the thermocycler wheezing like an old man on the stairs. Ines watched the display count down the cycles. Denature. Anneal. Extend. Her mouth was dry. She could hear the generator outside and the goat, quieter now, and the wind coming through the broken pane above the sink.

At the end of the run, the lab was silent.

She transferred the product to a fresh plate and held it up to the lamp. A faint, even pink fluorescence bloomed across the bottom of the well, steady and clean. Not the brown ash of the failed clones. Not the flickering half-life of the one that had lived for a day.

Something alive.

She sat down heavily on the stool. Her hands were shaking. She did not know whether she wanted to laugh or be sick. After a moment she did both, a choking, undignified sound that echoed off the tiled walls, and when she looked up Teo was standing in the doorway again with his bucket of water sloshing at his feet.

"It worked," he said, in a voice that barely dared to be hopeful.

"It worked for twelve hours," said Ines. "That's what I'm going to find out tomorrow. Whether the wheat holds long enough to be a crop, or whether it dies in the bread."

"And the buyer?"

Ines looked at the plate, pink and humming under the lamp, and thought of the black drop of ink, and the five generations, and the dormitory in Port Helio with its solar panels turning slowly in a city that had never once gone hungry.

"The buyer," she said quietly, "is going to get exactly what they paid for. And so is everyone else. That's the bit that frightens me."

She picked up the plate and carried it, very carefully, to the cold store at the back of the lab, and set it on the shelf, and closed the door.

Behind her, the thermocycler clicked off with a small, satisfied sigh, the way a machine does when it has finished a job it was never meant to do.
