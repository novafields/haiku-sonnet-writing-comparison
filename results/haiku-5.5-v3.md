## 01. The Azra Gambit

The transfer shuttle smelled of ozone and somebody's expensive cologne. Arthur Neegan sat in the rear cabin with his hands on his knees, the way his mother had taught him to sit in church, and watched Mars shrink to a rust-coloured coin in the port.

"The seats are uncomfortable," said the man opposite. He was narrow, grey at the temples, and his tunic bore the silver lattice of the Antares Diplomatic Corps. "They're built for people who expect to sit in them a long time."

"I'd like to know where I'm going."

"Ceres first. A longer journey after that, if you agree to it." The man smiled without moving his lips much. "My name is Sayle. I am a clerk, Mr. Neegan. I want that understood before we start, because you've spent six hours being frightened by men with rifles, and I regret it. They are soldiers. I am not. The difference matters to me."

Arthur looked at the insignia again. _Diplomatic in name only,_ Hanna at the assay office had muttered, then gone pale and refused to say more.

"Your soldiers took my door off its hinges."

"Your door was opened by a locksmith I have since paid a generous fee to apologise to. Nobody was hurt, and the men are not my soldiers in any sense you'd find useful." Sayle folded his hands. "Your plot on Pavonis Ridge is the most interesting ground in the system this year. The Azra vein under your cistern runs four metres wide at the shoulder and, we estimate, nine hundred metres deep. Every interested party has your assay numbers. Half of them have your address."

That was what had frightened Arthur most. Not the rifles. The address. He had told nobody where he lived except the Company, and the Company had sold him the lease for eleven thousand credits and a handshake.

"What does Azra do, Mr. Sayle? Tell me something the Company didn't."

Sayle looked pleased, the way a schoolmaster looks when a pupil finally asks the right question. "The Company says it accelerates decay. True. Set a sliver of it against a radioactive core and the half-life collapses from millennia to days. That is why the drives work. It is why the warheads are unfriendly. What the Company does not say is that the effect is not symmetrical. The metal does not merely hurry the decay along. It takes the energy and sends it somewhere." He paused. "Somewhere we have not located."

"Somewhere."

"Think of it this way. The Gate network was built on a metal that is scarce in the Belt and absent on Earth. Small picture: you are a man who wanted a vegetable garden. Large picture: a civilisation stretching from Ceres to four stars can now be fuelled, or stopped, by one vein under a cistern in Tharsis. Those are not separate facts, Mr. Neegan. They are one fact, seen from two distances."

Arthur thought of Marta's tomatoes in the glass house, the plants that had outlived her by three years and that he still watered on Sundays, out of stubbornness more than hope.

"And the offer?"

"We buy your lease. Two hundred thousand. Your name remains on the registry as discoverer, which costs us nothing and therefore counts as a kindness. You receive passage to Antares, a cottage in the hills outside Korriban, and a pension larger than your ambitions." Sayle leaned forward. "There is a corollary. Mr. Halvorsen of the Lunar Syndicate would also like your lease. He is less patient than I am. His last agent is no longer with us."

"Dead?"

"Cave-in. Shaft three, Hellas Basin. Sixteen men went down that morning and fifteen came up, and the fifteen were very sorry about the sixteenth. The shaft had been dry for a decade." Sayle spread his fingers. "I do not say the Company caused it. I say the Company has not been curious about it, and that is a failure of imagination I think you are better suited to correct than I am."

The shuttle shuddered. The cabin lights dimmed as the ship swung toward a crescent of white light: the transfer hub, the spinning ring, and beyond it the lattice of hulls waiting in the dark.

"You haven't told me who you work for."

"No, I haven't." Sayle stood and smoothed his tunic. "The principal would like to meet you before you decide, so you can see the faces of the people you're about to bet your life against. Come along. The Ambassador is waiting."

* * *

The room in the hub was grey and quiet and had no windows. A woman sat behind a plain desk, her hair cropped close, a pair of reading glasses pushed up into it. She did not stand.

"Mr. Neegan. Sit, please. You are not under arrest, which I want to say plainly, because I suspect the men at your door made it sound otherwise."

"They made it sound like a choice."

"It was a choice. The one they gave you was poor. I apologise for that." She took off her glasses. "Here is what I can offer that Mr. Halvorsen cannot. I can offer to keep the vein in human hands for another generation. Halvorsen will drain it in ten years and sell the drives to whoever pays most. I would rather see the Azra used slowly, by people who understand it."

"And what do you understand that the Company doesn't?"

She looked at him for a long moment. "That it is not ours. Not the Company's, not Antares', not yours. It has been on this planet for a very long time, Mr. Neegan. Somebody put it there. We are only the latest people to dig."

Arthur felt the cold settle in his chest, not fear exactly, but the sensation of a door opening onto a room much larger than he had expected.

"Two hundred thousand, you said."

"Two hundred thousand and a seat. Or no seat, and a longer conversation." She smiled for the first time. "Take the night. The Company's lease is not the only thing that expires in the morning."

## 02. Darkroom Revelations

The red bulb hummed like a fridge, and everything in the room had gone the colour of a bruise. Elliot had his back to me, bent over the developing tray, rocking it with the care of someone holding a sleeping animal.

"Don't breathe on it," he said.

"I'm not breathing on anything. I'm standing six feet away."

"You're breathing loudly. It's a very sensitive process."

I leaned against the sink and crossed my arms. Our prints were clipped to the drying line behind us: forty-odd frames from the abandoned car park on Rennick Street, shot at dusk, all of them blurred at the edges. Elliot had done the blurring on purpose. I'd been furious about it on Tuesday. On Thursday I'd started to see what he was doing.

"Okay," he said, lifting the paper with the tongs. An image rose out of the chemical, slowly, the concrete coming up grey and then white, a single figure standing in a doorway. It was me. I'd forgotten he'd taken that one.

"You didn't tell me you'd shot me."

"You were looking at the light. You never look at the light when people are pointing cameras at you. You look at the camera."

"That's a terrible thing to say."

"It's true, though." He slid the print into the stop bath and didn't look up. "I sort of like it when you don't notice me."

I should have made a joke. That was my job, usually. Instead I watched his hands, the ink-stained knuckles, the silver ring he'd started wearing on the middle finger of his right hand, and felt something shift that I didn't have a word for yet.

"Elliot."

"Yeah."

"You said on Monday you'd been thinking about things. You don't have to tell me the rest tonight."

He set the tongs down very precisely, the way you'd set down something that might break. "I've been thinking that I don't know what I'm supposed to look like. Not in a dramatic way. I just keep catching myself in shop windows and not recognising the person in them. The jaw's wrong, or the hair. Or I look like I'm wearing someone else's clothes." He laughed, a short, nervous sound. "Sorry. That's a lot to say under a red light."

"It's the right light for it."

"Is it?"

"Confessionals are always a bit red. Ask any priest."

He smiled, finally, and it changed his whole face. I'd been trying all semester to work out what he was, and I'd assumed I'd know when I got there. I'd assumed I'd be the one leading. I had a list in my head of the things I knew about myself: I liked men who were sure of themselves. I liked to be the one who decided when we went home. I'd never thought about what I'd do if the person in front of me was sure of nothing except that he wanted to be honest about it.

"Dani?"

"Sorry. Thinking."

"You do that a lot." He picked up the tongs again. "Can I tell you something else?"

"Go on."

"I'm scared of you a bit. Not in a bad way. You're so decisive. I've never met anyone who's so certain about where they're standing."

I looked down at my boots. I was standing in a puddle of developer I hadn't noticed, the hem of my jeans going dark. "I'm not certain about anything at the moment," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said all week.

He took a step closer, carefully, as though the floor might be wet with more than chemicals. The bulb hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a tap ran and stopped.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You can ask."

"Do you want me to keep going? Talking, I mean. Or should I stop?"

It was the first time anyone had asked me what I wanted without already knowing the answer. I didn't have one ready. I stood in the red light and said, "Keep going," and he did.

## 03. Stolen Moments in Roma

Alessandra came round the corner of Via della Stamperia at forty kilometres an hour, which was too fast for the street and not fast enough to be interesting, and the front wheel of her Vespa missed a tourist's ankle by the width of a bread roll.

"Attenzione!"

The tourist, a tall Englishman with a guidebook open against his chest like a shield, took a step back into a fountain. Not the Trevi. A smaller one, sulking in a corner, but he went in up to the shin anyway.

She killed the engine and pushed up her visor. "You're in the water."

"I'm aware of the water," said Ben, with enormous dignity. "I'm terribly sorry. I wasn't looking. I was reading about the Trevi, and it says the coins are collected for a charity, and I was trying to work out how much, and—"

"Ti sei fatto male?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Are you hurt, _idiota_?"

"Oh. No. No, not at all. I'm perfectly well. Thank you for asking. I'm so sorry about your bike."

"My bike is fine. The bike is an animal, it has survived worse." She climbed off, pulled a damp cigarette from behind her ear and looked at it sadly. "You are British."

"Yes. Is it that obvious?"

"You apologised to a motorbike." She offered him her hand, which he took too late, after he'd worked out that she wanted him out of the fountain. "Alessandra. I deliver things. Mostly lunch. Sometimes lawyers."

"Ben. I'm on sabbatical. I'm writing a book about the water systems of the Renaissance."

"Ah. So you come to Rome to look at water, and you walk into it."

"That's rather unfair."

"It's exactly fair." She grinned, and the grin was a little wicked, the kind that promised trouble to someone else. "Come. You need a coffee and a new pair of socks. The coffee is on me. The socks are a question of morality."

He followed her, of course. That was the first thing she'd noticed about him, the way he came after people as though he'd been raised to believe that wet shoes were a moral failing. In the bar on Via dei Cesarini, she ordered two espressos and a _cornetto_ in rapid Roman, the barman laughed at something, and Ben stood by the counter trying to say _grazie_ in a voice that made the barman laugh harder.

"Your accent," Alessandra said, "is like a cat being asked to sing."

"I've been practising from a phrasebook."

"The phrasebook is a liar. Nobody says _grazie_ like that. Say it like you mean it."

"Grazie."

"Better. Now say it like you're angry."

"I'm not angry."

"Good. Then you can't do it." She slid him the coffee. "Drink. We're going somewhere."

* * *

They went to a wine shop in Trastevere where the owner was an old friend of Alessandra's and did not charge her for the bottle she took, which Ben noticed and tried not to. They went to the Janiculum, where she made him climb the last stretch of steps two at a time and then stood at the top with her arms out, shouting the names of every saint she could think of at the city below. They went to the rooftop of a building near Piazza Navona, which she entered by a fire door that she assured him was unlocked, and which was not.

"You're not supposed to be up here," Ben said, when the lock gave way with a sharp snap of a hairpin.

"Nobody is supposed to be anywhere in Rome."

On the roof, she showed him a tray of old keys she had pocketed from the caretaker's office three weeks earlier. She showed him a silver spoon from a restaurant on Via Giulia. She showed him a small brass saint, the sort sold at the church doors, which she had taken from a chapel in Monti without anyone looking.

"Why do you do it?"

She shrugged. "Because I can. Because people are so careful. Like they think they're being watched by God. Nobody is watching. So why be careful?"

"That's rather a bleak way to live."

"It's a _free_ way to live." She turned the saint over in her fingers. "You don't know anything about me. You think you do. You have your little book. But I could be the worst person in Rome."

"Are you?"

"Maybe." She tossed the saint into her bag, then took it out again and handed it to him. "Here. Hold this. If anyone asks, you bought it."

He held it. It was warm from her hand. He could not decide whether to be appalled or delighted, and for the first time since landing at Fiumicino, he didn't feel the need to know which.

* * *

At half past seven, with the light going orange over the domes, they stood on the Ponte Sisto while the river below turned to hammered gold. Alessandra was leaning on the parapet, her chin on her arms, watching a boy in a rowing boat drift past.

"You'll go back to England," she said.

"In six weeks. Yes."

"And the book?"

"I'll finish it. I suppose."

"And the water?"

He laughed. "I think the water has been a bit wonderful, actually."

"Good." She didn't look round. "I'll forget you by Thursday."

"I doubt that."

"You doubt everything. It's your English disease."

"Alessandra."

She turned then. Her face was unreadable in the dusk, all its usual mischief gone very still. He wanted to say something clever, the sort of thing a man in a novel would say. Instead he said, "Thank you for the saint."

"Keep it."

"I can't."

"Then give it back to the church on the way home. Tell the priest a Roman girl sent you." She pushed herself off the parapet. "And Ben. Next time you walk into a fountain, look up first."

She swung onto the Vespa and was gone before he could answer, a flash of red helmet down the Lungotevere. He stood on the bridge with the small brass saint in his palm, watching the water, and found that his heart was going at a rate he could not account for in the scholarly literature.

* * *

## 04. The Last Outpost

The guy's been sitting at the end of my bar for six minutes and he hasn't touched the glass. That's the first thing I notice about him. The second is his hair, which is a set of tentacled dreads, some of them snapped off at different lengths, and which have clearly been hanging askew for longer than he has been drinking.

"Vega whisky, neat," he says. "Better make that a double."

I pour it. I don't make comments. You learn that behind a bar at the edge of the Andromeda rim, where half the patrons are sleeping off a debt and the other half are about to start one. Except for Gallo, who's been telling everyone since breakfast that the Dorsey shipment is going to arrive on time, and who's now leaning over his table to watch me like I've just grown a second head.

"You're not from the belt," I say.

"No." He knocks back the double and winces. "I'm from the Koronis station. Or I was. There's a thing I'm supposed to be doing, and I'm not doing it, and everyone keeps telling me that's my problem."

"Is it?"

"Probably." He turns the empty glass in his hand. "Name's Oren. Oren Fault."

"Nets."

"Nets. Like the fishing?"

"Like the nickname. Don't ask about the rest."

"I wasn't going to." He slides the glass across. "Could I have another?"

I look at him for a long moment. Then I pour. "Two credits. Pay now."

He pays with a card that's warm from being clutched, and I notice the bank crest on it is one of the big Ceres houses. Expensive. Then I notice the chip's been scratched, and the name on it doesn't match his face.

"You're not Oren Fault," I say quietly.

He doesn't flinch. He takes a long, slow swallow. "No."

"Who are you?"

"Someone who owes money to someone who doesn't like to be owed." He looks at me properly for the first time. His eyes are a pale washed grey, bloodshot at the rims. "I need a bed for the night and a way off this rock in the next forty hours. I was told this place doesn't ask questions."

"Who told you that?"

"A man called Dorsey."

I put the cloth down on the bar, very slowly. Across the room, Gallo has stopped talking. "Dorsey doesn't send people to me," I say.

"He said you'd say that." Oren gives a thin, tired smile. "He also said you owe him."

"I don't owe Dorsey anything."

"Four years ago, the Pell job. You remember that. You walked out with the transponder codes and gave him the other half of the cargo, and the Ceres consortium sent a bounty on a woman named Sarai Vell. They never got her." He tilts his head toward the card. "That's the name on this card. Sarai Vell. It was given to me by the consortium. They think I'm her."

The bar has gone quiet in a way it never does. Somebody at the back drops a glass. The noise makes everyone jump, and they all start talking at once, and I don't hear a word of it, because I'm staring at a man who has just dropped my old name on the counter like a live coin.

I don't tell him I've never been Sarai Vell. I don't tell him that she was my sister, or that she's been dead for three years, or that I buried her under a fake name on a station that doesn't exist anymore. That's a whole other story, and I'm not telling it to a stranger with tangled hair and borrowed credentials.

"Gallo," I call out, not loudly.

Gallo stands up, slowly. Two of his men stand with him.

"Oren," I say, and I look at the stranger, and I don't let him see what I'm feeling. "You're going to finish that drink. Then you're going to tell me who gave you that card, and why the consortium's bounty has a photo of my face on it."

"I can't tell you that."

"Then you'll tell me something else," I say. "Because if I'm holding the wrong end of this thing, I need to know before Gallo decides he's holding the right one."

## 05. Gone South

The thing nobody tells you about New Orleans is that it smells like a wet dog and a bakery had a fight, and the bakery lost but kept talking.

I'm standing on Decatur Street with my backpack digging into my shoulder, watching the bus from Memphis pull away with the back of my head still in the window. Hitched a ride with a guy named Ray, who had a Bible on the dashboard and a laugh like someone shaking a bag of bolts. He dropped me in the Quarter at six this morning, and said, "Don't do anything stupid, kid," and I said, "I'm already doing it, sir," which he did not think was funny.

Here's the thing. My name is Taylor. Not the one on the birth certificate. The one on the inside of my wrist, which I wrote in Sharpie in the bathroom at the Dollar General last night, because I was shaking so hard I couldn't hold a pen steady enough to write it on paper. I'm sixteen. I'm two states from Tennessee. And Mom is probably standing in our kitchen right now, listening to the counsellor's voice on the phone telling her I have "concerning behavioural patterns," and I can't even be mad at her for believing him, because I used to believe him too.

Two days ago I told them. I said I was a boy. I said it at the dinner table, over the green beans, and Dad put his fork down so carefully I thought he was going to cry. Then he didn't. Then he said things I'm not going to write down. I have them memorised, though. I have them all.

So I left. Simple as that. Thirty minutes from the decision to the dirt road, and a sack of bread and cheese and two hundred dollars from the coffee can in the pantry. I'm not proud of the coffee can. I'm sure I'll be proud of other things eventually.

The street is loud. There's a brass band somewhere behind me, playing something slow and mournful, and a guy in a gold suit is spray-painting his own name on the bricks of a closed-up pharmacy. Everybody's talking. Nobody's looking at me. That's the best thing I've felt in two days.

* * *

I've been sitting on the steps of St. Louis Cathedral for an hour when a girl sits down next to me. She's maybe eighteen. Black hair scraped back, a torn denim jacket with the sleeves cut off, a rosary tangled round one wrist like a bracelet.

"You're new," she says.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're clutching your backpack like it's going to run away." She nods at my bag. "You got a place to sleep?"

"Not yet."

"Figured." She pulls a cigarette out of a pack and offers me one. I shake my head. She shrugs. "I'm Rory. Well, that's what I go by. You can call me whatever you like if you're polite about it."

"Taylor."

"Taylor what?"

"Just Taylor."

She grins. "Oh, we're going to get along, Just Taylor. Everybody here's got a name they're not using."

I want to ask her what hers is. I want to ask why her eyes keep flicking to the corner by the bakery, where a man in a wide hat is leaning against the lamppost, watching us both. I don't ask. I've learned that much from the last two days. You don't ask the questions where the answers cost you something.

"Who's he?" I say instead.

Rory doesn't turn her head. "That's Dwayne. He runs the place by the river where the kids sleep. He's fine. He gives you a bed if you're quiet, and he doesn't ask where your parents are." She blows smoke at the sky. "Except he does ask. He asks everyone something. That's the price."

"What's the price?"

"You'll find out." She stubs out the cigarette. "Or you can go to the shelter on Chartres. They'll ask you for your birth certificate, and your parents' phone number, and then they'll call somebody. Choose your poison, Just Taylor."

The brass band has stopped. In the sudden quiet, I hear the sound of my own pulse.

"What if I don't want anyone to call anybody?" I say.

Rory looks at me properly then, the first time since she sat down. Her face does something complicated, a flicker of recognition, and then it's gone again.

"Then you're going to have to be very good at lying," she says. "Which you clearly aren't, because you told me your name."

"It's not my name."

"I know." She stands and holds out her hand. "It's the one you got to keep, though. For now. Come on. I'll show you where the good pancakes are, and you can tell me everything you're not telling me over the syrup."

I take her hand. It's warm and rough and it doesn't let go too quickly. Behind us, the man in the wide hat takes off his hat and holds it against his chest, and I think, very clearly, _I've made a mistake, and I'm going to keep making it._

But I follow her anyway.

## 06. My Apocalypse Buddy

The plan, as Midas explained it to Chewy, was simple. They would walk to the old Tesco on the ridge, the one with the walk-in freezer, and bring back a tin of something that wasn't beans.

"We've had beans for nine days," said Midas. "My bowels have started filing complaints."

Chewy did not reply. Chewy was a stuffed koala about the size of a toddler, with one glass eye, one replacement eye made from a button, and an audio chip that had been installed sometime before the Collapse by a company that no longer existed. When you pressed the paw, he said things like _"I love you more than the sun, Mr. Bubbles,"_ which was the last thing any of them had ever been able to make sense of.

"You're not going to say anything useful, are you?" Midas asked.

Chewy's paw twitched. A faint whirr. Then: _"Hug me, I'm lonely."_

"That's not helpful either."

They set off across the car park at dawn, which was when the wrecked cars were least likely to have bees living in them. Midas carried the rucksack, a crossbow with one working string, and a copy of _The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy_ from which he had torn out every page that did not contain instructions for making gin. Chewy rode in the front pouch of Midas's coat, sulking.

The Tesco was four miles away. It had been four miles away for the last six years, although Midas had been fairly sure at the time that it would take a good deal longer than that to reach.

The first disaster happened at the roundabout.

It was not a large disaster by the standards of the age. A dog came out of a hedge, saw Chewy's bright white fur sticking out of the coat, and decided that it had found the single greatest thing in the history of the world. It was the size of a horse and very keen on meat. Midas ran. Chewy yelled out _"I'm not a toy, I'm a MAN!"_ in a surprisingly deep baritone that the dog found deeply confusing, which gave Midas the twenty seconds he needed to climb onto the top of a bus shelter.

From the top of the shelter, he watched the dog sit down in the road, put its head on one side, and bark at the koala in a tone of enormous reproach.

"Right," said Midas. "We're going to be alright, Chewy."

_"Hug me,"_ said Chewy.

They reached the Tesco at noon. The doors had been smashed in long ago, and the aisles were full of things that had once been food and were now mostly a sort of coloured mould. In the back of the store, a steel door led to the freezer, which had a sign on it that said STAFF ONLY and below that, in marker pen, _Don't open. There's something in the freezer, Dave says._

"Dave's probably dead," Midas said.

"Dave was a lovely man," Chewy said, with a sudden clarity that made Midas drop the crossbow.

He opened the door.

Inside, there were no tins at all. There were four hundred frozen sausages, a bag of unidentified cubes that looked like cheese, and a man in a Tesco fleece, sitting upright against the back wall, with his eyes open and his hands folded neatly on his lap.

"Ah," said the man. "You'll be wanting the tinned peaches, then."

"You're alive?" said Midas.

"More or less. Dave's the one who found the freezer. I've just been keeping the sausages company." He looked at Chewy with interest. "Is that a koala?"

"It's Chewy."

"Charmed. Does it speak?"

_"Hug me, I'm lonely,"_ said Chewy.

"Oh, that's lovely," said the man, and began to cry.

It took them two hours to get the man back to the car park, because he insisted on bringing the sausages, and then another hour to persuade him that the sausages could not come with them, and then a further hour to discover that the four rucksacks they'd left behind had been eaten by the dog, which had followed them the entire way.

They limped back to the house at dusk. Midas had a cut above one eye, a bruised hip and the peaches, which were in a tin with a label that had come off, so nobody would ever know whether they were peaches or pears or possibly a sort of soup.

"Next time," he said, dropping onto the sofa, "we send the koala."

_"I love you more than the sun, Mr. Bubbles,"_ said Chewy, and the sun, which had been hiding behind the wreckage of the old power station all afternoon, chose that moment to set in a spectacular spray of orange and purple.

Midas looked at the koala for a long moment.

"Fine," he said. "You win this round."

## 07. Love in the Limelight

"You're blocking the Welsh poetry."

"Am I?" Julian Vane turned from the door, hat low, sunglasses pushed up, the grin already in place. "Then I'm in the right place. Nobody reads poetry in Wales, so it's the only section nobody will recognise me in."

"Everybody reads poetry in Wales. We just do it loudly, in the pub." Bethan Pryce did not look up from the box of paperbacks on the counter. "The bell over the door's broken. I assume that's why you didn't hear it chime."

"I heard it. I thought it was a warning."

"It's a bell, Mr. Vane, not a klaxon."

He laughed, the big laugh that had sold four million cinema tickets. It bounced off the shelves and nobody in the shop, which was a small room with a cat in the window, turned round.

"You know who I am."

"You're the one with the horse. Everybody knows that film, because it's on at the Gwyn every Sunday in January." She finally looked up. Dark hair pinned with a pencil, reading glasses on a cord, a jumper with a hole at one elbow. "The paparazzi are outside. Two cars, one with a dent in the back door. I saw them come round from the harbour."

Julian's face flickered. "You could have told me that before I came in."

"You didn't ask." She went back to pricing books. "Also, I'd have to admit I was pleased to see you running."

"Pleased?"

"Walking into someone else's shop with your face on, looking like a man who's been chased down a hill. Do you know how many people in this town would have given their front teeth to see that?"

"I'm very sorry to have caused you amusement."

"Caused? No. You've just been an inconvenience. There's a difference."

He leaned on the counter, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. "I'm usually a very good audience."

"Are you?"

"Ask anyone. Ask my director. Ask the girl who played my sister, who's now my wife."

"I'm not interested in your wife, Mr. Vane. I'm interested in whether you're going to buy something or stand there being seen."

He tapped a copy of _The Mabinogion_ on the counter. "This. And a recommendation. You're the expert, I'm told. The woman from the _Western Mail_ said you turn people away if they don't read."

"She's exaggerating. I turn people away if they ask for Dan Brown."

"Dan Brown isn't—"

"Exactly."

He laughed again, and this time it was less performance. She noticed. She wished she hadn't.

"Go on, then," he said. "Make me a book."

"Not for free."

"I'll pay."

"Good. Then you'll come back and tell me if you liked it, and I'll know whether to put you in the window next to the Dylan Thomas."

"You want a review from me?"

"I want you to finish something you start." She took the book and turned it over. "I know your reputation, Mr. Vane. Four films and you walk out of two of them before the credits. The cast list says you were cast in _Harbour Lights_ and then pulled out of the second act. The rumours in the Co-op say you're a man who needs everybody to like him before he'll do anything."

Julian's smile stayed fixed, but his shoulders lifted. "The Co-op is a cruel place."

"It's the most honest place in Aberystwyth." She slid the book into a paper bag. "Everyone likes you, you know. Everyone in the shop. The cat. That's what worries me."

"Why would it worry you?"

She met his eyes and held them, and it was she who looked away first, to fold the bag. "Because I'm not going to, Mr. Vane. Not yet. And you'll spend the next hour trying to change my mind, which will make me like you less, and you'll hate that."

"Are you flirting with me?"

"Certainly not. I'm pricing your ego. It's at a premium."

He took the bag. Their fingers touched on the handle, and for a half-second neither of them let go.

"Bethan," he said, testing the name on his tongue. "Is that Welsh?"

"It's a Welsh name for a Welsh woman who'll be closing up in ten minutes. The back door's through the stockroom. Mind the Hardy."

"And the cars?"

"Tell them you were buying poetry."

## 08. The Morska Conspiracy

The underhold was colder than sin and quieter than a tomb. The brass doors had been oiled so recently that the smell of it hung in the air like incense, and the gas lamps set into the walls burned low and blue, so that every face in the passage looked drowned.

Aya kept her hand on the wall. The stone was damp. Beneath it, faint and rhythmic, she could hear the great clockwork heart of the guild turning over in its housing, a sound she had come to know better than her own pulse these past eight months. It had stopped.

"It's stopped," said Katra, behind her.

"Hush."

"The pumps are stopped. The pumps have never stopped. Simeon, tell her."

"I can hear it," said Simeon, his voice tight. "Quiet, Katra. Please."

Aya's mask itched. She had sewn the silver filigree into the velvet herself, three nights running, pricking her fingers until the linen had a faint brown ghost on it. Her mother had worn a mask like it, once, to a masquerade in the Theatre Royal, and had danced with a duke who did not recognise her. Aya had been six, and had been very proud.

_Your gift is in your blood, child,_ her mother had whispered, at the window, the night before the hanging. _Don't let them tell you it's a sin. Don't let them tell you it's yours._

Aya put her hand on the door.

It was iron, black and banded, with a lock the size of a fist. The key was in her coat. She did not need the key. The lock had been broken for three weeks; Simeon had broken it, on the instruction of the guild's own Warden, who had then sold the information to the Crown for the sum of two hundred sovereigns and a promise of pardon.

Except that the Warden was in Newgate. He had been taken in the early hours of the morning, in a raid by the Lamplighters, with his coat half buttoned and his mouth still full of bread. The guild had been headless for six hours. Aya had been the one to make the sudden, dangerous decision to come down here tonight, with a dozen men at her back and a hard, bright fear in her chest that she called ambition, because the word was easier to bear.

She pushed the door open.

The underhold was lit. Not by gas. By candles, dozens of them, set into iron sconces around a great circular chamber whose ceiling vanished into shadow. At the centre stood a dais, and on the dais, a chair. In the chair sat the Warden's mistress, Dame Ysolde Crane, still wearing the grey travelling cloak she had worn to the funeral of her second husband three years before.

She was not in Newgate.

She was not alone.

On either side of the dais stood four men with rifles across their chests, and beside them two women with long-bladed knives, sheathed at their hips in leather that had been oiled until it gleamed. Behind the chair, a gap in the gloom resolved itself into a brass automaton, seven feet high, its clockwork eyes glowing faintly red. It did not move. It did not need to.

"Ah," said Dame Ysolde. "You've brought friends."

Aya did not answer. She was counting the exits. Two behind her. One at the far end, through a narrow arch, half-hidden by a curtain of chain. She would not reach any of them before the rifles fired.

"Take off your mask, Miss Lethbridge," said Dame Ysolde pleasantly. "I should like to see the face of the girl who summons the dead."

The name fell into the room like a dropped plate.

Katra made a small, strangled sound. Simeon took a step back and then stopped himself, very slowly, the way a man does when he realises the floor under him is not what he thought.

"I don't know what you mean," Aya said.

"Oh, come now. The Warden talked, before he was taken. He talked very prettily, in the end. He gave us a list of names. Yours is the only one that surprised us." Dame Ysolde tilted her head, and the candles caught the silver in her hair. "The daughter of Lord Ambrose Lethbridge, who sits in the Commons and votes against the Immateria Act every session. Who has a seat on the Board of Coal and Iron. Whose late wife was hanged for practising the very art you are practising now, in the cellar of a house on Cheapside, with a boy from the orphanage holding the chalk."

Aya's mouth went dry. "My mother was innocent."

"Your mother was careless," said Dame Ysolde, "which is the same thing, in a court of law. And so are you."

The automaton moved. It was barely a movement at all, a soft click of gears, a turn of the head. Its eyes fixed on Aya's face, and the red glow intensified until she could see her own reflection in the polished brass.

Simeon spoke, his voice cracking. "Let her go. Take me instead. I'm the one who opened the door."

"Simeon—"

"Hush, Katra. You don't know what she is."

"Oh, but I do," said Dame Ysolde, and she leaned forward, her elbows on her knees, her eyes shining with an interest that made Aya's skin crawl. "I want to know what she is worth. Miss Lethbridge, you have a choice. You can tell me where the scythan relic is kept, or you can tell me who else in the guild shares your gift, and I will consider the lives of your two companions as a payment in good faith. Or you can tell me nothing, and watch them hang from the lamp-hooks while I count the seconds."

Aya's mind raced. A hostage's worth was set by the hostage's usefulness, and she was useful only if she was alive. She could give them a name. Any name. A dead woman in Limehouse. A pauper who would never be found. The lie would hold for a day. Perhaps two.

But the relic was not hidden in the depths of the guild. It was in her own room, sewn into the lining of her second-best coat, and if the Crown learned that, they would not bother to ask her anything again.

"Let them go," she said, "and I'll tell you everything."

"No," said Katra. "Aya, no—"

"I'll tell you everything," Aya repeated, and she lifted her trembling hands to the silver mask, and slowly, carefully, began to undo the ribbon.

## 09. Gunpowder Rendezvous

The bar was called the Hollow Nickel, and it smelled like wet cardboard and spilled whisky, which was about what Revy expected from a town that didn't have a name on the map.

She took the stool at the far end, where the light was worst and the walls were thickest. Her boots went up on the rail. Her cigarette went behind her ear. Two Berettas sat on the bar in front of her, in plain view, like a pair of cats waiting to see who'd blink first.

The bounty was a banker named Halvard who'd gone south with four million in syndicate money and a yacht he couldn't sail. Revy had a photo, a map, and a wad of advance cash she'd already spent on bourbon. Somebody else had the same photo. She'd seen it on the wanted board at the station two hours ago, and she'd known, the way you know a mosquito is in the room, that the other hunter would be here too.

The door opened. Light fell across the floor in a long pale stripe.

Spike Spiegel walked in like he owned the place and had just decided to sell it. Long coat, loose tie, a cigarette in his teeth that he didn't seem to be smoking. His good eye was half-closed. The other eye, the glass one, caught the bar light and held it for a second too long.

He saw her. He didn't stop walking. He just smiled, slow and lazy, and said, "Huh."

"Huh?" Revy said.

"Figured you'd be here."

"You figured wrong. I got here first."

"Yeah." He slid onto the stool beside her and waved a finger at the bartender. "Bourbon. Whatever she's having, double it." He looked at the guns. "You planning on shooting me, or just making a point?"

"Still deciding."

"Good. I like a woman who takes her time."

Revy laughed, a short sharp bark that made the bartender glance over. "You're full of shit, Spiegel."

"Probably." He lit the cigarette, finally, and let the smoke curl up toward the water-stained ceiling. "I hear you got a dragon on your arm."

"You want to see it?"

"Wouldn't mind."

"Pay for the drink first."

"I'm broke," said Spike, with great cheer. "I'm always broke. It's a lifestyle choice."

They drank. They drank too much. Revy told him about a job in Macau, a dead Triad boss, a fight in a hotel elevator that had ended with three men in the lobby and no one ever learning which one she'd shot. Spike told her about a jazz bar on Mars, a girl with red hair who had been a syndicate enforcer's wife, and a man he'd once shot in the back of the head because he'd asked for a light.

"I've died once already," he said, flat, when she asked why he never seemed afraid. "Doesn't much scare me, doing it again."

"Your pal Jet must love that."

"Jet's got enough worrying for both of us." His good eye flicked to hers. "Rock says you took his shotgun once and fired it at a helicopter."

"It was a good shot."

"It was a terrible shot."

"It was a _good shot_, it just missed."

Spike laughed, the sound low and warm in the back of his throat. Revy realised she was smiling and made herself stop. Her hand had drifted to the Berettas. Spike's hand, she noticed, had drifted to the bar, two fingers resting next to the glass, close enough to hers that the knuckles nearly touched.

"Halvard's in the motel by the rail yard," Spike said quietly. "Room eleven. He's been drinking since noon. Ugly yacht, ugly woman, ugly everything."

"You checked."

"I always check. Don't you?"

"Yeah." She didn't move her hand. "Thought we'd go together."

"Thought you'd try to beat me to the door."

"I'm going to beat you to the door."

"Good." He grinned. "Let's go, then."

* * *

The motel was a line of low white boxes with peeling doors and a single flickering sign. Room eleven was at the end, its curtains drawn, a crooked lamp burning in the window. Revy stood to the left of the door, back against the wall, one gun up and angled at the frame. Spike stood opposite, cigarette still dangling, his gun held loose and easy at his side like a man waiting for a bus.

"Three," he said.

She shook her head. "Two."

"Okay. Two."

They moved together.

Revy kicked the door. It burst inward on a crack of splintering wood and the room opened in front of her: an unmade bed, a wardrobe with its doors hanging open, a small square table with a bottle on it, and a fat man in a torn shirt scrambling backward across the carpet with a revolver in both hands.

"Don't!" Halvard shrieked.

Revy's gun was already on him. Her finger had already taken the first pull of the trigger. She saw the revolver's barrel tracking toward her chest, saw the sweat on his forehead, and in the half-second before she fired, she heard the click of a second hammer behind her.

Spike was standing in the doorway. His gun was pointed directly at the back of her head.

They froze, all three, in a silence that stretched and snapped.

"Spike," Revy said, without turning.

"Revy," Spike said, without moving.

"You're pointing that at me."

"Yeah. You're pointing yours at him. So we're all very busy."

Halvard whimpered. The revolver shook in his fat hands.

"You planned this," Revy said.

"Didn't plan anything." Spike's voice was perfectly calm, almost amused. "Thought you'd shoot him and leave me holding the bag. You always do."

"I always do," she agreed. "And you always let me."

A long pause. The lamp in the window buzzed. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and stopped.

"Don't make me do this, Revy," Spike said softly.

"You're the one with the gun in my back."

"Yeah. I know." He sighed, and there was something in it she had not expected, something tired and aching and almost tender. "Whatever happens, happens."

She laughed, once, and felt her own hand begin to relax. "You're such a fucking cliché."

"Only on Tuesdays."

Halvard made a small, hopeless noise and dropped the revolver on the carpet.

Neither of them moved for another full second. Then Revy lowered her gun, slow and deliberate, and Spike lowered his, and they both turned at the same moment to look at the fat man on the floor, who was staring at them in dawning, horrified understanding.

"Well," Spike said. "That's one way to make a point."

## 10. Útiseta

The mound was called Gravhögen, which Astrid said meant "the grave hill," and then said it again in a different voice, as if the words tasted better the second time. It sat at the edge of the birch wood, half a kilometre past the last of the red houses, and in the June gloaming it looked like a sleeping animal under a blanket of moss.

"We have to be there before the church bell," Astrid said. "Not after. Before."

"Why?"

"Because after is when the wrong people come."

Linnea didn't ask who the wrong people were. She had been in the village eleven weeks now, and she had learned that most questions in Astrid's company made the air go thin and strange, the way it did when a storm was coming. Better to nod. Better to carry the blanket.

They had prepared all afternoon, in Astrid's grandmother's kitchen, under a clock that ran four minutes fast. The old woman had not been there. She was asleep upstairs with the curtains drawn, and Astrid had moved around the room with the quiet confidence of someone who knew exactly where every drawer was. Salt, in a twist of waxed paper. A spoon of honey. A thin brass needle, which Astrid had tested on her own thumb, watching the bead of blood with an expression of pure concentration. Two candles, one white, one black, which they had lit at the table and extinguished, one after the other, with their fingers.

"You have to be honest," Astrid had said. "If you lie during útiseta, it hears you."

"What is it?"

"It's a thing that listens."

Linnea had laughed. Astrid had not.

Now they climbed the mound in the dark, their feet slipping on the moss, the birch trunks white around them like the bones of a long-dead forest. At the summit, Astrid knelt and began to clear a space with her hands. There was a flat stone beneath the moss, carved with lines that Linnea could not quite make out in the dimness, and a shallow hollow beside it that looked like it had been worn by a great many knees.

"Sit," Astrid said. "Put the blanket around you. Face the stone. Don't look at the trees."

"What's in the trees?"

"Nothing. That's why you don't look."

Linnea sat. The moss was damp through the blanket. Below them, the village was dark except for one window, where a light burned in someone's kitchen. Stockholm felt like a different planet. She tried to remember the sound of trams on Kungsholmen, the noise of her old friends laughing outside a café, and found only the smell of birch and rain.

"Now," Astrid said, sitting down beside her, very close. "You ask. Out loud. Ask about someone you want to know about."

"Ask what?"

"Ask about Mattias."

Mattias was in their class. He had a girlfriend in Umeå and a Walkman with a tape of Ace of Base he played so loud on the bus that the whole back row could hear it. Linnea had never said a word to him.

"Why would I want to know about Mattias?"

Astrid gave her a sideways glance in the gloom. "Because you do," she said, quite simply. "Everybody wants to know about someone."

Linnea took a breath and said, in a voice she hardly recognised, "What does Mattias think of me?"

Silence.

The birch leaves stirred, though there was no wind. A bird began to call somewhere in the wood, three notes, a pause, three again.

Then, from the trees behind them, faint and dry, came a sound like a stick being drawn slowly across a stone.

Linnea's breath caught. Astrid's hand closed over her wrist. "Don't look," she whispered.

The stick sound came again. Closer.

"It's the wind," Linnea said, too quickly.

"There is no wind."

"Then it's an animal."

"Animals don't scratch in a pattern," Astrid said, and her voice had changed, lost its bored teenage drawl and become strangely formal, almost singsong. "Listen. Three, and then two. Three, and then two. It's counting."

"Counting what?"

"Us."

A cold weight settled on Linnea's chest, pressing down, heavy as a wet sack. She could not breathe through it. She could not look away from the stone. The carvings on its surface seemed to shift in the moonlight, lines curling into shapes she almost recognised, faces, a hand, a word that might have been her own name.

She told herself it was the moss. The shadow. Her own fear, rising up through her stomach like bile, painting pictures on the dark.

"We should go," she said.

"Not yet." Astrid's grip tightened. "It hasn't answered."

"Answered what?"

"Your question."

As if in response, the bird in the wood stopped calling. The silence that followed was absolute, a vacuum that swallowed the sound of their breathing. Linnea felt, rather than heard, a presence shift at the edge of the clearing, something moving through the birches in a slow, deliberate line, the way a person walks when they are looking for something they have dropped.

Then the wind came up, sudden and sharp, rattling the leaves and tugging at the blanket, and with it came a voice.

It was a boy's voice. Not Mattias's. Higher, softer, speaking a single word in Swedish that Linnea did not catch.

Astrid let go of her wrist and stood up very slowly.

"Oh no," she said, not to Linnea but to the trees. "Oh, we didn't mean it. We were only playing."

The wind dropped. The birch leaves fell still. The stone lay flat and silent under its moss.

Linnea stood up on legs that did not feel like hers and looked down the hill toward the village, where the one lit window had gone dark.

"Astrid," she said. "Whose kitchen is that?"

Astrid did not answer. She was already walking down the slope, quickly, not looking back, and after a moment Linnea followed, the blanket trailing behind her in the wet grass like something she had forgotten to let go of.

## 11. Therapeutic Boundaries

Marcus Bell always walks in the same way, which is the first thing I notice about him now. I used to be able to tell whether he'd had a good night from the way he took his shoes off in the changing room. Now he's on the other side of the couch, and I'm the one on the paper sheet, and I'm too busy noticing the fine grey scatter at his temples to tell whether he's sleeping.

"Hi, Jake," he says.

"Hi."

"You've been doing the home programme?"

"Some of it."

"Some of it is what I expected." He pulls the rolling stool closer and sits, and the wheels make the same small squeak they made on the training track in Leeds, eleven years ago. "Lie back. Left leg to the edge. Let's see where you're at."

I lie back. The ceiling has a water stain shaped like Ireland. I don't look at Marcus. I look at Ireland.

He takes my left ankle in both hands. His thumbs settle on the outside of the joint and his fingers wrap around the heel, not gently, not roughly, but with the precise, unfussy grip of someone who has held a thousand ankles and does not think about any of them. That's the worst part. He doesn't think about me at all.

"Heel slide," he says. "Bend the knee, let the foot come toward you. Stop when I say."

I bend. The knee resists, a dull grinding at the back of the joint, like a rusted hinge. The scar above my kneecap pulls tight, and the old pain in my hamstring flares up through the muscle, a hot wire pulled along the bone.

"Stop."

I stop.

"Good. Breathe out." He rests his hand on my shin, just below the knee, to hold the position. His palm is warm. "You're guarding. That's fine. It's normal. But I need you to let the weight go into my hand. Think of it as handing me something you don't want to carry."

"I'm not carrying anything."

"You're carrying a lot of things, Jake. Your shoulders are up around your ears."

I don't answer that. He's right. My shoulders are rigid, my jaw locked, and every muscle from my neck to my hip seems braced for a blow that never comes.

He shifts his grip. His fingers slide up my calf, the pads pressing firmly into the gastrocnemius, following the line of the muscle toward the back of the knee. I know the anatomy. I knew it before I could read. The popliteal fossa, the gap behind the knee, the place where the nerve runs close to the skin. He's thinking about it too. I can see it in the way he holds his breath as he brings his hand to the joint.

"I'm going to bring the knee up toward your chest now," he says. "Keep the back flat on the couch. Don't let the pelvis tilt."

"I know how to do a straight-leg raise, Marcus."

"Then do it for me."

I lift. The leg comes up, slow and tight, his hands supporting the heel and the back of the thigh. The hamstring screams, and I hold it, and I can feel the sweat start at my temples. He doesn't say anything. He just watches the muscle quiver under his palm, and I know he's measuring it, the exact angle of the knee, the tension in the tendon, whether I've crossed the line from stretch to strain.

"There," he says quietly. "That's the edge. Hold."

I hold. I hate that I'm breathing hard. I hate more that I can feel my own heartbeat in my hip, in the place where his fingers rest against my skin, and that I know he can feel it too.

We don't look at each other. We have not looked at each other for the whole of this session, and it's a kind of game now, a careful, silent refusal. He is a physio. I'm a patient. Nothing else is happening in this room, and both of us know exactly how much effort it takes to keep it that way.

"Lower it," he says. "Slowly. Like a drawbridge."

I lower it. The leg descends in a long controlled arc, and as it does, his hand slides from the back of my thigh to the inside of my knee. A slight pressure. A tiny rotation, the kind of thing you'd do if you were checking for tracking, the patella sliding in its groove. His thumb rests just below the inside of the joint, on the medial collateral ligament, and presses once, gently, to see whether it flinches.

It does. I can't help it. My knee jerks, and I gasp, and the sound is louder than I want it to be.

"Sorry," he says, and his voice is rough. "I know. That's the ligament. It's still angry."

"I'm not angry."

"No." He takes his hand away. The air on my skin is suddenly cold. "But you were, last time. You threw the towel at me in the gym."

"That was eleven years ago."

"It was three weeks ago, Jake. You're on the ceiling, not the floor." He stands. The stool rolls back with its small, familiar squeak. "Same time Thursday. Do the bridging exercises. Ten reps, three sets, and let me know if the hamstring cramps."

I sit up. My leg is shaking. I look at him properly for the first time in forty minutes, and he looks back, and there's a tiredness in his face that I don't recognise, the same tiredness I used to see at the end of a training session, when he'd spent the whole morning holding my shoulder down while I cursed at the ceiling.

"You're good at this," I say.

"I'm good at my job."

"That's not what I meant."

He picks up the clipboard and tucks it under his arm. "I know what you meant," he says, and he opens the door, and the corridor light falls across his face, and for one second he almost smiles before he goes.

## 12. High Seas and Low Vices

The dragon woke before I did. It always does. I lie in the narrow bed in the factor's annex at Canton with the shutters still closed and feel it stirring in my belly, a cold, patient animal, unwinding itself from my ribs and asking politely for its breakfast.

I was a decent officer once. Ask the captain of the _Thessaly_. Ask anyone at Whampoa. I took the surrender at Kowloon with a clean sword and a straight face, and I was mentioned in despatches for it, in a sentence that made my mother weep into her tea at Bath. Now I'm the second lieutenant on the depot ship _Argus_, with orders to inspect the fire-safety rota of the Company godowns, and no one has asked me to inspect anything for six weeks.

"Mr. Merrick." The boy at the door has a face like a drowned kitten and a tray of hot water. "The Captain's compliments, sir, and you're to take the morning muster at eight."

"Tell him I've been taken ill."

"Sir—"

"Tell him the malaria's back. He'll believe that. Everyone believes it now."

I wash in the basin, then dress in the grey linen the Company tailor made for me in February, before the hands started shaking. The mirror shows a man of thirty-two who looks fifty. My eyes are the colour of dirty water. My jaw has the faint tremor I've learned to hide by clenching my teeth, so that people say I'm stern, which is useful.

Outside, Canton is already loud. Hawkers, bells, the stink of the river, the cry of a bird-seller sitting on a bamboo stool with a cage of finches. I walk down the Thirteen Factories' lane, past the flags of the Americans and the Dutch, and past the lacquered doors of the Hong merchants with their gold-leaf tigers. I know the route as well as the back of my own hand, and I hate it as well.

At the end of the lane is a crooked house with a red lantern, and a boy with a string of cash coins waiting outside. He grins when he sees me.

"Mr. Merrick! Cousin is waiting."

"Is he."

"He has the good stuff. From Bengal." The boy holds up two fingers. "Two silver for a pipe, sir, and he gives you the candle for free."

"I'll give him one dollar, and not a cent more."

"Cousin says two."

"Cousin can come and tell me that to my face."

Cousin comes out of the back room a moment later, a fat, sleepy man in a dressing gown embroidered with cranes. He smiles without showing his teeth.

"Ah, the Lieutenant. You've been away."

"I've been busy."

"You've been ill, I heard."

"I've been drinking," I say, because it's easier. "Same thing, in your trade."

He laughs, a soft wet sound. "Sit, sit. You're grey as a shark's belly this morning. I'll get the pipe."

I sit on the cushioned bench. The room smells of sandalwood and of something sweeter and more rotten beneath it. A woman sleeps in a corner under a pile of silk. She might be twenty. She might be fifty. I don't look at her.

The pipe is brought. The flame is lit, a candle held to the bowl. I lie back, and the first breath goes into my lungs like warm honey, and the dragon uncoils, and for a long, sweet moment there is no navy, no Company, no Lady Fanshawe's letter asking whether I would consider a posting home. There is only the ceiling and the slow, steady pulse behind my eyes.

"Better?" says Cousin.

"Much."

"Good. Now, Lieutenant. You owe me for last month."

I open my eyes. "I'll have it by Friday."

"By Friday you'll owe me for this month." He spreads his hands. "It's simple arithmetic. My arithmetic is always simple. Yours is the problem."

"What do you want?"

"A favour. Small. You'll like it."

"I doubt that."

"The _Argus_ is taking a cargo to Macau on Thursday. Chests of Bengal, marked as ballast. I'd like to know which holds they're in, and when the watch changes."

I stare at him. "You're asking a royal naval officer to spy on a Company ship."

"I'm asking a sick man for a little help with his fire-safety rota." He smiles again. "And in return, I forget you've been buying pipes on Hog Lane since before you came to Canton. You'd be surprised how many Captains would be interested in that."

The dragon hisses. I feel it in my teeth.

"Friday," I say. "The pipe, and the money. And I'll tell you what I can."

"Of course, Lieutenant. You're a gentleman."

I walk out into the sunlight with my hands in my pockets and the blue taste of smoke on my tongue. The boy with the cash is waiting. He pockets my dollar and grins.

"Cousin says he's very pleased with you, sir."

"Tell him I'm very pleased with the pipe."

At the end of the lane, I stop by the harbour wall and look out at the grey masts of the _Argus_ riding at anchor in the brown water. My old ship. My old life, lying there with her sails furled, waiting for me to come back and be a decent officer again.

Somewhere behind me, the bird-seller's finches begin to sing, and I think, quite clearly, that there is no sound in this city I can hear without wanting to be asleep.

## 13. Sisyphus Gives Advice

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_My husband has started leaving the lid off the marmalade. I've asked him three times. He says it's a "small thing." I say it's not a small thing if I'm the one who has to scrape the fur off the top. Am I being unreasonable?_

_— Furious in Fenwick_

Crow, write this down, and do not peck the paper.

Dear Furious,

You are not being unreasonable. You are being precise, which is a different thing and much more annoying to the person on the receiving end. Your husband is not being cruel. He is being a man who has never once had to scrape fur off marmalade, and so the lid is, to him, a rounding error.

Here is what I'd try. Stop asking him about the lid. Asking three times turns the lid into a debate, and debates are where people get entrenched. Instead, leave a spoon in the jar, and the next time he opens it without the lid, put the lid on the table where he will see it, with a cup of tea beside it. You are not training him. You are making the lid visible. Most small grievances die in the open air.

If it doesn't work, I'll write back, and you can tell me whether the fur is winning.

— S.

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_Dear Sisyphus,_

## 14. Lost & Found in Osaka

"You're going to hurt that poor amp."

The voice came from the top of the stairs leading down into the Den Den Town arcade. Hana stopped with one hand on the rail. She had been walking past the stalls of second-hand keyboards for forty minutes, carrying a plastic bag with a Korg monotron and a bundle of cables she'd been too shy to ask the shopkeeper to test. The sound she'd been following had been coming from the crowd by the entrance: an electric guitar, screaming through a battered amplifier, playing something that sounded like a sped-up theme song from a robot show.

She looked up. A girl was sitting on the edge of a flight of steps, cross-legged, a red Stratocaster in her lap. She had short hair the colour of cherry blossoms that had been left in the rain. Her jacket was too big. Her grin was far too wide.

"I'm sorry," Hana said. "I didn't mean to stop."

"You were listening, though. Everyone listens to me. Can't help it." The girl hopped down the steps and stuck out a hand. "Ren. Well, that's what my friends call me. You can call me whatever, if you don't mind the ringing in your ears."

Hana looked at the hand. She did not take it. "Hana."

"Hana! Like the flower. Nice."

"Like the word for nose."

Ren burst out laughing, loud enough that two salarymen looked round. "That's brilliant. I'm going to use that when people ask me to be quiet."

"I'm not asking you to be quiet."

"You're thinking it."

Hana didn't answer. Her face was heating up in a way she couldn't control. She could feel the ends of her ears going pink, the traitor blush that had followed her through every class at her school since the first grade.

"What were you playing?" she asked, instead.

"'Cyber Maiden Mio.' The opening. Everybody in this city's obsessed with it, which is why I'm doing it. Free publicity." Ren shrugged. "You knew the chord change, though. In the bridge. You were nodding."

"I wasn't."

"You were. Your head was going." Ren tapped her own skull. "Right there. I saw it."

"It was the bass line," Hana said quietly. "It goes down a fifth and then up a semitone. It's a Phrygian thing, from the original pulse track. Most people don't hear it."

Ren's eyes went very wide. Then they narrowed, and her grin changed, became something sharper and more interested. "You play?"

"Synthesizer. Mostly. I make things on the Korg in my bedroom. Nothing anyone would want to hear."

"Show me something."

"I don't have it here."

"Show me something on your phone, then."

Hana hesitated. Then, hating herself a little, she took out the old handset and pressed play on a file she'd recorded at three in the morning, a looping arpeggio made of rain sounds and a falling sine wave. It sounded thin on the phone speaker. Ren listened with her head cocked, eyes half-closed, her fingers tapping on the body of the Stratocaster in time.

"That's sad," Ren said.

"It isn't supposed to be sad."

"It's sad. But it's good sad." Ren opened her eyes. "It's the kind that makes you want to go and get a bowl of noodles and sit by a window."

"That's a strange thing to say."

"I know. I'm a strange person. Everyone says so." Ren stood up and slung the guitar over her shoulder. "Are you hungry?"

"I'm—" Hana looked at the clock on the arcade wall. Twenty past two. Her mother would be home by four. "I suppose."

"There's a kaiten-zushi on the next street. The plates go round on a conveyor belt, and the cheap ones are all just tuna and rice. But they've got the good tea, the bitter stuff. You'll like it. Come on."

"I don't know you."

"Yet."

Hana looked at the bright, stupid, unbearably confident face in front of her, and her heart did something it hadn't done before, a little skip, like a missed step in the dark.

"Only one plate," she said. "And I'm paying for my own."

"Deal." Ren clapped her on the shoulder, then took it away again, quickly, as if she'd remembered something. "Sorry. Sorry! Too much. I always do that. My sister says I touch people like a dog with a stick."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine, I was out of line. I'm going to be better. I promise."

They walked out through the crowd together, past the screaming amplifiers and the boys selling anime figures on folding tables, Ren's guitar case bumping against Hana's knee at every step. At the crossroads, the sign for the restaurant flashed red and gold, and somewhere beneath it, a conveyor belt was turning slowly in the warm, fishy air.

"Hana," Ren said, as they stepped into the queue.

"Yes?"

"Do you want to hear the bridge again? When we get there? I'll play it on the amp. Properly."

Hana thought about the rain sounds on her phone, the sad, good thing she'd made in the dark. She thought about the fifth going down and the semitone coming up.

"Yes," she said. "I'd like that."

## 15. The Watch That Remembered

The watch came in a box the size of a sandwich, and Theo had to be talked into wearing it by his mother, who said it was a "smart" watch and therefore had to be better than his old one, which had a crack in the face that looked like a lightning bolt.

"It does everything," said the box.

It did not do everything. It did step counts, and heartbeat, and a little animation of a dog that wagged its tail when Theo reached his goal. It had a button on the side that said REWIND in small grey letters, and when Theo pressed it for the first time, sitting on the bottom stair of the flat with his trainers on, the world went backwards.

It was not dramatic. There was no flash of light, no whooshing sound. The clock on the wall above the cooker simply ran in reverse for about six seconds, and then the kettle in the kitchen clicked off, and his mother said, "Theo, I said _wait_," from the doorway, and he realised he had already been told this.

"Okay," he said. "Okay."

He tried it again at school, in the lunch queue, when Ellie Parsons shoved in front of him and told him he smelled of gym socks. He pressed the button. The queue reversed. Ellie went back to her place, and Theo went back to his, and when she turned round she looked slightly bewildered and said, "Sorry, Theo," and he said, "It's fine," and it was.

For two weeks, it was the best thing in his life.

He rewound maths tests. He rewound the time he'd dropped his tray in front of the head of year. He rewound a conversation with his dad about the holiday in Cornwall that had gone badly. He began to think of the watch as a sort of mercy, a way of stepping sideways out of every embarrassing or horrible moment, and he got a little reckless with it, the way kids do with anything that makes them feel powerful.

He didn't notice the changes at first. His voice dropped, a little, in the middle of the second week. Then it cracked on the word "Mum," which made Ellie laugh so hard she had to sit down. There were hairs on his upper lip that hadn't been there at the start of term, and a spot on his chin the size of a fingernail. He grew an inch overnight, or felt like he had. His trainers pinched.

"You're growing up fast," his dad said, staring at him over his tea. "Did you have a growth spurt or something?"

"Dunno."

"You look about fifteen."

Theo laughed, awkwardly. He was eleven.

At school, people started looking at him differently. Teachers spoke to him in a different tone, as though he were older than he was. Older kids in Year Nine nodded at him in the corridor. A girl from the drama group, Priya, asked him whether he had a girlfriend, and when he said no, she said, "Oh, you will soon," in a voice that made his stomach twist.

It was on a Thursday that Theo realised what the watch was doing.

He pressed REWIND to undo a fall in the playground, where he had skidded in the mud and landed on his hands, and the world rolled back, and he stood up again, clean and dry. Then he saw his reflection in the window of the science block. He was taller than he had been at breakfast. His face was sharper, older. A line had appeared between his eyebrows.

He pressed the button again. The reflection went back, a little. The line softened. Not enough.

He pressed it again, and again, and each time the world rewound, and each time he got a little older when it moved forward. His hands on the watch strap were long-fingered and pale. When he tried to remember how old he had been when he got the watch, the number slid away from him, like something written in water.

The watch's little dog was barking now. Its tail wagged so fast it blurred.

_Use count,_ the screen said, in tiny letters. _You have used REWIND 47 times._

That night, Theo sat on his bed and tried to work out how it had happened. He had thought of the watch as a rewind button on his own life. But every time he rewound, he was not only moving the clock back. He was taking something out, from the places in himself where the years were kept, and the watch was putting that back into the world as something ahead of him. He had been trading years for mistakes.

"Okay," he whispered. "Stop. No more."

He didn't press it.

But it was too late for that, because at breakfast the next morning, his mother set down a plate of toast and stared at him with a look of dawning horror, and said, "Theo, who is this boy?"

He looked up. He was sixteen, with a stranger's face. In the corner of the kitchen, the old wall clock was ticking backwards.

"It's me," Theo said. "Mum, it's me."

She didn't believe him. Nobody did. At school, a girl he had never spoken to called him by a name he had never heard. The records office said he had been enrolled in Year Ten for two years. His old friends walked past him in the corridor as though he were a teacher.

In his bedroom, the watch lay on the carpet where he had thrown it, its screen cracked. The dog had stopped barking.

There was only one thing left to try. Theo picked the watch up, turned it over, and pressed REWIND one final time. He felt the year fall away, all the years he had spent, and he closed his eyes and hoped, very hard, that the world would forgive him for the boy he had been.

## 16. Coney Island, July 1937

She stopped just before the shooting gallery, where the boardwalk planks were still warm from the afternoon, and let the crowd move around her like water around a stone.

The carnival lights were buzzing on, one string at a time, the wheel first and then the Cyclone and then the glass-blown bulbs above the Steeplechase gate. Somewhere a brass band was playing something she half recognised and could not name. The sea was grey and quiet beyond the rail. She could smell frying dough and salt and the sweet rot of spilt lemonade.

_His locks._ The words came back, again, the way they had all afternoon. An old-fashioned phrase, almost a warning. She pushed it down, neatly, as she would fold a letter she didn't want to read.

She had come because she had said she would. That was all. She had said it to him at the Eldridge Street dance hall in June, with the band drowning out the room and his hand cool against her back, and he had looked at her with those dark, unhurried eyes and said, _Coney, the shooting gallery, the last Saturday in July. Come if you want to know what I am._

She didn't know his name. That was the part that kept her awake. She knew his hands, which were long and clever and bore a thin white scar across the left knuckle. She knew his voice, which dropped when he was amused and sharpened when he was not. She knew that when he laughed, he looked at the floor first, as though he expected someone to scold him for it.

She did not know where he lived, or who had taught him to dance so well, or why every man at the hall had moved aside when he walked past with his hat in his hand.

"You came," said a voice behind her.

She turned.

He was standing under the shooting gallery's hand-painted sign, a cigarette between two fingers, unlit. His coat was dark wool, too heavy for July, and his curls were pushed back from his forehead as though he'd been running his hands through them for an hour.

"I said I would," she said.

"You did." He smiled, slowly. "Most people don't do what they say, Miss Hale."

"You know my name."

"I know a great many things about you." He tucked the cigarette behind his ear. "Which is why I was worried you wouldn't come. It's harder to find someone who doesn't want to be found."

"I'm not hiding."

"No? You went very quiet at the hall. You kept looking at the door."

"I was looking at the band."

"Were you?"

She did not answer. She let her gaze drop to the rifles lined up on the rack beside the gallery, their brass sights polished, the paper targets fluttering on the back wall: a row of painted ducks, a row of painted soldiers. The man behind the counter was watching them both with the blank, bored patience of someone who had seen a great many couples flirt over his guns.

"Shall we take a turn?" the young man said. "I'll buy you lemonade. I'm told it's the only thing sold on this boardwalk that doesn't leave you with a sunburn."

"What do you want, Mr.—?"

"Mr. Vance. For now." He offered his arm. She did not take it. He did not seem to mind. "I want you to be honest with me, which will be the novelty of my week. You're clever, Miss Hale. You're going to ask me questions I'd rather not answer, and I'll answer some of them, and you'll decide whether I'm telling the truth. That's the bargain."

"And if I don't like the answers?"

He glanced sideways at the shooting gallery, at the rack of rifles, at the red-painted lever on the counter that the man had just pushed back to load a fresh cartridge.

"Then you'll walk away," he said softly. "And I'll be sorry for it. I always am. But I won't follow you, Miss Hale. I'm not that sort of man. Not yet."

_Not yet._ She heard the two words land in her chest and stay there, a small cold stone.

"Lemonade," she said. "Then you can tell me what your hands are for."

He laughed, the low, surprised sound that made him look at the floor. "Oh, you're going to be trouble."

"I've been told."

They walked together along the boardwalk, through the bright tumbling noise of the carnival, past the fortune-teller's booth and the man selling pink sugar on sticks. At the lemonade stand he bought two paper cups and handed one to her, and his fingers brushed hers, and this time neither of them pulled away.

"The scar," she said. "On your knuckle."

"Bar fight," he said. "In Jersey."

"You're lying."

"Only a little." He sipped his lemonade, watching her over the rim. "The truth is a long story, and the boardwalk closes at midnight."

The brass band struck up something faster. Somewhere behind them a child shrieked with laughter, and the Cyclone rattled overhead, cars roaring down into the dark.

"Then tell me the short one," she said.

He looked at her for a long time, and something in his face changed, grew more careful, more dangerous. "The short one," he said, "is that I've been waiting for you since June, and I'm not sure yet whether that's good news for you or for me."

## 17. Caribou Season at the Lodge

The second morning, JT woke before the others and lay in the bunk listening to the cabin tick as it warmed. Somewhere outside, something was breathing. Not wind. Wind had a shape to it. This had a rhythm, slow and deliberate, like a man trying not to snore.

He dressed in the dark. His boots were still wet from yesterday. Mike was asleep on the cot by the stove, one arm flung over his face, and their father's bunk was empty, the blanket folded square at the foot the way he always folded it, the way he'd been doing it since JT was small enough to hide in the closet.

The porch was cold enough to hurt. JT stopped on the top step.

The antlers were stacked along the railing. Eleven sets, maybe twelve, tines locked together like the fingers of people praying. Last night there had been four. JT was sure of it, because he'd counted them while his father was lighting the lamp, and his father had said, "That's enough for the trip," in a voice that didn't sound like his.

"You're up early," said Mike, behind him.

JT jumped. Mike was standing in the doorway in his long underwear, barefoot on the boards, looking at the antlers without any surprise at all.

"When did they get here?"

"Dad put them there." Mike came down the steps. "He said they were getting restless. Said we shouldn't leave them out in the open."

"We didn't kill any more."

"Didn't we?"

JT wanted to say yes. He wanted to say it so badly that his mouth went dry. But he thought of the first caribou, the one that had watched, ears flicking, and of the way it had fallen. Quiet. As though it had wanted nothing more.

"Where's Dad?"

Mike pointed toward the treeline with his chin. "He went to see the buck."

"Mike, there wasn't a buck. We didn't see one."

"Sure we did." Mike's voice was pleasant, almost bored. "It was the one in the clearing. He's been waiting for us to come back for it."

They followed the tracks in the snow. The hooves were wide and deep, pressed hard into the crust, and they went in a straight line from the porch to the trees. There were no tracks coming out. JT kept waiting to see a second set, his father's boots, the drag of a rifle sling, but there was only the one line, and beside it, every few feet, the shallow prints of a man walking in his son's footsteps.

The clearing was exactly as JT remembered. Brush, snow, the open space. His father stood in the middle of it, rifle slung over his shoulder, hands at his sides. He wasn't looking at the trees. He was looking at the sky, where the sun had come up pale and thin and wrong, as though someone had painted it on glass.

"Dad?"

His father turned. His eyes were too bright. His lips moved, and for a moment JT thought he heard the rest of a sentence, something low and rough, like a stag in rut calling across the valley.

Then the sound came again, from the trees, and it was not a stag. It was closer to a child humming a tune with its mouth shut.

"He's still here," his father said softly. "He wants us to stay for the season."

"We're leaving today," JT said. "Mike and I. We're taking the truck."

His father smiled. It was a kind smile, which was the worst thing JT had seen all morning.

"Son," he said, "we aren't hunting anymore. We're keeping him company."

Behind JT, in the brush, something stepped over a fallen log. JT did not turn around. He kept his eyes on his father's face, and his father kept his eyes on something past JT's shoulder, and neither of them said what they both were thinking, which was that the buck had been standing there the whole time, close enough to touch, and that this time nobody was going to be able to look away.

## 18. Hell Is Other Demons

Lucifer woke at ten past nine, which was early for him, and found Lilith sitting on the edge of the bed with her back to him, painting her toenails a colour he suspected was called Arterial.

"You're up," he said.

"I've been up since six." She blew on her toe. "You snore like a cathedral organ."

"I don't snore. I _resonate_."

"You snore in D minor."

He lay back and stared at the ceiling, which was black and stippled with tiny glowing dots, like a cheap planetarium. Somewhere below, a scream rose and fell, the familiar sound of a soul being introduced to the lower floors. He could tell from the pitch that it was a financier. Financiers always went high.

"Is that Hendricks?" he asked.

"Probably. He wanted the second circle, and we've put him in the fourth, where the bailiffs are."

"Bit harsh."

"He sold his mother's wedding ring to a man who sold it back to his wife for three times the price. I think the bailiffs have a point."

Lucifer sat up. His wings, folded like damp umbrellas along his back, rustled and stretched. He had not been in an especially good mood for some centuries, but this morning he was surprisingly quiet, a thing that had settled in him over the last few weeks, like silt in a riverbed.

"Are you going to say it?" Lilith asked.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying since Thursday."

"I've said nothing since Thursday."

"Exactly. That's what I mean." She finished the last nail and looked up at him, and her face was not the face of a demon at all, but of a tired woman in her late forties with dark, shrewd eyes and a faint line between her brows. "You were lonely, Luci. You can tell me that. I'm not going to put it on the list."

He said nothing. She sighed and got up.

"Right," she said. "Coffee. Then you can come and watch me fire the new chief of staff. He's been stealing souls from the cafeteria."

* * *

The office of the Lord of the Pit was on the ninety-ninth floor of a tower that had been built at a 13-degree angle, so that every visitor had to lean slightly forward to walk in. The windows looked out over the lake of fire, which had been drained and refilled four times since the Renaissance and now contained a small, nervous collection of ducks.

"The ducks are a sanctuary," Lucifer said, when Lilith raised an eyebrow. "The Geneva Convention requires it."

"There's no Geneva Convention in Hell."

"There is now. I signed it on Tuesday. Nobody read it."

The new chief of staff was a squat, pink-skinned demon called Brannoch, who had been promoted after the last one was eaten by a carnivorous filing cabinet. He stood before the desk clutching a clipboard with trembling hands.

"My Lord," he said, "I can explain the cafeteria."

"You can explain the souls," Lilith said. "The cafeteria's just where you were caught."

"It's a _budget_ issue. We're short-staffed. The sinners keep being sent to Purgatory for administrative reasons, which means we have to fill the quotas with reheated leftovers from Dante's notes, and nobody's ever done a proper audit of the—"

"Brannoch," said Lucifer, not unkindly, "you've been eating the cabbage souls."

Brannoch went quiet. Then, very slowly, his eyes slid sideways toward the window, as though the ducks might offer him an alibi.

"They're _ever_ so crunchy," he whispered.

"I'm sure they are," said Lilith. "Get out. Go and have a long think about Dante."

When the door had shut, Lucifer leaned back in his chair. "You were a bit hard on him."

"He's a glutton. Gluttony is a sin. I'm just doing my job." Lilith perched on the edge of the desk and picked up a pen, which she clicked three times in succession, a habit that had annoyed him for four thousand years. "Are you going to tell me why you've been sending me letters?"

"I haven't."

"You've been leaving them in my coat pocket. Every Tuesday for six weeks. Notes on hotel paper. 'Dinner, if you're free. I'll make the risotto. Don't bring Adam.'"

"That's a very good risotto."

"It's _awful_, Luci. You can't cook. You once set the Thames on fire."

"That was an accident. The Thames had it coming."

She laughed, a sharp, surprised sound, and then she stopped laughing and looked at him with an expression he had not seen on her face in a very long time.

"I'm not coming back to the old arrangement," she said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"You didn't have to." She set the pen down. "But I might come to dinner. If the risotto's edible. And if you promise not to make a speech about how all the world is your kingdom."

He considered this. "The world is technically my kingdom."

"Lucifer."

"Fine." He stood, shook out his wings, and offered her his arm, with a flourish that sent a small sulphurous puff of smoke into the air. "No speeches. Just a risotto that might give you food poisoning and a conversation about the cabbage souls."

She took his arm. Her hand was warm.

Below them, the ducks began to quack, very loudly, in unison, and somewhere far beneath the floor a financier shrieked a note that could have been a hymn, or a curse, or simply the sound of someone who had been, for the first time in a long while, noticed.

## 19. The Sand and the Sword

The sand in the Ludus stank of old blood and wet dogs. I lay on my back in the courtyard before dawn with my face turned toward the gallery steps, and I counted the stones in the arch above me until the light began to show through the gaps, which took eleven minutes, I think. I have counted many things in my life. Coins, mostly. Days, in the early years. Men who were still alive when I lay down to sleep and were not alive when I woke.

Antonius came down the steps with a cup of watered wine and a lamp that smoked. He was the only one of the trainers who still spoke to me as though I were a man and not a line in a ledger.

"You didn't sleep."

"No."

"Drink. It'll help with the bad dreams, if not the rest."

I took the cup. The wine was warm and tasted of iron. Somewhere up in the palace district, a bell began to ring, slow and far away, the signal that the Emperor's litter had left his house on the Palatine. There would be games today, the first in a month. A summons for the crowd. They had been told that Marcus Aurelius would be present, and so every man with a sesterce would be in the stands by noon, hungry for blood and sunlight and the chance to say later that they had seen it.

"You're not fighting," Antonius said. "Not in the Circus. The Praetor wants the Thracian for the morning, and you're in the afternoon, against the net man."

"I'm always in the afternoon."

"You're the afternoon's favourite, Varro. That's something."

I drank the rest of the wine and set the cup on the step. My shoulder ached where a Parthian arrow had grazed it, four years ago, in a border skirmish I still could not quite remember. The skin had knitted badly, puckered into a pale ridge. Girls in the brothel near the Subura used to trace it with their fingers and ask if it hurt. It did not hurt. It only reminded me what I had been before I was bought.

I had been a farmer's son in the hills above Venusia, with a mother who sang badly and a father who never once looked up from the plough. The debts came, and then the men with the rope. My father sold me to a slaver for eleven denarii and a bag of spelt. I was fourteen. I have forgotten his face. I remember the price.

The gladiators' barracks were a long stone hall with a row of low bunks along each wall. The air was thick with the smell of olive oil and sweat and something sharp and sour that might have been fear, or might have been the garlic the cooks put into everything. A young Gaul was sitting on his bed, whittling a piece of ash into a crude little horse. He did not look up. His hands were steady.

"Are you going to sleep, Varro?" he asked.

"Are you?"

"No." He carved a delicate line along the horse's flank. "They say the Emperor has been reading Epictetus in the mornings. He tells the Senate that a man must bear what he cannot change."

"And what can't he change?"

"Us." The Gaul blew the wood dust away. "The crowd will not spare us because the Emperor is kind. They'll spare us if the games are good, and the wine is cheap, and nobody in the stands has to think about the grain dole, or the tax men, or the Parthians on the eastern border, who are very likely to come and eat us all in our beds before the next harvest."

"You've been talking to the scribes again."

"I've been listening to the flies." He grinned and showed a gap where a tooth had been. "They say the grain riots in the Subura were put down by the Praetorians last spring. Forty dead. Their bodies went in the Tiber, and the fishermen wouldn't eat the eels for a month."

I thought of the girl who had traced my scar, and wondered whether she had been one of the forty, and was ashamed that I did not know.

At noon, the trumpets sounded. We marched out into the Circus, under the great painted banners, through a roar that felt like being struck by a wave. The sun was white. The sand was hot through my sandals. I could smell roasting meat and smoke from the braziers and the sweet heavy scent of the perfumed silk that the matrons had brought to hide the stink of the crowd. I looked up, once, at the imperial box, and saw a small grey figure in a plain white toga sitting very still beside a row of Vestal Virgins, his hands folded on his knees. He looked tired. He looked like a man who would rather be somewhere else.

The Thracian won the morning. I did not watch.

I was called in the afternoon, against the net man, a Nubian called Shimba who had killed nine men in the arena and had never once been wounded. We would not fight to the death today. The Praetor had ordered a demonstration: a match of skill, not of slaughter, for the Emperor's amusement. The crowd would be disappointed. They always were when the blood did not come.

I stood in the sand and waited for the signal, my trident in my hand, and I thought of my father's back bent over the plough, and the eleven denarii, and the grain riots in the Subura, and the Gaul's little wooden horse, which he had given me that morning without a word.

The trumpet sounded. The net came down, and I stepped aside.

And the crowd, to my astonishment, began to cheer for the Emperor, for the Praetor, for the sun, for anything but us.

## 21. Sealed Pattern

I'm not doing it, I'm not doing it, I'm not doing it. Lorna has her hair up in a clip that's about to give out, and she's laughing at something on the menu, and the candle on our table is throwing that warm flickering light across her cheekbones, and somewhere in the back of my head a little voice has already started reading the restaurant's Wi-Fi password off the chalkboard by the bar. It's _Tavern2019_. Because of course it is. The owner clearly thought about it for approximately four seconds.

Stop it. You're on a date. Be a person.

"So you're a security consultant," Lorna says. "What does that actually mean? Like, do you hack banks?"

"Not banks. Banks have lawyers. Lawyers are much easier."

She laughs, a real laugh, surprised out of her. I make a mental note: Lorna's laugh comes out of her nose a little when she's genuinely surprised. Useful for later. Which is a terrible thing to notice, and I'm going to stop noticing it right now, and I'm going to listen to what she's actually saying.

What she's saying is that she runs a small bookshop in Kentish Town, and she's been trying to get the owner of the flower shop next door to stop parking his van across her loading bay. She's got a dog called Pickle who has eaten two library cards and a hat. She's been to Iceland. She wants to go again.

I'm nodding. I'm asking the right questions. I'm also building a model of the bookshop's back door in my head, a hinge that's probably original, a deadbolt I could open with a bent paperclip and a bit of patience, a camera above the till pointing at the wrong shelf. I hate this. It's like someone has tuned my nervous system to a frequency that only hums when I'm near danger.

"Are you okay?" she says.

"Yes. Sorry. Sorry. I was thinking about the flower van."

"You're thinking about the flower van? You're so weird." She grins. "I like it."

Okay. Okay. That's the actual human moment. You can have that one.

I sip my water and let it sit in my mouth. The restaurant is busy, the kitchen door swinging open and shut, a smell of garlic and something burning sweetly in a pan. A waiter with a ring through one eyebrow glides past with a tray of bread. Behind him, the window glass is fogged at the corners. Three men in the far booth are arguing about football in voices that carry, and one of them keeps tapping his phone face-up on the table, screen unlocked.

Four digits, one-one-one-one. No. Not a pattern. Just the same number four times, and an obvious one, because he's checking the time every thirty seconds and you can see the little fingerprint smudge from across the room.

Stop it.

"Can I ask you something?" Lorna says, leaning forward on her elbows. "Something a bit random."

"Of course."

"Do you play Magic? You had that card in your wallet earlier, the one with the dragon. I've been trying to figure out if you're a secret nerd."

I look down at my hand. Somehow, while I was looking at the flower van, I'd got the Ancestral Recall I keep in my wallet out of my pocket and onto the table. A little ruined, sleeve worn at the corners. My dad gave it to me when I was twelve.

"I'm a secret nerd," I say, and I'm already grinning. "I play once a month at the shop on Rathbone Street. Mostly Commander. I've got a black-and-green deck that's been losing badly for three years."

"Oh my God." Lorna sits back, delighted. "I play Izzet. I've been through every rulebook in the city. I've got a Blue Elemental Blast sleeved in a Pickle-proof case."

"Pickle is not allowed near your cards."

"Pickle has _opinions_ about my cards."

We're off. For forty minutes it's easy. We argue about whether Lightning Bolt is a fair card in a casual format, and whether anyone who plays Mana Drain should be allowed to sit at a table. She tells me about the time her friend got a Black Lotus in a pack and tried to sell it on eBay with a photo taken on a windowsill, and I tell her about the deck I built around a card that got banned six months after I finished it. For a few minutes, I forget to run the audit. I forget about the three men in the booth, and the phone, and the four-digit lock, and the fogged window that doesn't latch properly on the left.

Then the waiter brings the bill on a little wooden board, and Lorna reaches for her purse, and her phone falls out onto the table.

It's unlocked. Of course it is. She's been showing me photos of Pickle, a whole album of them, and the screen is still bright.

And the wallpaper is a photo of her and an older man at what looks like a wedding, both of them laughing, and the lock screen is a grid of nine dots, the cheap, traceable kind, with a visible smear across the bottom-left corner, where her thumb has been a thousand times.

I can see the pattern. I can see it the way you see a word in a foreign language once you've learned it. A hook shape in the top row, a dip into the middle, a long diagonal down to the right, then up, and a final flick. It's an L with a tail. A mistake a tired person makes at the end of a long day.

I say nothing. I look at the bill.

"Oh, don't mind that," she says cheerfully, scooping the phone up and putting it away. "It's just my dad's birthday. He's the one in the photo. He's always hated the lock screen. I keep meaning to change it."

"You should," I say.

And then, because I cannot help it, and because the sentence has already escaped from my mouth before the rest of me can grab it back: "You really should. That pattern's on the grease marks. Anyone could trace it from across a room in a bar. You'd be surprised how easy it is."

The table goes very quiet. Lorna's smile does not vanish. It just goes still, the way a cat's face goes still before a jump.

"Right," she says slowly. "Thanks for the security advice."

Oh God. I've done it. I've done the one thing you can't do on a first date, the one thing my ex used to say was the sure sign I'd never be able to switch off. I am, in her eyes, the man who's sitting across from her memorising her phone.

"I'm sorry," I say. "That came out wrong. I didn't mean to—it's a reflex. I can't not notice that kind of thing. It's like a tic."

"A tic."

"Yes."

"Like, you look at the back doors of restaurants and think about lock picks?"

"Sometimes. Usually. Yes."

She looks at me for a long moment, and I can see her weighing it: a joke, a warning, a man who's just outed himself as a paranoid weirdo, or a man who's trying very hard to be honest in a way that makes his palms sweat. Then she sighs and picks up her glass.

"Okay," she says. "I'll change the pattern. Tomorrow. And you're going to show me your deck, in person, next month, at the shop on Rathbone Street. And if you use one single bit of your scary skills on Pickle, I'll bite you."

"That's fair."

"And you're paying for dessert."

"That's fair too."

Outside, the rain starts, a soft grey curtain falling across the window. The three men in the booth finally stop arguing. The phone with the smudged screen goes face-down on the table.

I look at the back door, just once. The bolt is old. The hinge is original. The camera is pointed at the wrong shelf.

Then I look at Lorna, laughing at something I haven't said, and I think: there's nothing in this room I need to break.

It's the strangest thing I've ever felt. I don't know how long it'll last. But I order the chocolate fondant, and I don't check the exits once.

## 22. Overthinking It

Okay. Okay. Mr. Tanaka said we have to pick partners and I'm going to pick Jamie because Jamie is sitting by the window and the window is the only seat in the room where nobody can see your face while you're talking to them. Except that's not true, because the window reflects everything, and if I look at Jamie I'll see myself in the glass, which is worse. So I won't look at Jamie. I'll look at the periodic table on the wall, which is fine, because the periodic table is never going to ask me anything I didn't prepare for.

Jamie is already walking over. Oh no. Oh no, Jamie is walking over, and I haven't finished the sentence I was going to say.

I had three sentences. I had them in the bathroom this morning, in the mirror, while I was brushing my teeth and pretending not to hear Dad singing in the kitchen. Sentence one: "Do you want to do the volcano one or the water cycle one? I think the volcano one is better because it has more variables." Sentence two: "I can make the poster if you do the data table, I'm good at tables." Sentence three, the backup: "I don't mind either way, whatever you want." Three sentences. A little menu. I could pick from it and look like a normal person.

"Hey," Jamie says, sliding into the seat across from me. "Do you want to do the volcano one or the water cycle one?"

Which is exactly sentence one, which is so unfair I want to laugh, and which means I have to say sentence one back to them, out loud, with the right amount of enthusiasm.

"Volcano," I say.

It comes out as a sort of squeak. It's not a bad squeak. It's just a squeak. Jamie nods like it's a totally normal answer, which it is, and writes VOLCANO at the top of the blank page with a green gel pen, and I feel my face go hot in a way I cannot stop, and I think, very clearly, _you are a complete disaster, Mia Harper._

That's not fair. I'm not a disaster. I got a 96 on the last science test. I know what a magma chamber is. I could tell you the difference between a shield volcano and a stratovolcano with my eyes closed, if you asked me the right way, in a room where nobody was looking at me. I'm just bad at saying the good stuff out loud. Everything in my head comes out in a complete, crisp, grammatical order, and then it gets to my mouth and falls apart like wet paper.

Jamie is still writing. "So should we do the lava flow or the ash cloud first?"

Ash cloud is a trap. Ash cloud is the kind of question where a dumb person says "I don't know" and a smart person says "Lava flow, because it's the most visual," and I'm not sure if Jamie is testing me, or if Jamie is genuinely asking, or if Jamie is just a normal kid who wants to get the poster done before lunch. But my brain is already running the simulation: if I say ash cloud and Jamie laughs, I'll die. If I say lava flow and Jamie thinks I'm bossy, I'll also die. If I say "whichever you think," Jamie will think I don't care, and then I'll sit here doing nothing for twenty minutes while Jamie writes everything, and at the end Mr. Tanaka will say "Mia, did you contribute?" and I'll have to say "I did the table," and it'll be a lie, because I didn't do the table, I just watched Jamie do it.

"Ash cloud," I say, then immediately add, "or whatever you think is better, honestly, I'm fine either way."

Oh no. Oh no, I've said the backup sentence too, and now it's stuck to the front of the first one, like a sticker on a sticker, and Jamie is looking at me with a tiny confused frown that makes my stomach drop through the floor.

"Sure," Jamie says. "Ash cloud first. Cool."

_Cool._ They said cool. That's a good sign. I think it's a good sign. Cool is what you say when you're pleased. Or when you don't care and you're trying to be polite about it. Which one is it? I can't tell, and I'll never be able to tell, because the only way to know would be to ask, and I'm not going to ask, because asking is the thing that makes people think you're needy, and I'm not needy, I'm just —

Okay. Breathe.

I'm going to look at the window now. Just for a second. Just to reset.

My reflection is there in the glass, sitting very straight, my hands clamped together under the table, like a kid waiting outside the head teacher's office. Behind me, the classroom is full of noise: someone's laughing about a dog, the pencil sharpener grinding, two girls at the back whispering about a boy I don't know. And in the window, at the edge of the glass, I can see Ethan Cole, in the far corner, bent over his lab notebook. He's got his pen in his mouth. He keeps chewing on it when he thinks nobody's looking.

I've been trying not to think about Ethan for three weeks. That was the plan. Don't think about the way he holds his pencil. Don't think about how he always says "Thanks, Mrs. Ramsey" in a quiet voice when she hands back the tests. Don't think about the way he laughed, on the bus, at something I said that I still can't remember, and how I went home and replayed the laugh twenty-seven times before I fell asleep.

Jamie is talking again. "I'll do the chart, if you want to write the explanation? You're really good at explanations."

I look at Jamie. Properly, this time, with my eyes and not the reflection. A nice, tired, slightly grumpy face. A stain on the sleeve of their jumper that looks like ketchup. A kind of patient, hopeful expression, like they really do just want to get the poster done before lunch.

"Okay," I say.

It comes out as a whisper. But it's a real word, in a real voice, and Jamie grins, and it's over. The first thing is done. Jamie is already sketching a little volcano in the corner of the page, lopsided and enthusiastic, with arrows coming out of the top.

I pick up my pen. I try to write the first line of the explanation, and the words come out in perfect order, crisp and grammatical, the way they always do in my head. _Magma rises through the crust when the pressure in the chamber exceeds the strength of the surrounding rock._

I hold the pen very still.

Then, with a deep breath and no further consultation with my inner committee, I write it down. Every word. And when Jamie leans over to read it and says, "Oh, that's really good," I say "thanks" before my brain has a chance to tell me it's embarrassing.

Across the room, Ethan Cole looks up from his notebook. He catches my eye through the gap between two pairs of shoulders, and for a second, he doesn't look away.

I don't look away either.

Then the bell goes, and everyone stands, and I've got eleven seconds to decide whether that counted as eye contact or just the window reflecting badly, and I don't know, I really don't know, and I'm going to spend all afternoon overthinking it.

## 23. Rehearsing Rejection

Okay. She's going to be late, and she's going to say it's the Tube, and that's fine, that's a reasonable thing to say, people are late on the Tube all the time. She'll come up the stairs of the café with her coat half off and say "sorry sorry sorry" and I'll say it's fine, and she'll say it's not, and then she'll sit down across from me and I'll see her face and I'll know.

I'll know what?

Nothing. I'll know nothing. That's the thing. I'll look at her face, which is what I always do, and it'll be the same face that's been kind to me for six years, and I'll tell myself it's a different face, because that's what I do.

Rehearse it. Fine. If she says "We need to talk," I'll say, "Oh god, is it the thing about the flat?" and she'll say, "No, it's not the flat," and then I'll say, "I know, I know, I've been a lot lately," and she'll say, "You haven't been a lot," and I'll say, "I have, Nina, you're just too kind to say," and she'll laugh the small laugh, the one that means she's tired of me, and then she'll say "I think we should slow down," and I'll say, "I'm fine with that," which I won't be, and I'll go home and stand in the shower until the water runs cold.

She has a job with actual deadlines and people who depend on her and I have a job where I answer emails from people who don't know what they want, and I'm sure she's going to tell me about something terrible that happened at the office, something with a client or a boss, and I'll listen and say all the right things, and then she'll say, "Anyway, how's your week?" and I'll say, "Fine," because the truth is I spent Thursday deciding whether to delete her number, and I didn't, and I'm still angry at myself about that.

## 24. Blood Debt

The Hall of Thirteen Bells had not rung in forty years. Its roof had fallen in the spring of the great flood, and now the rain came through the broken tiles and pooled between the flagstones in dark mirrors. The bronze bells hung from the rafters at irregular heights, green with age, and the crowd had hung their lanterns from the bell-ropes, so the whole hall flickered like the inside of a lantern itself.

Mori Hayato stood at the north end, beneath the largest bell, with his feet set shoulder-width on the cracked stone. His heels were turned slightly out, the knees soft, his weight low and centred. He wore the black hakama of the Jigen-ryū, the cord of his sword belt tied in the old double knot. The scabbard was empty. There would be no blade tonight. There was a rule against it, and the rule had never once been broken in this hall, because the men who came here to die did not come to be trusted with steel.

Across the hall, Wei Lan stood at the south end, under the smallest bell. She had her back to a pillar, one foot forward, the other planted loose behind her, her hands held low and open in front of her hips. She wore a sleeveless grey tunic and cloth shoes with the soles worn through at the ball of the foot. Her left wrist was wrapped in red cord. She had not looked at him once since she entered.

Between them, the crowd pressed in along the tiered stone steps that had once held pilgrims. Men with coin purses in their fists. Women with their hair pinned up, shouting numbers. Somebody near the front was selling lotus seeds. The master of ceremonies stood on a broken plinth and raised a hand.

"Two hundred on the Jigen-ryū. Three hundred on the Pigua. Nobody leaves until the bell rings twice."

Mori had no interest in the wagers. He was watching her feet.

The bell rang once. The sound rolled down through the hall and the crowd fell silent.

Mori moved first, as the Jigen-ryū always did. He dropped into a low crouch and drove forward, his right foot sliding across the wet stone with a sound like a blade drawn from a sheath. His hand went to his hip, the empty scabbard forgotten, and the draw came in a flat horizontal arc at the height of Wei Lan's ribs. It was a feint. He had never needed the blade. The arc was meant to make her step back.

She did not step back. She stepped sideways, to her right, out of the line of the arc, and her left arm came up and around in a loose loop, the red cord catching the lantern light as it went. It was not a punch. It was a whip, the forearm snapping outward from the elbow, the wrist a hinge that let the tip of the motion travel faster than the body. The cord cracked against the side of his neck, a sharp sting that made him blink.

_Piguaquan,_ he thought. _Not a straight line in the whole art._

He spun on his heel, his left foot pivoting to bring his hips round, and came in with a rising strike aimed at the place under her arm. The Jigen-ryū strike was short and hard, the fist travelling no more than a hand's width before it stopped dead. Wei Lan dropped her weight, let his knuckles pass a finger's breadth above her ear, and stepped inside the arc of his arm. She was close enough now that he could smell the rain on her tunic and the sharp bitterness of ginger on her breath.

Her right palm struck the centre of his chest, not hard enough to break anything, but hard enough to make him take a step back. He recovered at once, rolling his shoulders and dropping his guard, and she was already moving. She circled to his left, toward the pillar, her steps quick and light, the grey tunic flaring as she turned. Every step put her at a new angle. He turned to follow, his feet sliding, keeping his centre between her and the bell.

The crowd made a noise like a wave breaking.

Mori understood then what the masters had meant. The Jigen-ryū taught that a fight was a single decisive moment, the blade drawn and the enemy already dead before he knew he had been cut. But this was not one moment. It was a hundred small ones. She did not block his attacks. She let them pass, a hand's width away, and each time she passed she left a little of herself behind: a fingertip against his wrist, a flick of the cord across his knuckles, a heel driven into the back of his knee, light enough that he could not be sure which was the real strike.

He tried to close the distance and set his feet, to bring his weight down into a straight thrust that would end it. She was not there. She had dropped, sweeping her leg in a low arc across the wet stone. He stepped over it. She rose beneath his arm, her palm slapping up under his chin, snapping his head back. The lanterns swung overhead. The bell above him gave a faint, dull hum as his shoulder brushed its lip.

For a moment they stood almost chest to chest, his left hand gripping her right wrist, her left elbow pressed into the soft spot under his ribs. Neither of them moved. The crowd went silent again.

"Your master taught you to be patient," Wei Lan said quietly.

"My master taught me to finish things."

"Mine taught me the opposite." She twisted free. Her red-wrapped wrist came around in a last, flat whip that caught him across the forearm. He grunted. She stepped back, two paces, and lowered her hands again.

Mori breathed in. Breathed out. He let the empty scabbard drop to his side, a gesture no one in the hall had seen in forty years. Then he went low and fast, straight at her, his right hand driving forward in the one clean line the Jigen-ryū had kept since the days of the samurai clans.

She met him with her open palm, not her fist. The heel of her hand struck his wrist, deflecting the line by a finger's width. Her other hand came up under his elbow. In that instant his centre of gravity went out of him, and he was falling forward, the stone rushing toward his face.

He caught himself on one knee. His palm slapped flat against the wet flagstone, rainwater splashing up into his eyes.

When he looked up, she was standing a pace away, her hands still low and open, her chest rising and falling slowly. She did not smile. She simply waited.

The second bell rang, cracked and tuneless, from somewhere in the rafters.

Mori got to his feet. His legs shook, and he refused to let it show.

"Three hundred on the Pigua," shouted the master of ceremonies, into a roar that drowned out the rest of his words.

Wei Lan inclined her head, the smallest bow, and Mori returned it. Neither of them would ever say the other had won. Neither of them would tell anyone afterward how close it had been, or how many times she had let his strike pass by because she could.

## 25. Breaking Formation

Eight counts. Five, six, seven, eight. That's the first thing Director Han says to me, before I even have my shoes off: "Five, six, seven, eight, and your hips are a full beat early. Again."

I'm standing in Studio Four at Haneul Academy with my hands on my knees, sweat already running into my eyes, and a mirror wall that goes all the way up to a ceiling so high it might be a church. Nineteen other trainees are lined up in four rows of five, every one of them in identical black leggings and white tanks, every one of them breathing through their noses the way they've been taught. I'm in the back corner, third row from the wall, the girl from Newark with the Nike cross-trainers and the Bluetooth speaker in her gym bag.

Back home, my crew called me Dani Pop. Popping, locking, a little waacking when we got bored. We learned in the gym behind the bodega on Clinton Avenue, on a warped floor that tilted toward the drain. Nobody counted anything. You listened to the bass and you let your body say what it wanted, and if you were good, people nodded. That was the whole curriculum.

Here, the music is a bright, sharp synth track that has been rehearsed into the floorboards for months. The choreography is called "Lucid Heat," and it is sixty-four counts long, with a formation change every eight. There is a name for every movement, and every name is in Korean, and the translator the academy gave me has been helpful only in the way that a stop sign is helpful.

"Dani," Han says. He doesn't look at me. He looks at the mirror, where all of us are reflected in a grid of black and white. "Your arm on the third eight. Show me."

I do it. The move is a sharp arm roll, the wrist snapping out from the elbow, the shoulder dropping, the whole arm a clean line from fingertip to collarbone. In the street, I'd add a tiny pop at the end, a little stutter in the wrist, so the arm ends up looking like it got hit by the beat. It's a signature. People at home would clap for that.

"No," Han says. "Again. Exactly as the count."

I do it again, exactly as the count. It feels like wearing somebody else's skin.

The track loops. Five, six, seven, eight. We move left in one line, two steps, a hitch, a sharp turn that lands us all facing the wall. Our arms snap up together, the fingers flexed, the palms out. Nineteen bodies in a single breath. It is beautiful, I'll admit it. It's like watching a flock of birds that's been told exactly where to land.

Then the bridge. The bridge is my problem. Four bars of half-time, slow and heavy, and the choreography drops into a floor sequence that's meant to look loose and wild. Han wants everyone to snap into a sharp, mechanical freeze on the downbeat, then flow into a slow roll. But I can feel my body wanting to fall into the groove, my knees bending deeper, my weight rolling down through my hips, the way it did when the bass was heavy in the gym and the room was full of people yelling.

My left hand lifts on its own. A little ripple travels up my forearm, from wrist to elbow, a wave with nowhere to go. Han's head snaps round.

"Dani."

"Sorry."

"Stop." He walks down the line, heels clicking. He stops in front of me, close enough that I can see the fine lines around his eyes, the cold patience in them. "You have talent. Real talent. I saw your audition tape. Your body knows the music in a way I cannot teach. But this is not the street. This is a group. You are not one dancer. You are one-twentieth of a machine, and the machine does not need your soul. It needs your precision."

"I can be precise."

"Can you?" He tilts his head. "Your attitude says otherwise. You think the rules are for other people."

"The rules are fine. I just think the dance should breathe."

Somebody near the front snickers. Han doesn't react. "Breathing is for after you have earned the right to be in the front row," he says. "Five, six, seven, eight. Again."

I want to walk out. My bag is by the door. My bus ticket is in my pocket. My mother is in Newark, working a double shift at the hospital, and she doesn't know that I have been practising in the hallway after lights out, or that I have a bruise on my shin from the corner of the bunk bed because I can't stop moving in my sleep.

The track starts again. The formation rises. I'm in position, back row, third from the left. I do the first sequence exactly as the count. Five, six, seven, eight. Left, two steps, hitch, turn. My body is so tight I can hear the joints clicking.

Then the bridge comes, and my muscle memory betrays me.

My knees drop. My hips roll. My weight falls through the floor into a loose, low pop, the kind that comes when the bass hits so hard you forget you have a name. My arms begin to snap, and the wrist catches the beat and flicks, and there it is: the old stutter, the one that came from the gym floor on Clinton Avenue.

I stop. I don't mean to. My body just stops, leaving the rest of the line to drift past me, nineteen trainees in a row turning their heads as one, and in the mirror I see myself standing alone in the back corner, sweating, my arms still half-curled, the wave still running out through my fingers.

Han doesn't shout. He just says, very quietly, "Again."

I think about the two options. The first is simple. Shut my mouth, lock my wrists, fit the shape. Be a piece of the machine. Maybe I'll get the front row in a year, maybe I'll be on a stage in Seoul with lights and a camera crew and my mother's face in the crowd on a screen somewhere. Maybe I'll stop being the girl who pops, and start being someone who simply belongs.

The second is to keep the stutter. Let the wrist catch the beat. Let the bridge breathe the way it wants to. I'll likely be out of the academy by the end of the month, back on the bus to Newark with my bag over my shoulder and the bruise on my shin, and nobody will ask me what I was trying to prove.

The track loops. Five, six, seven, eight.

I take a breath. I think about Dani Pop on a tilted floor behind a bodega, nobody counting, nobody watching, nothing but the bass and the blood in my ears.

Then I drop into the bridge, and I let my hips go, and I catch the beat with my wrist, one clean stutter at the end of the third eight, exactly where it doesn't belong.

Han's eyes meet mine in the mirror. He doesn't stop the music. He just watches, and something in his jaw tightens, and I can't tell whether it's anger or something else.

"Again," he says.

I do it again.

## 26. The Court of the Dreaming

The Court sat in a room with no ceiling, which was the sort of thing Dream found tiresome. Above the dais, the stars hung in their wrong constellations, and the floor was a sheet of black glass in which everyone who entered saw their own faces, slightly older than they remembered.

"Case number four thousand, one hundred and six," said the clerk, a grey and dusty figure with a quill for a tongue. "The Dreamscape of Max, a schoolboy of the Midlands, currently contested by First Love and by Mora, of the Slavic Hollows."

Dream sat on the throne of black stone and did not blink. His face, pale and long, was a mask that had been very carefully made to look like nothing at all. Inside it, he was thinking about his lunch, which had been a small bowl of cold barley and an apology from a nightingale.

"Who brings the complaint?" he asked.

First Love stepped forward. It was small, pink-cheeked, slightly bashful, with the face of a boy who had just been handed a sealed envelope and told it was for him. Its hands were clasped around a bunch of wildflowers that drooped a little under its grip.

"Me, sir," it said. "I built that dream. Over eleven months. It was a lovely dream. The field was yellow, and the sky was the right kind of blue, and there was a girl named Hannah who always laughed at the same joke. Max went there every night. It was the most comfortable place in his whole week."

"And now?"

"Now there's a monster in it."

Mora stepped forward, lazily, as if walking down a stair she had walked a thousand times. She was tall and draped in a grey shawl, her hair long and dark and loose, and her eyes were the colour of a clouded sky. She was beautiful in the way a thunderhead is beautiful, from a safe distance.

"I'm not a monster," Mora said. "I'm a _tenant_."

"You broke into the wrong room," said Dream.

"I broke into a room that had no lock and a wallpaper full of pastel nonsense. The boy was lying in the field, waiting for something to happen. Something was going to happen. I simply made sure it happened to him."

"You sat on his chest."

"I sat on his chest, yes. That is my nature. He is quite a frail child, sir. He needs building up."

"You fed on his breath."

Mora spread her hands. "A girl's gotta eat, too, you know?"

The room was quiet for a long moment. A faint sound of rustling came from the back row, where a flock of bored dreams were watching the proceedings through a crack in the wall.

"I want to be very clear," said Dream, "that this is a dispute about who owns a dreamscape. It is not a matter of dinner."

"With respect, my lord," said Mora, "everything's a matter of dinner."

Dream tapped one finger against the arm of the throne, once, with the slow precision of a clock striking an unfortunate hour. "The dream belongs to First Love," he said. "It was built by its hand. It was given to the boy, and the boy has been living in it, and that is the end of the matter."

"And the boy's fear?" Mora asked, sweetly.

"The boy's fear is his own."

"Then he won't mind if I visit it once in a while, will he? Just a little light visiting."

"He will mind."

"Does he _know_ that?"

First Love made a small, despairing sound. "She's making a mess of my lovely field. Look at the wildflowers. They've all gone dead."

Dream did look. For a second something moved across his mask, a faint, unmistakable irritation, like a man who has been asked to referee a dispute between two cats over a cushion, and has discovered that both cats have solicitors.

"Stop it," he said, to no one in particular.

"Stop what?" said Mora.

"Stop being clever about the wildflowers." He leaned forward. For the first time in the session, his voice went down into a register that made the glass floor ripple, and every face in the room flickered and went briefly, painfully young. "Mora. You may not sit on a child's chest again. You may not feed on his breath. You may not so much as look at his dream through the keyhole on a Tuesday."

"And if I do?"

"Then I shall give your next nightmare to a very polite man with an umbrella, who has been waiting two hundred years for something to do."

Mora's mouth twitched. "You wouldn't."

"I would be delighted."

"Fine," said Mora. "I'll sit on someone else. The grandmother in Hull has been asking for it."

"That is not an improvement."

"It's a lot of breath, though."

Dream sighed, the faintest sound, like a page turning in an empty library. He looked at First Love, whose wildflowers were still drooping. He looked at Mora, who was smiling the smile of a creature that had never once been told no and had enjoyed the experience very much.

"First Love," he said. "The field is yours. Replant what you can. Build a fence, if you must. Put a hedge around it. Put up a sign that says _Keep Out_."

"That'll make the boy curious," said Mora.

"That's the trouble with sign-making," said Dream, not looking at her. "Everyone reads them."

He rose, and the stars in the ceiling-less room shifted into their correct places, and the glass floor cleared. The clerk bowed. The dusty dreams in the back row began to file out, muttering to one another about the Hull grandmother and her cats.

Dream paused at the edge of the dais. He looked back once, over his shoulder, at Mora.

"And Mora," he said. "Next time you want to eat, go to the chippy."

"They don't do breath in the chippy."

"No," said Dream, and something small and dangerous flickered in his eyes. "But they do give very generous portions of vinegar."

## 27. Blood Moon

The moon over the Carpathian foothills was the colour of a bruise, and Yennefer had been watching it for an hour before the vampire arrived.

She stood on the lip of a ruined chapel, its roof gone, its altar a heap of blackened stone. Wild roses had grown up through the cracks. The air smelled of lilac and gooseberries, which was her doing, a small warding charm she had woven into her hair for the occasion, since she had not expected to need a perfume that could mask the taste of fear.

"You're late," she said, without turning.

"I'm early." The voice came from the shadows behind the altar, lazy and amused, with a crackle beneath it like a fire turning over in the grate. "You simply expected me to be later."

Yennefer turned. He was leaning against a fallen pillar with his arms folded, a long red coat hanging open over a black shirt, a wide-brimmed hat tipped low over his eyes. His sunglasses were a deep, poisonous orange, and when he lifted his head, they caught the moonlight and held it.

"Alucard," she said.

"Miss Vengerberg." He tilted his head. "Yennefer, I think, if we're being friendly. And I'm told you are the most powerful sorceress in the northern kingdoms, which is a title I would have assumed belonged to someone with a beard."

"You've been asking about me."

"I've been asking about everyone. It's a habit of mine. Old things like to know who's left."

She took a step forward. The roses at her feet curled inward, leaves blackening at the edges, as though a frost had come and gone. Alucard watched them die with the mild interest of a man observing rain.

"Do you know what I am?" she asked.

"A witch who was once a hunchback and is now not. A woman who has paid a great price to be beautiful, and who still wakes at night to check her spine. A creature who cannot bear children, because the magic that made her lovely ate the part of her that could." He smiled, a flash of white. "You're a very complicated little sorceress, Yennefer. I find you rather delicious."

Her palm lit. A bright thread of blue power ran across the fingers, crackling and humming, and she held it there between them like a question.

"Don't," she said. "You'll get burned."

"I expect I shall. That is the entire point." He did not move. "You have a choice, you see. You can destroy me with that charming little spark of yours, and I will regenerate, probably annoyed, and then you'll have made an enemy of the Hellsing Organisation, who would not be pleased. Or you can talk to me for a while. I'm very good company when I'm not hungry."

"Are you hungry?"

"Always."

"Then you'll have to make do with the roses."

He laughed, low and rolling, the sound of gravel shifting under a heavy wheel. "I've been given worse offers."

She let the spark die, slowly, the way a dying fire settles to embers. He watched it go with something that might have been disappointment.

"Why did you come here?" she asked.

"To find a worthy opponent."

"Is that what you're looking for? An opponent?"

"I'm looking for someone who can end me, or at least make me bleed." He pushed his hat back, and his eyes, behind the orange lenses, were suddenly very bright. "Most of my kind are cowards. They feed and they hide and they tell themselves they are gods. I despise them. I've slaughtered whole nests of them for the simple pleasure of watching them die begging to be something else."

"And you think I'm different."

"I think you're _afraid_, and you hide it under a very good glamour." He stepped away from the pillar at last, closing the distance between them in a single unhurried stride. "Which makes you the first interesting thing I've met in four hundred years."

She did not step back. "I am not afraid of you."

"No? Your heart's beating very fast."

"That's the wine."

"You haven't had any wine."

"It's the moon."

He laughed again, and it was almost gentle this time, the laugh of something very old that had forgotten how to be kind. "I am the moon's favourite child, Yennefer. Don't lie to me. I can hear every drop of blood under your skin."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The wind moved through the broken roof, and the roses shivered, and the blood moon hung above them, huge and hungry and red.

Yennefer lifted her chin. "You talk too much," she said. "For a monster."

"And you talk too little, for a witch." He was close enough now that she could see the faint silver scars at his throat, the ones that had already begun to knit back into smooth, pale skin. "We both have faults, my dear. Shall we see whose is more amusing?"

"Name your terms."

"Ask me a question. Any question you like. I'll answer it truthfully. Then you answer one of mine. And if either of us lies, the other is permitted to bite."

"Bite?"

"With teeth, Yennefer. What did you think I meant?"

She considered this. The moon was watching. The lilac scent of her warding charm was fading, and beneath it she could smell the iron in the air and the cold, clean metal of his guns, holstered at his hips beneath the coat.

"Why do you wear sunglasses at night?" she asked.

Alucard looked at her for a long, long time. Then he took them off, slowly, and the blood moon shone straight into his eyes, and they were not what she had expected. They were tired, and wide, and very, very lonely.

"Because I don't want to see the moon when I'm in the mood to kill it," he said quietly. "Your turn."

## 28. Prison Strings

Jolyne Cujoh doesn't trust the woman with the auburn hair, and the woman with the auburn hair has been smiling at her for eleven minutes straight.

"You must be Jolyne," says Makima, pleasantly, from the far side of the visiting table. Her hands are folded neatly on the steel. Her yellow eyes are soft. "Welcome to Hakodate Correctional. I'm the Deputy Warden's assistant. I'm told you're very strong."

"I'm told you're very creepy," Jolyne says. "Doesn't mean I'm going to let you touch me."

"Oh, I wouldn't dream of it." Makima tilts her head. "Though I'd love to know what you're thinking."

Jolyne's handcuffs clink against the table. Her wrists are rubbed raw from the transfer, and the ankle cuffs are cold. The butterfly tattoo on her arm is smudged from three days without a proper wash, and her two buns are half collapsed, the stray strands sticking to her cheeks. She looks at the scar running across the back of Makima's left hand, a thin white line that doesn't quite match the smooth skin around it, and says nothing.

Stone Free isn't here. It's the one thing that's gone wrong about this place. The guards took her Stand's tether out of the transfer bag at the gate, a bright metal leash the size of a bootlace, and locked it in a box in a room she isn't allowed to enter. Without it, the string inside her body stays coiled up tight, and she can feel the tension of it sitting in her belly like a hook.

"Your Stand," Makima says, reading her face with uncanny ease. "Stone Free. It's a beautiful name. Very honest."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't talk about it like you know anything about it."

Makima laughs softly, a bright, musical sound that makes the guard by the door shift his weight. "I know a great deal about it, Jolyne. I know it unravels. I know it can pull a thread from your own skin and tie a man's throat in a knot with it. I know that when it's scared, it starts to hum."

Jolyne's fingers tighten on the edge of the table. She can feel the hum now, very faint, in the bones of her wrists. Stone Free is in the box, but it isn't asleep.

"You're a Stand user," she says flatly.

"No." Makima smiles. "I have something better. A contract."

"With who?"

"With a man who runs this prison. Mr. Ackerman, the Prime Minister's secretary, who is also my dog." She says it as lightly as if she's talking about the weather. "He has a very generous appetite for pretty things, and he's always been ready to pay for the privilege of being owned. It's a lovely arrangement. Everyone gets what they want."

"Except the people he's selling."

"Oh, Jolyne." Makima's eyes go soft and sad, a kind of wistful pity that makes Jolyne want to put her fist through the wall. "You think you're the only one who's been framed. You think the world is fair, and somebody made a mistake with your file. But the mistake was never a mistake, was it? Somebody wanted you here. And now here you are."

The guard by the door clears his throat. The fluorescent light above the table buzzes.

"Who framed me?" Jolyne says, quietly.

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

"Then you'll have to earn the answer." Makima leans forward. Her voice drops to a whisper that seems to fill the entire room. "I'm going to make you an offer. A deal. Tell me one thing you've never told another living soul, and I'll tell you the name of the man who put you in that cell."

"No."

"Are you sure? Think about your father. He always had a clever way of getting out of trouble, didn't he? And yet he couldn't save his own daughter from a box with a lock on it."

Jolyne's hands close into fists. The hum in her wrists rises, a faint crackle under the skin, the first thread of Stone Free stirring at the edge of her body where it's been folded away. She feels it pressing against her ribs, curious, angry.

"Don't you talk about my father," she says, very low.

"Ah." Makima's eyes flicker, bright and interested. "There it is. The temper. The fire underneath. You're so like him, Jolyne. Lovely to look at, and utterly without restraint."

The string breaks through.

It doesn't tear. It _pours_, a bright silver thread snaking out from the back of Jolyne's hand, looping over the steel table, slithering across the floor toward Makima's chair with a faint, eager whisper. The guard shouts. Somewhere down the corridor, a door bangs open and footsteps come running.

Makima does not move. She watches the thread curl up over her shoes, then over her ankle, and she smiles.

"Oh, that's lovely," she says softly. "Go on. Show me the rest."

The thread stops an inch from her throat.

It stays there, trembling in the air, a single bright quivering filament. Jolyne can feel its tension, its eagerness, and beneath it something else: a cold, precise intelligence threaded through the string like a needle through silk. Her own thread is being held. Gently. Not by force. By invitation.

"You feel that?" Makima murmurs. "That's me. I'm holding your string, Jolyne. Not tight. Just enough to know where it goes. And I'd like very much to know where it goes."

"You're a Devil," Jolyne breathes.

"I'm a contract, dear. I'm whatever you agree I am." Makima's eyes don't leave Jolyne's face. "You're not a bad person. I can see that. You're loyal. You love the people who love you. Shall we see how long that lasts when the rest of your friends are on the other side of the glass?"

The door bangs open. Three guards pour through it, batons up, shouting. Jolyne's body twists, the string snapping back into her skin with a sick, stinging pull, and she falls back against the chair, gasping.

Makima stands, slowly, smoothing her skirt. She looks down at Jolyne with genuine warmth, the way a teacher looks at a clever but difficult student.

"We'll talk again," she says. "I'll be the one who keeps the door open. Try not to hurt yourself on the way out."

She walks past the guards without a glance, and they part for her like grass in the wind.

Jolyne sits in the cuffs, her heart hammering, the string coiled tight and humming in the cage of her ribs. She stares at the empty chair, at the faint warm mark where Makima's hand rested on the table.

_Dogs are supposed to die for their owners,_ she thinks, and she has no idea who said it, or whether it was a threat, or a promise, or a warning she should have heard earlier.

But she knows, with a cold clarity that makes her stomach twist, that the woman with the auburn hair does not intend to let her leave this place alive, unless she chooses to become something she can no longer call her own.

## 29. Containment Notes, Recovered

**Foundation Incident Report SCP-████-A, Addendum 14**  
_Filed by: Dr. Emmeline Vos, Senior Researcher, Site-19_

I found the first note in the pocket of my own lab coat, folded into a neat square, on a Tuesday morning in March. The coat had been hanging in my locker for two days. I had not worn it since the Friday before, when I'd taken it off to go to the staff bar and forgotten about it.

The note was in my handwriting. I'm certain of that. I know my own loops, the slightly backward slant on the lowercase g, the way I cross the t's on the left before I go back to the right. The paper was Foundation-issue, the pale grey stock we use for field logs. I checked the watermark under the lamp. It was genuine.

It said:

_Don't open the east door on Thursday. Tell Hollis it's the coil. It isn't the coil. You will want to argue and you must not argue. Destroy this._

I didn't destroy it. I put it in a sealed bag and took it to Dr. Hollis, who read it twice, then looked at me with an expression I've come to recognise over the years as "pity dressed as professionalism."

"Emmeline," he said, "did you write this?"

"I don't remember writing it."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know. I'm answering the question I can."

* * *

_Note recovered from the pocket of Dr. Vos's lab coat, 3 days later:_

_They're going to say you're mad. You are not mad. You're tired, and you've been sleeping in the archive, and the lights in the east corridor flicker because someone has been playing with the power regulators. The coil is a lie. Do not let Hollis take the samples. There's something in the samples._

* * *

_Note found beneath the dissecting microscope in Lab 3, Thursday morning, folded very small:_

_It happened on the 14th. Yes, the 14th. I know because I saw the calendar on the wall of the canteen and it's already the 16th and nothing has changed. Nothing will change. You will lose the east door. Four people will go through it. One will come back out, and it won't be them._

_I'm sorry about Ruiz._

* * *

The Foundation's first hypothesis, offered during the Monday review by Dr. Ngata of the Temporal Anomalies Division, was that these notes represented a genuine case of retrocausal communication, with my future self reaching back through some currently unknown mechanism to warn me of an event that had not yet occurred.

The second hypothesis, offered by Director Harrow, was that SCP-████-B, the contained entity we have been studying for three years in a sealed chamber in Sub-Level Four, had found a way to influence my handwriting and my thoughts through the air ducts. SCP-████-B is a mimetic entity. It is very good at copying things. It has, in the past, duplicated the voices of guards and the faces of dead researchers. Its containment protocols are the most rigorous on the site.

The third hypothesis, which nobody said aloud at the review but which I could see in every face around the table, was that I was losing my mind.

I have spent the last two weeks reading my own notes. My entries from the previous month are scrupulously accurate, and their tone is calm and methodical. Then, on the twelfth of March, they change. My handwriting grows looser. My sentences become shorter. Some of the timestamps are wrong, hour and minute entered in a rush, and a few pages carry the faint smear of a thumb that is not mine. I cannot tell whether I made those entries or whether I simply don't remember making them.

* * *

_Note, handwritten, found in the filing cabinet of Dr. Hollis's office. Dated 17 March, not in my hand. Hollis insists it isn't in his either._

_Stop reading the logs. Go and stand in the courtyard at noon. Don't look up. Count the birds. There will be eleven._

I went to the courtyard at noon. I didn't look up. I counted the birds. There were eleven.

I did not tell Hollis. I'm not sure why. Perhaps because I didn't want him to know how close I'd come to believing.

* * *

On the afternoon of the twentieth, the east door alarm sounded.

It was not a containment breach. Containment had held for the whole morning. It was a false positive triggered by a maintenance tech who had left a wrench on the sensor plate, and by the time security reached the corridor it had already stopped ringing. Nobody was hurt. The door did not open.

But I was standing in the east corridor when the alarm went off, because I had walked there without quite knowing why. And as the sirens died, I looked down at the floor, and saw a sheet of grey Foundation paper lying face-up beside the drain. It had not been there a moment before.

_They're in the vents now. Four of them. Three are asleep and one is walking. The one walking is you. Don't let her find you. She'll tell you it's a kindness._

The handwriting was mine. I'm sure of it. And the ink was still wet.

* * *

I have been placed on administrative leave pending psychiatric review. Dr. Hollis has been kind about it, in the way that people are kind to someone they've already decided is ill. I have been given a room on the third floor with a window that does not open, and a bed that is slightly too short for me, and a set of medical forms that ask whether I have experienced any "perceptual disturbances" in the past thirty days.

I answered yes to all of them. I'm not sure whether that was honest.

The notes keep coming. I find them in my room, tucked under the pillow, slipped inside my shoe, folded into my cup of tea. Sometimes they warn me of things that are about to happen, and the things do happen. Sometimes they warn me of things that don't. Last week one of them told me that Hollis would be dead by Friday. He is still alive. He brought me a cup of tea this morning and asked whether I was sleeping, and I said no, and he looked at me with exactly the same expression he had worn when I first showed him the coat.

Every note is in my handwriting. Every note is written by someone who knows things I don't.

I have started to wonder, quietly, late at night, which of us is the containment, and which of us is the thing being contained.

Last night I found a new note on the windowsill. It was folded very small, like the first one.

_You're going to want to believe this is the easy version. It isn't. Stop writing the report. Keep the pen. You'll need it when the door opens._

I looked at it for a long time. Then I went to my desk, and picked up the pen, and started, very carefully, in my own neat hand, to write the next line.

## 30. Archive of the Unmarked Doors

**SCP-████ — "The Quiet Stacks"**  
_Object Class: Euclid (provisional). Exploration Log, Team Halcyon-7, Recovered Materials Excerpts._

The doors began appearing in late 2019, and by the time the first survey team was sent to investigate, there were nine hundred and forty-two of them on record. They were all identical: plain, unpainted timber, six feet high, set into whatever structure happened to be standing where they appeared. Abandoned warehouses in Ohio. A derelict school in Lagos. The back wall of a shuttered cinema in Lyon. Each one had a brass handle, cold to the touch, and no keyhole. Each one opened, if you pulled it, onto the same thing.

The archive.

Team Halcyon-7 went through the door at Site 47-C, a flooded hydroelectric plant in northern Quebec, on the morning of 14 June. The team consisted of six members: Dr. Imran Teague (Lead), Dr. Solveig Marr (Archivist), Agent Dana Okafor (Security), and three technicians. The following excerpts are drawn from the team's recovered logs.

* * *

**Exploration Log, Entry 1 (Teague):**

The space beyond the door is not a room. It is an interior that appears to have been designed for an institution that never intended to be seen. Shelving rises in long, receding rows, tiered to a height at which the top shelves become indistinct, and the rows continue further than the light goes. The air is cool, dry, and smells of paper and something metallic that I have not yet identified.

The shelves are full. We have counted fourteen rows before we stopped counting. Each shelf holds boxes, cloth-bound ledgers, reels of film in tin cases, and loose folders tied with faded red tape.

Dr. Marr has opened the nearest folder. She reports that the documents within are written in a language she describes as "a dialect of Old Norse that has been very heavily reconstructed," and that the diagrams accompanying the text show a city built in terraces down the sides of a single enormous crater.

* * *

**Recovered Document Excerpt A (translated by Dr. Marr):**

_...the Resonance Failure of the eleventh year was not a war, as the chroniclers say, but a correction. The Concord had grown too loud. Every mind in the terraces was singing the same note, and the note was hungry. The Stacks were sealed so that the record would survive what the living could not. We do not know who sealed them. We know only that the seal is weakening._

* * *

**Exploration Log, Entry 3 (Okafor):**

We found personnel files.

They are in a section marked, in the same red tape, with a symbol that resembles a stylised eye. Dr. Marr identified the symbol as the logo of the Harmon Foundation, a pre-war archival trust. I believe it is the same symbol we use on our internal clearance stamps, though the lines are slightly thinner and the eye has four pupils.

The files are dated between 1871 and 1894. The first name in the box is _Teague, Henry Imran._ Dr. Teague has read it aloud, and his voice was perfectly steady throughout, though he went very pale. The second is _Marr, Solveig Anne._ The third is _Okafor, Dana Ruth._

All three files contain photographs. In each, the subject is wearing clothes I recognise from our own staff roster. The dates on the back of the photos are in our current calendar.

I have not told the others about the ones beneath, which are dated 1812.

* * *

**Exploration Log, Entry 6 (Teague):**

We encountered a crew working in the far stacks.

They wore pale grey coverall suits with sealed hoods and carried shrink-wrap bags, a stack of blank archival boxes, and what appeared to be a Foundation-issue containment spray canister, though the markings were in a script I did not recognise. They were lifting a section of shelving down from its brackets. As the shelving came away, the rows behind it went dark, as though the lamplight could no longer reach them.

We called out to them. They turned, all at once, and fled. They did not run in the manner of frightened people. They moved with the disciplined, coordinated speed of a team that had rehearsed the withdrawal many times, and they were gone between two shelves before our flashlights could follow.

Agent Okafor pursued them for four hundred metres. She reports that the far stacks are now simply dark. Her light did not reach the end of the passage. She returned without having found any of them, and with a small grey tin case, which she has placed in the evidence crate without opening.

* * *

**Recovered Document Excerpt B (found in tin case, handwritten in the Foundation's standard log format):**

_Archive Subsection Q-9 removed per Directive 14. Subsection contained references to the Concord, the Resonance, and the seal. Removal proceeding on schedule. Note to self: check the personnel files before the team arrives. The team arrives in June. You will be reading this in June. You will not like what you find._

_Authorised by: O5-3._

* * *

**Exploration Log, Entry 9 (Marr):**

I have located a reading room at the centre of the archive. It is a circular chamber lit by a single hanging lamp, with a long oak table at its centre and twelve chairs drawn up around it. Eleven of the chairs are pushed in neatly. The twelfth has been pulled out, and the table in front of it is covered in documents, some of them in Foundation format, some in the Harmon script, all of them dated within the last nine months.

The documents are logs of visits to the archive. The visitors' names have been redacted, except for initials. One set of initials appears on eleven separate entries. They read _O5-7_. Another, appearing on four entries, reads _O5-2_. There is a third set, on the last entry, in a looser hand: _O5-9._

Dr. Teague has pointed out that the Overseer Council of the Foundation has, to our knowledge, never authorised any entry into the archive. Its members have never been reported to be missing. Their current whereabouts are classified under the standard need-to-know directive. Dr. Teague wishes it recorded that he did not authorise this exploration, and that he has been waiting a long time to say so.

* * *

**Exploration Log, Entry 11 (Okafor):**

The team has reached the terminus of the shelving. There is a door at the far end.

It is identical to the door through which we entered, in every respect except one. It bears a small inscription, scratched into the timber just below the handle. It reads, in Foundation script:

_Halcyon-7. You are not the first. Do not open the door marked "Retention."_

Dr. Teague has suggested we leave immediately. Dr. Marr has suggested we open the door. Agent Okafor has suggested we vote. We have voted. The result was three to two in favour of leaving, with one abstention from a technician who is now refusing to speak.

We are leaving. I am writing this entry standing in the doorway with my hand on the handle of the door we came through. The light behind me has begun to fail, one row at a time.

As I close the door, I hear, faintly, from somewhere in the far stacks, a sound like a hundred pages being turned at once, and then, in a voice that I know is mine, saying very clearly:

_Don't tell them. They'll only come back._

* * *

**Addendum (Site-47-C Records Office, filed 17 June):**

All six members of Halcyon-7 returned from the archive. Five of them remember the exploration in full. The sixth, Technician Ruiz, has no memory of entering the archive at all, though his badge records show him in the building for the full duration of the team's absence. He has requested reassignment to the surface. His request is pending.

The door at Site 47-C has been sealed and marked with a Euclid-class warning. An inspection of the flooded plant's basement, conducted two days later, found no trace of the door, the shelving, or any of the materials recovered from the archive. The basement has been wet and empty for forty years.

The tin case remains in the evidence crate. Dr. Marr has requested that it be opened by a team that does not include anyone whose file appears in it.

Her request has not yet been granted. Nobody on the review board is willing to say which of them that would exclude.

## 31. Offworlder

The Minister of Science was waiting for me in a room that had been built to make people feel small, which was the first thing I noticed about it. The ceiling was low. The chairs were set eighteen inches lower than mine, so that when I sat down I had to look up at him, and when he leaned forward I had to look up further still.

"Envoy Ysolde," he said, pleasantly. "You walked through the lower quarter this morning."

"I did."

"Without an escort."

"I had an escort. He was very quiet about it. I think he was embarrassed to be following me, and I didn't have the heart to tell him I could hear his footsteps the entire way."

The Minister smiled. His name was Teodor Vasc, and he had been posted to this colony for eleven years, which was a long time to be a politician in a place where nothing ever seemed to happen except rainfall and the slow erosion of the old administration's paperwork. His hair was silver. His hands were folded on the table. He had clearly decided, some time ago, that he liked me, or disliked me, or wanted to find out which.

"Command has informed me of your transgression," he said. "They're very upset."

"Command was not on the lower ring this morning."

"No. But the lower ring was watching you." He tapped the table with one finger. "Tell me what you saw."

I could have lied. I had been briefed for six weeks on the official line, the one written by a committee on the home world that had never set foot on Kessel: that the colony had divided, over six generations, into two populations, the Upper and the Lower, the Sealed and the Open, and that the Open were a degenerate, feral subspecies whose genetic drift had made them unsuited to the rules of interstellar governance. That was what the briefing said. That was what the Ministry of Settlement had written on the front of every report.

What I had seen was a market on the Stone Bridge. Children carrying baskets of pale, fleshy fruit. A woman with long, translucent fingers, bargaining over a bolt of woven cloth with the swift, precise gestures of someone who had been doing it since childhood. A group of men at a tea stall, laughing, their skin as dark and mottled as river stone, speaking a dialect I did not know, but whose rhythms were unmistakably the rhythms of a language. Nobody had looked at me with hostility. Several had looked at me with curiosity, and one old man had given me a small clay bird, which I still had in my coat pocket.

"I saw people," I said.

"Yes," said the Minister. "That's the trouble, isn't it? Once you've seen people, the briefings don't quite fit."

"The briefings say the Open don't have a shared language."

"They have three. They don't say that they teach each other in the markets, because the Ministry would have to admit that the Open have something like a culture." He smiled, faintly. "We don't ask them to write things down. It's easier if they don't."

"Why?"

"Because if they wrote things down, they'd have to be accountable for them." He leaned back, and the shadows slid across his face. "Envoy, you're going to be asked to sign a treaty in four months. A very generous one, from the Ministry's point of view. It gives Command the right to draw water from the Lower Rivers, to relocate the Open to the northern highlands, and to designate their settlements as protected zones for scientific study. You'll be praised as a peacemaker. You'll be given a medal."

"And if I don't sign it?"

"Then the Ministry will send someone who will." He spread his hands. "Who do you think they'll choose? Someone who has been to the Stone Bridge, and eaten the fruit, and been given a clay bird by an old man? Or someone who has never left the Upper Ring?"

I said nothing. He had been waiting for that.

"The point," he said gently, "is that you have already become a problem for the Ministry, and the Ministry has therefore made you useful. I can help you, Envoy. I can make certain that the treaty is written the way you want it, with the water rights protected and the northern highlands left unsurveyed for another decade. In return, I need you to tell Command that the lower ring is stable, that the Open are cooperative, and that your walk this morning was purely a matter of curiosity."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll be sent home in disgrace, and the treaty will go ahead without anyone on Kessel who has actually listened to the people it's about." He tilted his head. "I'm offering you a choice between a bad thing and a worse one, and calling it generosity. It's the oldest trick in the Ministry's book. Did they teach you that, on the home world?"

"They taught me that diplomacy is the art of finding the thing both parties can live with."

"And what can the Open live with, in your opinion?"

I looked at him, at his silver hair and his patient hands and the small, careful smile that had been practised over eleven years of quiet, unending rainfall. I thought about the old man with the clay bird, and the woman with the translucent fingers, and the children carrying baskets of fruit.

"I think they can live with being asked," I said. "I think that's the thing the Ministry has never tried."

The Minister was quiet for a long moment. Then he laughed, softly, and it was not an unpleasant sound.

"You're going to be very difficult," he said. "I'm glad. I was beginning to think they'd sent me a clerk."

He stood, and I stood, and for a moment we faced each other across the lowered table, two people who had each decided what the other was for.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Walk again. The lower ring. Take the escort, but let him be quieter. And bring me the clay bird's name, when you learn it. I'd like to know who I'm dealing with."

I put my hand on the pocket where the bird was hidden.

"It hasn't got a name," I said. "I think it's supposed to be given one."

"Then give it one," the Minister said, and went out, leaving the door open behind him, which was, I realised, the most honest thing anyone on Kessel had done for me since I'd landed.

## 32. Polymerase Chain Reaction

The gene sequences came in on a dead drive wrapped in a cloth that had once been a surgical gown. Mara unwrapped it under the single work lamp in the greenhouse she called the lab, with her breath fogging in the cold and the pumps humming their long, unhappy note through the wall.

Forty-one lines of code. Each one a corporate promise that had expired eleven years ago.

_SPLICE-7 / Tomato, Solanum lycopersicum (Var. Redrock Premium). Terminator sequence: 1 generation. Lifespan of expressed trait: 1 cycle. Patent: Verdance Agro-Biologics. Do not propagate beyond licence period._

She knew the language well enough by now to hear the sneer in it. Verdance had sold the seeds on the promise that a farmer could plant them once, harvest a bumper crop, and buy fresh stock every season from the company's regional depots. They'd built the sterility into the genome so cleverly that the second generation wouldn't flower at all, or if it did, the fruit came out hollow, grey, and stinking of rot. By the time anyone worked out that the terminator was not a bug, Verdance had already shut its doors, and the regional depots had been looted, and the farmers who'd planted the Redrock Premium in the spring of the Collapse year had nothing left to plant but the dust.

Mara's settlement, Harrow's Ford, had been planting Redrock for six years. The seed stock was almost gone. The last viable tomatoes had gone to the children in March.

"Is it workable?" asked Jonas from the doorway. He was thin, sunburnt and shaking slightly, the way he always did when he'd been working with the cold-chain freezers too long.

"Maybe. The terminator's a splice. It's fragile."

"How fragile?"

"Very." She pulled up the schematic on the cracked screen. "It's built on a retrotransposon with a hairpin loop. It'll degrade if you look at it wrong. I'd guess we've got a window of maybe three hours from thaw to lose the switch entirely."

"Then the window's now."

"Jonas, I haven't even got the thermocycler calibrated."

"You've got a car battery, a salvaged centrifuge and a kettle. That's a lab." He grinned, tired and fierce. "Make it work."

* * *

The work was a patchwork of improvisation. The thermocycler had come from a veterinary clinic in the valley, and its temperature sensor had been replaced with a thermistor pulled from a dead refrigerator. The pipettes were glass dropper tubes that Mara had drawn out over a camping stove, their tips crooked and uneven. The reagents were stored in a jam jar lined with foil and kept on ice from the stream.

She ran the first cycle at dawn. Denature at ninety-four degrees, twenty seconds. Anneal, thirty. Extend, forty. Thirty times. The machine clicked and hummed, and she watched the progress bar crawl across the screen of the old tablet she'd cannibalised for parts, and listened to the wind against the greenhouse glass.

She'd learned most of what she knew from the old textbooks in the library in the valley, the ones with the broken spines and the pencil notes in the margins. A PCR was a copying machine. You heated the DNA until it came apart, then cooled it so short strands could latch onto the ends of the target sequence, then let an enzyme build the missing halves, one base at a time. Repeat. Each cycle doubled the number of copies, so after thirty you had a billion pieces of whatever you'd asked for, if the machine didn't burn itself out first.

It was miraculous. It was also, she'd come to believe, a kind of confession. Every copy was a little less perfect than the one before it, and the imperfections piled up, and somewhere in that cascade the old corporate promises began to fail.

She'd tested the splice on her own arm three nights ago, in the dark, with a sterilised needle and a vial of the modified bacteria. The rash had come up on her wrist by morning, an angry red ring that itched like fire. Two days later it had faded. The skin under it looked slightly thinner, slightly smoother, as though something had been polished off the surface. Her fever had come and gone in a single night. She'd slept better than she had in years.

Jonas had asked her, afterward, whether she'd felt any different. She'd said no. She'd lied. She felt faster. Her reflexes were sharper. Her headaches had stopped entirely. And there was a faint, sweet sensation at the back of her throat, every morning, as though she'd been tasting something she'd forgotten the name of.

The untested modifications were medicine, she'd decided, in the same way that a sharp knife was food. Both could kill you if you didn't respect them.

* * *

The contract arrived at noon, wrapped in a cloth bag and delivered by a boy on a bicycle with no tyres.

The message was written on a scrap of cardboard in a neat, anonymous hand.

_Splice for Greyhaven Concern. Redrock terminator, reversed. Need propagation, not just viability. Payment in cold storage. Ten kilos of salt, two full boxes of antibiotics, one freezer unit. Meet at the old depot, Thursday, dusk. Come alone or don't come._

Mara read it three times. Then she read the signature, which was a small, inked symbol of a green leaf wrapped around a stalk of wheat. The Greyhaven Concern.

She knew who they were. Everyone in the valley knew who they were. They'd taken over the old Verdance depot at Fallon Creek four years ago, and they'd been buying up every scrap of biological material they could find since. They also took children, sometimes, to work in the grow-tents, and nobody had ever found out where the children went after that.

"Don't do it," said Jonas, when she showed him the cardboard.

"We need the cold storage."

"We need the seed. We don't need Greyhaven."

"They can give us the freezer. That's the only thing that'll keep the last stock alive through winter."

"They'll take the splice, Mara. They'll take every bit of it, and then they'll sell it back to us at ten times the price, or they'll patent it and send the bailiffs."

She looked at the cardboard for a long time. Outside, the wind had dropped, and the greenhouse glass was going dark, and in the distance she could hear the stream running fast over its stones.

"I'll go," she said. "I'll take the splice. But I won't hand it over."

"Then what will you give them?"

"Nothing they can use." She folded the cardboard into a small, neat square and slipped it into her pocket. "Something that'll look good for three hours, and then fall apart the moment anyone tries to propagate it."

Jonas stared at her. Then, slowly, he started to smile.

"You're a bloody good liar."

"I'm a bloody good biohacker," Mara said. "There's a difference."

She went back to the thermocycler. The cycle had finished. On the tablet's cracked screen, the final sequence glowed in a neat grid of coloured bands, bright and perfect and utterly alive, though she knew, with the same certainty she'd once had about the old copying machines, that by Thursday it would already be beginning to forget itself.

She reached for the pipette, and felt the faint, sweet taste at the back of her throat, and thought, very clearly: _one generation_.

Then she began to write the label.
