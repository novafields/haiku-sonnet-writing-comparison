## 01. The Azra Gambit

The transfer vessel _Lindqvist Reach_ hummed in the way Arthur Neegan had come to associate with money: a deep, expensive note that the hull seemed to absorb rather than emit. He sat in a leather chair bolted to the deck, his mining coveralls still streaked with the red regolith of his plot, and watched Mars shrink beneath the viewport like a coin dropped into a well.

"You may find the chairs uncomfortable," said the man across from him. He wore the grey-and-silver dress of the Antares Diplomatic Corps, its insignia a pair of crossed ellipses. "They were designed to discourage fidgeting. It is a Corps tradition."

"I'm not fidgeting."

"No. You are doing something more interesting. You are trying to decide whether to be frightened." The man smiled. "That is sensible. My name is Dorran Pell. I am a junior attaché, which means I am the one who delivers invitations, and invitations are never the most important thing in a room."

Arthur looked at the insignia again. Two weeks ago he had been a first-generation engineer with a leasehold, a debt to the Ardent Consolidated Mining Company, and a fondness for the quiet of his own workshop. Now four men in powered grey plate had walked through his front door, bowed to his mother's portrait without being asked, and told him that the Azra vein under his kitchen floor was "of interest to several parties."

"I didn't know it was an interest to anyone but me," Arthur said. "I drilled a test core. The readings were wrong. I assumed the instrument was faulty."

"The instrument was not faulty. Azra is not supposed to exist in quantity outside the Kuiper survey zones. Ardent has been hiding its shipments for nine years. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Almost nobody does. That is the first thing you must understand about this business, Mr. Neegan. The metal is the easy part. The lies are the hard part, and everyone is telling them."

The viewport polarised, dimming the sun to a hard white disc. Beyond it, the orbital rings of Phobos cut the dark in a slow, indifferent arc. Arthur thought of the Azra he had seen in the cold chamber at the survey station: a dull grey lattice that sang faintly when touched, and in the presence of which his dosimeter had clicked like a nervous animal.

"Why me?" he asked.

"Because your plot sits on a vein that the Ardent geologists swore did not exist, and because you were the first to report it honestly." Pell leaned forward. "Honesty is rare on Mars. It is also very expensive. We would like to make you an offer that accounts for both."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will still be a rich man, briefly. Ardent will hold your lease, your debt will be forgiven, and you will be encouraged to take a long holiday on Ceres. Several of your neighbours have already taken such holidays. Their plots are quiet now."

Arthur felt the cold in his hands, the same cold he had felt when the instrument first clicked. "And the other parties?"

"Ah." Pell glanced toward the forward hatch, where a shadow passed behind frosted glass. "The other parties are not as patient as I am. Shall we go and meet them?"

The corridor beyond the hatch was lined with pale blue panels that brightened as they passed, as though the ship were noticing them. At its end stood a door of dark, polished wood, incongruous on a vessel of hard alloy.

"Earth oak," Pell said, following his gaze. "Shipped at enormous cost. The Antares Corps believes that a room should remind the visitor of what he has lost. It is a very old idea, and a very effective one."

Inside, the room was small and warm. A single figure sat behind a table of black glass, a slender woman in a high-collared jacket, her hair grey at the temples and cropped close to a skull that seemed made for patience.

"Mr. Neegan," she said, in the flat, unaccented Standard of someone who had learned it as a second language many decades ago. "I am the Envoy Sahrin Oake. I represent interests in the Ceres belt, the Ganymede yards, and, in a manner of speaking, the stars beyond them. Please sit."

He sat.

"Your vein," she said, "is larger than Ardent's survey suggests. Perhaps forty tonnes in the lower strata. It will take your lifetime and your grandchildren's to extract it. Or it will take a fortnight, if you have the right friends."

"I don't have friends."

"You have us. That is worth something, though not perhaps the thing you are hoping for." She folded her hands. "Azra decays heavy isotopes at a rate that makes the drive engineers weep with joy. A hull shielded with it could cross the Oort in months. A warhead tipped with it would be a very short argument. Everyone in this room knows this. The question is who will be permitted to hold the first ton."

Arthur swallowed. "You're offering me a share."

"I am offering you a choice. You may sell your lease to the Corps at a fair price, which I will not insult you by naming in a room with this much oak. You may sell it to Ardent, who will bury you in arbitration and then bury the lease. Or you may remain on Mars, under the protection of the Antares Corps, and work your own land for the Corps' benefit, with a salary, a house in a better district, and a name on a list that no other party is permitted to read."

"And the fourth option?"

Oake's expression did not change, but something behind it sharpened. "There is no fourth option, Mr. Neegan. The universe is kinder than that. It only gives us three doors, and most people walk through the wrong one because it has the nicest handle."

The ship shuddered faintly as it changed its burn. Through the thin bulkhead came the sound of Pell's voice, low and amused, speaking to someone in the corridor about tea.

"Think of it this way," Oake said. "The Azra on Mars is a single grain of sand on a beach that stretches between the planets. Whoever holds that beach holds the tide. You are holding the grain. Be careful how you close your hand."

Arthur looked down at his own hands, rough and scarred from years of drill-work, and wondered how long it would take them to stop trembling.

"I'll need time," he said.

"Of course," said Oake. "We have three days to Ceres. Use them well. The coffee is excellent, and the other passengers are very dull, which is the best thing I can say about any company." She rose. "Mr. Neegan. The dark between worlds is not empty. It is only crowded with people who have not yet decided what they want."

## 02. Darkroom Revelations

The red bulb hummed above the enlarger, and the whole room smelled of stop bath and warm plastic. Dani had worked in darkrooms since she was fifteen, and she had never once felt the room was hers until tonight, when Elliot shut the door and the only light left was the colour of a blood orange held up to the sun.

"You can start the second print," she said. "I'll do the timing."

"You always do the timing." His voice came from somewhere near the sink. He didn't sound annoyed. He sounded like he was smiling.

"Because I'm the one who knows how to do it."

"Mm." A rustle of paper, the soft slap of a sheet going into developer. "I like watching you do it, though."

She didn't answer. She counted to thirty in her head, lifted the print with the tongs, and let the image bloom in the tray: a rooftop, a crooked antenna, a sky going grey at the edges. Her frame. The one she'd taken the morning he'd first shown up in class, all black cuffs and a silver ring on every finger, sitting on a radiator with a paperback open on his knee. She hadn't meant to photograph him. She had photographed the light falling through the window across his hands, and he had happened to be in it.

"It's good," Elliot said quietly, looking over her shoulder. "The contrast is nice."

"You're not supposed to say that about my work."

"You're not supposed to ask me about mine, either, and you've been doing it all semester."

She laughed, and the sound came out thinner than she meant. She was aware of how close he was. Not touching. Just close enough that she could feel the heat off him in the cramped space, the way you feel a stove you've forgotten is on.

"Okay," she said. "Tell me."

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he wouldn't. The timer ticked on the wall. Somewhere above them a pipe knocked twice.

"I've been thinking about what I said to my mum last month," he said at last. "The thing about the name. I told her I'd been thinking about a different one."

"And?"

"And she cried for a while, and then she made me a sandwich, and then she asked if I was still going to wear the black eyeliner to my cousin's wedding." He huffed a breath that was almost a laugh. "I said probably. She said fine. I think she's still working out what it means."

Dani's chest did something complicated. "And what does it mean to you?"

"I don't know yet." The red light caught the edge of his cheekbone, his lashes lowered. "That's the honest answer. I just know that when I'm in here, nobody's looking at me like I'm a question they're supposed to solve. Including you."

"I don't look at you like that."

"You do, though. You look at everything like you're going to take it apart and put it back better." He glanced up, and his eyes were steady. "It's kind of terrifying. In a good way."

She wanted to say something clever. Something that would turn the moment back into a joke, the way she always did when someone got too close. The words didn't come. Instead she set the tongs down very carefully on the edge of the tray and turned to face him.

"Elliot," she said. "I don't know what I'm doing either."

"Okay."

"I've always known what I want. Who I want. I'm not confused about that." She paused. "Or I wasn't. I thought I was attracted to a certain kind of person, a certain shape of... I don't know. Confidence. Definition. Someone who makes it easy to know where I stand."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in a dark room with someone who makes me feel like the ground is moving, and I'm not sure if that's because of you or because of me."

He was very still. Then he stepped closer, slow enough that she could have stopped him, and didn't. His hand came up, hesitated in the air between them like he was checking for permission, and she nodded once.

His fingers touched her jaw, lightly. Cold from the chemicals, warm underneath.

"I'm scared," he said.

"Me too."

"Good." His thumb moved, barely. "I'd hate to be the only one."

She kissed him, or he kissed her, she couldn't tell afterward and didn't care. It was clumsy at first, teeth and a bumped nose and an apology muttered against her mouth, and then it wasn't clumsy at all. Her hands found his waist, the thin cotton of his shirt, and she felt him breathe in sharply, as if he'd been holding it for months.

She had always thought of herself as the one who led. She still wanted to. But there was something in the way he leaned into her, trusting and a little astonished, that made her want to step back and let him show her what he wanted, to give him the space to find his own shape in the moment instead of fitting himself to hers.

"Tell me if this is too much," she murmured.

"You're not too much." He laughed softly. "I think you're the most sensible person I know."

"That's the nicest insult anyone's ever given me."

"It wasn't an insult."

The timer clicked off. The room went quiet except for their breathing and the faint drip of the tap. The red light made everything look like it was underwater, slow and warm and uncertain.

Later, when they walked out into the grey dawn with the prints drying in a cardboard folder under her arm, Elliot slid his hand into hers without a word. Dani didn't let go. She didn't know yet what she was becoming, or what kind of person she would be on the other side of this semester. But she was curious for the first time in years, and curiosity, she was learning, could be its own kind of courage.

## 03. Stolen Moments in Roma

The motorino came around the corner of Via del Corso at a speed that would have been reckless on a racetrack, let alone in a tourist crowd, and Ben Ashcroft only had time to think _oh, that's a bit much_ before the rider leaned, the bike tilted, and the back wheel skidded to a halt six inches from his shin.

"Guarda dove cammini, idiota!" the rider shouted, and then, more quietly, in English that was perfectly clear and mildly contemptuous: "You're standing in the middle of the road reading a book."

"It's a guidebook," Ben said, which was not the point, and then, because he was British and could not help it, "I'm terribly sorry. Are you all right?"

She pulled off her helmet. A cascade of dark hair, a face that looked permanently on the edge of a laugh, and eyes like someone had dropped a match into them. She wore a leather courier jacket with a dozen patches, a bright yellow delivery satchel, and a look of profound irritation that seemed to be aimed mainly at the guidebook.

"Am I all right? I'm delivering prosciutto to a restaurant that closes in twenty minutes, and you're apologising to me." She said something rapid in Italian that Ben caught only partially, something about his mother and a goat. "Scusa, that was rude. Sort of. I'm Alessandra."

"Ben. I'm — sorry, I'm not usually like this. I've been reading about the Trevi Fountain and I got distracted by a paragraph about coins."

"Coins," she repeated. "You were nearly killed by a motorcycle over a paragraph about coins."

"In my defence, the paragraph was fascinating."

She tipped her head, studying him. "You're English. Academic?"

"On sabbatical. Art history. I'm supposed to be writing about Bernini."

"Bernini is over there," she said, pointing with the helmet, "and the fountain is in the other direction, and you're standing exactly where nobody should stand, which is why I nearly killed you." Her mouth curved. "Come on. I'll show you the right way to look at it. You'll need to throw a coin with your eyes closed. Everyone does it wrong."

"I thought you were in a hurry."

"I am always in a hurry. That's not a reason to stop living."

* * *

By noon they had crossed the Piazza di Spagna at a run, because Alessandra had decided the Spanish Steps were "full of people who think they are in a film," and Ben, who had been prepared to dislike her intensely, found himself laughing so hard he had to sit down on the fountain's rim. She had left him there with the prosciutto and returned with two paper cups of espresso and a pastry she claimed was "better than anything in your guidebook," which was true.

She was the most alive person he had ever met, and she was, he was fairly sure, making fun of him.

"You're going to fall in," she said, leaning against the railing beside him.

"I'm perfectly stable."

"You're standing on the wrong side of the rail, _tesoro_." She gestured. "The Inglese always stand on the wrong side of everything. It's very endearing. Like a dog who thinks it is a cat."

He looked down at his shoes, then back at her, and realised she had her hand in her satchel, and that something small and silver had vanished from the stall beside them, and that a woman in a cream-coloured coat was shouting at a boy on a scooter, and that Alessandra was not looking at any of it at all.

"Did you just —" he began.

"Did I what?" She smiled. "You have a very active imagination, Mr. Ashcroft. It's from all those books."

* * *

The afternoon went badly, which was to say magnificently. Alessandra took him through the back streets of Trastevere at a pace that left him breathless, past laundry hung like flags above narrow alleys, into a small courtyard where an old man was playing the accordion to a single pigeon. She made him dance with the pigeon's owner, a stout grandmother who called him _bello_ and pinched his cheek. She bought a bag of cherries from a stall and then, with a glance at the vendor, tucked two into Ben's jacket pocket "for later," which he suspected was not a gift.

At the Pantheon they were nearly thrown out for climbing the wrong staircase. At the Campo de' Fiori, she bargained for a hat in a fluent, scathing dialect that reduced the seller to helpless admiration. At the top of the Janiculum, as the sun dropped behind the domes and the whole city turned the colour of apricots, she stood at the wall with her arms folded and said nothing at all for a long time.

"Are you all right?" he asked eventually.

"I'm thinking about something else." Her voice was softer than he had heard it. "That's all."

"Do you want to tell me?"

"No." She looked sideways at him. "But thank you for asking. Nobody usually asks."

* * *

They parted at the bottom of the hill, near the tram stop, where the streetlamps had come on and the air smelled of woodsmoke and diesel.

"I have to go," Alessandra said. "My shift starts at ten tomorrow, and I have a lot of prosciutto to deliver."

"Will I see you again?"

"Maybe." She reached into his jacket pocket, pulled out one of the cherries, and bit into it, juice running down her chin. "You're very predictable, Ben Ashcroft. You'll go back to your library and write about Bernini and feel guilty for having fun. And then one day you'll see my motorino come round a corner and you'll stand very still in the middle of the road, and I'll know you're thinking about me."

"That's an extraordinary assumption."

"It's a very good one." She licked her thumb, grinned, and shoved the helmet back over her hair. "Arrivederci, _inglese_. Don't read any more guidebooks."

The motorino roared into the traffic and was gone. Ben stood on the pavement for a long moment, one hand in his pocket, feeling the last cherry stone and the faint, impossible sense that something had shifted in the ordinary grey tilt of his life.

He pulled the guidebook out of his bag. Under his breath, he said, "Coins," and then, to nobody in particular, "Oh, God."

## 04. The Last Outpost

"Vega whisky, neat. Better make that a double."

I'm already reaching for the bottle before the stranger finishes the sentence, because the Outpost has taught me that people who order doubles at the end of a shift are usually trying to drown something they'll be sorry about in the morning. The bottle is the good stuff, the twelve-year-old Ceres blend that Dorn smuggles in from the Kessler yards, and I pour two fingers into a heavy glass and slide it across the scarred counter.

"Rough cycle?" I ask.

The stranger looks up. Tentacled dreads, pale green at the tips, have half-collapsed into a knot at the nape of their neck, and there's a grease-smear on one cheek and a bruise along the jaw that's already gone the colour of an eggplant. Their eyes are amber, slit-pupilled, and very tired.

"Something like that," they say. Their voice is low, with the faint hiss of a respirator's habit. "Is there somewhere a person can sit and not be looked at?"

"Booth by the vent. It's loud enough that nobody listens, and the air's better. Lots of people use it to not be looked at." I glance down the bar. Old Teague is pretending to read a datapad, Juno and the twins are arguing over a card game they've been losing since the Equinox, and the Cerulean crew is shouting at the holo-screen again. "Name's Nets, by the way. Nobody here uses the long one."

"Thessaly." They pick up the glass and drink, and the fact that they don't cough is its own statement. "Thank you, Nets. I'll pay for the drink."

"You'll pay for the drink," I agree. "Nobody's getting a discount tonight. We've got a machine that's been hiccupping since Tuesday, and Dorn says the new filter's coming on the next supply run, which means it's coming in six weeks, which means I'm the one dealing with the stink."

Thessaly laughs. It's a short sound, surprised out of them. "Do you always talk this much?"

"Only when the customers look like they're about to fall apart on my floor."

"I'm not going to fall apart on your floor."

"Good. Mop's broken."

* * *

They drift to the booth. I bring the bottle, because the bottle is a better conversation partner than most patrons, and because I want to see the way Thessaly sits: spine against the bench, one hand around the glass, the other flat on the table as if they expect the table to move. That's a sailor's habit. Or a runner's.

"So," I say. "You're not from the belt."

"No."

"Long way from anywhere, then."

"The long way is the only way people come to the belt." Thessaly stares into the whisky. "Some of us have business out here we'd rather not explain."

"Business," I repeat, and I let the word sit between us, because I've learned that the most dangerous thing you can do with a stranger is get comfortable with them too fast.

Thessaly looks up, and something moves behind the amber. "Is that a problem?"

"It's a bar. Everybody's got business. Mine is keeping the ice cold and the rent paid. Yours seems to involve somebody's ship getting shot at. I can tell by the scorch on your collar."

They touch the collar. Their fingers come away black. "It's a bad night for scorch marks."

"It's a bad week." I lean on the table. "Look, you don't have to say anything. But if somebody's looking for you, I'd rather know before they walk through that door."

"Why?"

"Because this is the Outpost, not a police station. We don't ask questions here. We just make sure nobody gets carried out."

"That's not very reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be true."

Thessaly's jaw works. Then they reach into an inside pocket and set something on the table between us: a small, flat data chip, black and dull, with a silver stripe along one edge. It's the kind of thing I haven't seen since I left Ganymede, the kind of thing that means somebody had a lot of money and a lot of very bad intentions.

I don't touch it. "Is that why you're drinking doubles?"

"It's why I'm not sleeping." Thessaly's voice drops. "There's a man on the Ceres platform who's been paying people to find this. He doesn't care what they have to do. And the last crew who had it are..." They stop. "They're not coming back."

I feel the chill run down my spine, the old reflex that tells me I should walk away right now. I could. Dorn would understand. The Outpost has survived a lot worse than one stranger with a dangerous chip.

But Thessaly is looking at me the way I used to look at people when I was still hoping someone would notice me in a crowd. Like I'm the last door they haven't tried.

"Okay," I say slowly. "You've got my attention. What's on it?"

"A map," Thessaly says. "And a name. Yours, I think."

The vent above us clanks. Somewhere behind the bar, the drink machine coughs out a fresh cup of something green and wrong. Across the room, Juno slams down a card and swears, and the whole crowd laughs, and nobody notices the way my hand is suddenly cold on the glass.

"Mine?" I say.

"Your last name," Thessaly corrects gently. "The one you left behind."

## 05. Gone South

Okay, so the first thing you need to know is that the bus station in New Orleans smells like fried dough and wet cardboard, and I love it, which is the most embarrassing thing I've admitted to anybody in the last three days.

I'm sitting on a bench next to a bag of Cheetos and a suitcase with one broken wheel that isn't mine. I'm wearing the hoodie I stole off the back of Mom's chair on Tuesday, which is three sizes too big and smells like the cheap detergent she buys in bulk, and every time I breathe in I want to scream and also feel super guilty for wanting to scream. My head is a mess.

Here's what I know. I'm sixteen. My name is Taylor. I told my family I'm trans two days ago, and it didn't go well. Like, really didn't. Dad went quiet and then started saying the thing about "that's not how God made you," over and over, like a broken record. Mom cried and then asked if I was "going through a phase," and I said no, and she said "We'll see," and that was the worst thing anybody has ever said to me.

So last night I packed my backpack and a pair of sneakers and walked to the highway, and I got a ride from a guy named Roy in a pickup full of fishing lures, and he dropped me off here, at a gas station two miles from the actual bus station, where I was hoping nobody I knew would ever set foot.

That was the plan. I didn't really have a plan after that.

* * *

"Hey."

I jump. There's a girl sitting at the other end of the bench. She's maybe eighteen, with bleached hair braided into a bun and a leather jacket covered in faded band patches, and she has the kind of face that looks like it's been up all night and doesn't plan on sleeping soon.

"Hey," I say back, because my mom would kill me if I didn't.

"You look like you're about to throw up on that suitcase."

"I'm not. I'm just tired."

"Mm." She holds out a hand. "I'm Jade. Not real name. Real name's none of your business, and yours is probably something you don't like either, so let's skip it."

I stare at her hand. Something in me goes cold. "You're a runaway."

"Obviously. You're one too." She shrugs. "Nobody sits in the New Orleans bus terminal at six in the morning with a stolen hoodie and a face like that unless they're running from something. Relax. I'm not gonna sell you to anybody."

"Who would buy me?"

Jade's smile goes thin. "You'd be surprised who buys people in this city, kid. Don't be stupid about who you trust."

I want to tell her I'm not stupid. I want to tell her I've been stupid for sixteen years, pretending to be somebody I'm not, hoping that if I was just quieter, just more like the boy my dad wanted, everything would fall into place. But my throat closes up, and I just nod and look at my shoes.

* * *

Jade takes me to a diner on Canal Street that smells like coffee and bacon grease, and orders me pancakes like she's been doing it for years. She tells me the rules without really explaining them: don't sleep in the park at night, don't take rides from anybody who asks your name twice, don't let the guys on Bourbon call you sweetheart because they're not being nice. And don't go to the shelter on Rampart Street unless you're desperate, because they call the cops on anybody who looks "suspicious."

"Who's suspicious?" I ask.

"Anybody who isn't a grown-up with a plan." Jade stabs a pancake. "Or anybody who looks like they're gonna be trouble. Which is most of us."

"Are you going to be trouble?"

"I already am." She grins. "Two years on the streets, honey. I've been everything from a waitress to a bouncer to a girl who stole a car in Memphis and drove it into a lake by accident. Got a lot of stories. Some of them are even true."

I laugh. It comes out weird and cracked, and Jade looks at me funny, like she didn't expect it.

"You're gonna be okay," she says, more quietly. "I mean it. You've got that look. Like you're gonna figure out who you are the second somebody lets you."

My eyes burn. I look down at the syrup on my plate. _Who I am._ I don't even know if I'm allowed to think that yet.

* * *

Later, in a park near the river where the air is thick enough to swim in, Jade introduces me to a skinny kid named Marcus who sells sketchbooks at a stall on Decatur, and who gives me a look like he's measuring my shoes. He tells me the city will eat me alive if I let it, and then asks if I can draw.

"Not really," I say.

"Doesn't matter," Marcus says. "Everybody here draws something. Even if it's just their own name on a wall." He shrugs. "You want a sketchbook? Free. Just don't leave it in the rain."

I take it. My hand shakes when I open it to the first blank page, and I think about my dad's voice, and my mom's face, and the word "phase," and I think about how loud it is in my head right now, how many things I want to say and can't.

_I'm Taylor,_ I write, very small, on the inside cover. _I'm sixteen. I'm scared. I'm not sorry._

Jade reads it over my shoulder and says nothing. But when I look up, she's nodding, slow and serious, like she's just watched somebody take the first step onto a bridge they weren't sure would hold.

"Okay, kid," she says. "Welcome to New Orleans. Don't trust anybody who says it's fun."

## 06. My Apocalypse Buddy

The plan, as Midas explained it to Chewy at seven-fifteen that morning, was simple: walk four miles east to the old Tesco distribution depot, retrieve the fourteen tins of peaches that Midas was reasonably sure were still in the back of Aisle Nine, and walk four miles home again before the rain came in off the Stilton hills.

"That's it?" said Chewy. Chewy was a stuffed koala of indeterminate age, one ear missing, and a voice box that had been wired in some long-dead factory to say "Hello, friend!" at every opportunity. It had said this to a passing raider in the second week of the collapse, and the raider had shot it. Chewy had not stopped talking since. The raider had not stopped running.

"That's it," Midas said. "Four miles. Peaches. We'll be home for tea."

"Hello, friend!" said Chewy.

"Not yet, you sodden lump. Let's get the rucksack."

* * *

The first complication arrived at mile two, in the form of a fence that had once been a bypass and was now a sort of extended metal beard, with a moderately sized cart-shaped hole in its middle. Midas stood before it for some time, considering his options, which were limited to climbing it, walking around it, or sitting down in the mud and sulking.

He chose option four, which was to tie one end of a frayed nylon cord to a fence post, the other end to Chewy's left foot, and throw Chewy over the top.

"Hello, friend!" Chewy shouted, cartwheeling through the air in a manner that suggested it had considered this sort of thing before. It landed on a rusted car roof, bounced once, and came to rest upside-down in a puddle.

"Well," Midas said. "That's one way to do it."

He climbed the fence. He caught his trouser leg on a wire, tore it, swore in a manner that would have made his grandmother reach for her rosary, and dropped down the other side directly onto a patch of nettles.

"Hello, friend!" said Chewy, from the puddle.

"Don't," said Midas.

* * *

The second complication was the dog.

It was not, strictly speaking, a dog. It had been a dog once, perhaps a pleasant spaniel with an innocent smile and a fondness for tennis balls. Now it was something built of scrap and rope and tooth, with a head that had been crudely welded to a bicycle frame and a voice like a tractor being slowly strangled. It came out of a drainage culvert at a sprint, and Midas, who had survived six years of the apocalypse largely by running away from things, ran away from it.

He ran for about forty yards before he realised he had left Chewy in the mud.

He stopped. He turned around. The dog had stopped too, and was staring at the koala with an expression that might, on a living animal, have been confusion.

"Hello, friend!" said Chewy.

The dog tilted its welded head. Then it took a tentative step forward, sniffed at Chewy's stuffed ear, and very carefully picked the koala up in its jaws.

"Oh, for heaven's sake," Midas muttered, and walked back to take it off him.

The dog growled. Midas growled back, which was embarrassing, because the dog was clearly better at growling. In the end, Midas offered it a half-eaten packet of biscuits from his pocket and said, "You can keep the koala if you promise to give him a bath," and the dog, who had apparently been waiting for exactly this sort of deal, dropped Chewy in the mud and trotted off happily with the biscuits.

"Friend," said Chewy, dripping, "is very pleased."

"I'm glad someone is."

* * *

The third complication was the depot, which had been looted, burned, and then, for reasons nobody could explain, filled with a colony of very aggressive geese. Midas found Aisle Nine. He found the peaches. He found, under the peaches, a wooden box containing forty-two mint humbugs, a single pair of ladies' tights, and a letter from a Mrs. Dorothy Pilling of Hexham, addressed to a son who had almost certainly been dead for a decade.

He read the letter aloud to Chewy while the geese hissed at them from the loading bay.

"'Dearest Terry,'" Midas read, in his most solemn voice. "'I hope this finds you well. The rain has been dreadful. Your father is still refusing to eat any of the cabbage. I have enclosed a photograph of the cat, who has taken to sleeping on the radiator. Please do come home soon, my darling, before the world goes entirely to pot.'"

He folded the letter very carefully and put it in his breast pocket.

"Well," he said. "At least somebody got their wish."

"Hello, friend," said Chewy, with what Midas chose to interpret as sympathy.

* * *

They got home at dusk, which was four hours later than planned and considerably wetter. They had lost a rucksack, a boot, and most of their dignity. They had gained fourteen tins of peaches, a box of mint humbugs, one set of ladies' tights (which Midas had decided to use as an emergency sling), and a dog named Bartholomew, who had followed them most of the way back and was now sitting outside the cottage with an expression of enormous loyalty.

Midas looked at the dog. He looked at the koala under his arm, still faintly damp, its single ear drooping.

"Right," he said. "This is exactly what I meant by 'home for tea.'"

"Hello, friend!" said Chewy, cheerfully, and Bartholomew wagged his bicycle frame.

Midas opened the cottage door and went inside, and the peaches, at least, were perfectly intact.

## 07. Love in the Limelight

The bell over the door of Pen-y-Bryn Books gave a small, offended jingle as the man slipped inside, pulling a dark cap low and turning up the collar of a coat that cost more than the shop's entire front window. He pressed his back to the door and let out a breath.

"Don't," said Nia Lloyd, without looking up from the box of second-hand Dickens she was pricing. "Whatever it is, I'm not buying it, and I'm not hiding you in the back."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask."

"I was going to say _good afternoon_. Which is a lovely thing to say to a shop owner, I think you'll agree."

She looked up then. He was tall, and handsome in the particular way of men who had been told so since adolescence, with cheekbones that belonged on a poster and a mouth that was clearly used to being watched. She recognised him, of course. Everybody in Wales recognised him. He had spent most of the last year playing a brooding Welsh coal-miner's son in a costume drama that had been on the front of every magazine in the Co-op.

"Marcus Vale," she said flatly.

"Sadly, yes." He smiled, and it was a good smile, wide and warm and aimed directly at her. "Forgive the intrusion. There are three men with cameras outside the chemist, and one of them has a ladder. I panicked. I saw a bookshop and thought, _there, that's a place where nobody screams._"

"You thought correctly. Nobody screams in here. It's too quiet." She returned to her pricing gun. _Thunk. Thunk._ "Ten minutes, then you leave. You're blocking the Hardy."

"The Hardy is wonderful," he said, drifting toward the shelves with his hands in his pockets. "Tragic, obviously. Everyone in Hardy is doomed. It's very reassuring."

"It's not reassuring. It's a warning."

"Oh, I like that." He pulled out a battered copy of _Tess_, flipped it open, and read a line aloud in an accent so clipped and posh it was almost parody: "'Tragedy is the cradle of the soul.'" He glanced up at her, eyebrows raised. "Do you agree?"

"I think you're reading the wrong book for someone who's trying to hide from paparazzi."

"Hiding is a skill I'm not very good at."

"You don't say."

* * *

He tried, of course. She had expected that. He was an actor, and actors were, in her experience, incapable of standing in a room without trying to make it love them. He told her about the film. He told her about his co-star's divorce. He told her, with what he apparently believed was a flawless air of self-deprecation, that he had been named _Sexiest Man in Cymru_ three years running and had only ever been able to stand the honour because of his mother's cooking.

"Your mother must be proud," Nia said.

"She is. She keeps a scrapbook."

"Of course she does."

He laughed, delighted. "You're very unimpressed, aren't you?"

"I'm busy."

"Busy pricing books no one will ever buy."

"Yes. That's what busy means." She finally met his eyes properly, and something in her chest went tight and annoying. "Mr. Vale, I've been running this shop for eleven years. I've had a famous poet sleep in the window, a politician try to sell me a signed copy of his own autobiography, and a very drunk barrister propose to me in the poetry section. I'm not impressed by people who think a good cheekbone is a personality."

He stared at her for a long second. Then he set _Tess_ back on the shelf with exaggerated care.

"That," he said softly, "was the most interesting thing anyone has said to me this month."

"Then you need a more interesting month."

"I'm beginning to think I do."

* * *

The bell jingled again. A small boy in a rain-soaked anorak stumbled in, clutching a comic, looked at Marcus, gasped, and ran straight back out again.

"Brilliant," Nia said. "Now the whole village knows."

"Oh, God." He ran a hand through his hair, which did nothing to make it look less perfect. "I'm sorry. Truly."

"Don't be. It's a small town. It's always been going to happen eventually."

"Is that a comment on my celebrity or your patience?"

"Both."

He leaned against the counter, not quite close enough to touch, and watched her price a copy of _Wuthering Heights_ with a thoughtful expression. "You're not even slightly curious about me, are you?"

"I'm curious about why you think coming in here was a good idea."

"I told you. It was the only quiet room in Wales."

"Mm." Nia set the book down. Her hand brushed the edge of the counter, and his fingers were there, not quite touching hers, close enough that she could feel the warmth off his skin. She didn't move. Neither did he.

"You're doing it again," she said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Trying to make me like you."

"And is it working?"

"No." Her voice didn't waver. "But you're very nearly charming, and it's annoying how much that bothers me."

He looked at her, and for the first time since he had walked through the door, he seemed to forget the lines. His smile was smaller now, slower, and genuinely uncertain.

"I don't think anyone has ever told me no in quite that tone before," he said.

"Then it's been good for you."

"Terrifying, actually." His mouth twitched. "Can I come back tomorrow? When the men with ladders have gone?"

"You can come back when you've read a book without pretending to read it in front of me."

"That's a very specific condition."

"I've been running this shop eleven years, Mr. Vale. I'm extremely specific."

* * *

He left twenty minutes later, after buying nothing, pulling his cap low, and pausing at the door with his hand on the frame.

"Goodbye, Nia Lloyd," he said, reading the name off the shop's old brass plate. "I'll be the one who isn't impressed by anything. Next time."

She didn't look up. But after the bell had stopped jingling and the street was quiet again, she found she had stopped pricing books altogether, and was standing very still at the counter, her heart doing something absurd and entirely unbusinesslike.

She picked up _Tess_. Still open to the page. She didn't put it back.

## 08. The Morska Conspiracy

The underhold of the Brass Tide was colder than the streets above it, and quieter, and the silence pressed against Aya's ears like wet wool. Behind her, Katra's breath came in short, careful bursts through the cloth of her mask. Simeon's boots, spiked with steel, made no sound at all on the flagstones; he had spent eight years learning to walk the way cats walked, and he never let anyone forget it.

"Too quiet," Katra whispered.

"I know." Aya's gloved hand drifted to the brass knuckles at her hip and rested there. The lantern in her other hand threw its light across a corridor of black iron doors, each stamped with the Morska sigil: a whale's tail curling over a clockwork key. "The raid took the Steward at four. Three hours is long enough for the rats to have fled. It is not long enough for them to have gone quiet."

"Perhaps they're dead," Simeon said.

"Then the dead have excellent manners," said Aya, and started walking.

The door at the end of the corridor was the largest of them, riveted with hammered copper and sealed with a lock as big as a man's heart. A single steam-pipe ran above it, weeping a thin, oily condensation that gleamed in the lantern light like tears. Aya had stood before this door a dozen times over the last six months, always at the head of a crew who did not know what she was, always pretending that the arcane mechanisms in its frame were no more mysterious than a clock's escapement.

They were not. The key was made of bone and brass together, and when she laid her palm on the lock, it _breathed_, a cold, dark throb that seemed to rise through her wrist and settle behind her eyes. She had inherited her mother's gift for the immateria, and it had never once been kind to her.

She had never told Katra or Simeon what the gift actually cost. She had simply let them watch her summon the things from the dark beneath the city, the shapes with too many joints, and let them believe she was a clever young woman with a talent for bargains.

"Aya," said Simeon quietly. "Your hand."

She looked down. Black threads of something like ink were spreading under her glove, creeping toward the knuckles. She drew the hand back sharply, and the threads recoiled like frightened animals.

"It's fine," she said. "It's the lock. It knows me."

"Does it?" Katra's voice was tight. "Or does it know what you are?"

Before Aya could answer, the door swung open on its own.

* * *

The light inside was red, flickering, oil-sick. The underhold was a vast cavern cut into the bedrock, its ceiling lost in shadow and its walls lined with brass pipes that wheezed and hissed like the lungs of some enormous, dying beast. At its centre, on a dais of black marble, rested the Scythan artifact: a curved blade the length of a man's forearm, its edge etched with runes that seemed to crawl in the dim light.

And standing beside it, with a revolver in one gloved hand and a dark-coated bodyguard at either shoulder, was the Steward of the Morska guild.

Her name was Ilsabet Varro, and she was supposed to be in the Tallow Street gaol with a bullet in her knee and a noose waiting in the morning. She looked very much as she had at the last council meeting: grey streaks in her hair, a scar bisecting one eyebrow, a smile as precise as a surgeon's blade.

"Ah," she said. "The ghost of the Tide has come to visit."

Aya's heart hammered. She kept her face still. "Ilsabet. I thought you were in gaol."

"So did the constabulary. They're terribly trusting men, when someone pays them enough." Ilsabet's gaze slid over Katra and Simeon, lingered on Simeon's spiked boots, then came back to Aya with a dreadful, interested calm. "You've been spending your nights moonlighting in my guild, my dear. Masked, of course. Very dashing. And very clever. You summoned the Tallow Street Hound in front of forty of my men and they still haven't stopped talking about it."

"I didn't —"

"Don't lie. It's beneath you, and it's wasted on me." Ilsabet lifted the revolver a fraction, not quite aiming. "Mask, please. I'd like to see the face of the woman who has been stealing my people's loyalty."

Aya's hand rose to the silk. Her fingers trembled. She could feel Katra's gaze on her back, burning with betrayal and fear, and she could feel the black threads under her glove stirring again, eager and hungry.

She unmasked.

The silence was absolute. Then Ilsabet laughed, low and delighted, like a clock striking midnight.

"Lady Aya Hallowell," she said. "The daughter of the Lord Chancellor's dearest friend. The girl who was supposed to be marrying the Duke of Vellmere in the spring." She shook her head slowly. "Your mother was hanged for immateria magick, child. Did you think no one would recognise your eyes?"

"My mother was innocent," Aya said, and her voice came out cracked. "She was trying to heal a child in the Vellmere slums. They hanged her for it."

"Yes. And the child died anyway." Ilsabet's smile was gentle now, which was far worse. "The city does not reward mercy, my dear. It only remembers who was hanged."

* * *

They were taken without a struggle, because the bodyguards were very large and the revolver was very steady, and because Aya could see the wisdom of not dying in the first five minutes of a conversation.

Katra was bound with copper wire at the wrists. Simeon was held against the nearest pipe with a knife at his throat and a look on his face that Aya had never seen before: a naked, unhidden grief. He was looking at her, not at the blade.

"I'm sorry," Aya said, to all of them and to none.

"Don't," Simeon rasped. "You're the only reason I ever wanted to be part of anything."

Ilsabet paced slowly across the dais, her heels echoing on the marble. "Now, Lady Aya. Let us discuss your value."

"I'm not valuable."

"Oh, you are. You're the daughter of an earl. You're the only heir to a fortune that the Lord Chancellor is desperate to get his hands on. And you're the one person in Morska who can open the Scythan's casing without the runes burning the operator's flesh to the bone." Ilsabet tapped the glass case beside the artifact with one finger. "I have been waiting for someone with your blood for a very long time."

Aya's mind raced. The lock. The threads under her skin. Her mother's voice in her memory, low and tired, saying _never let them see you bleed for the magick, Aya; they'll sell you by the pint._

"If you want the casing open," Aya said slowly, "you'll have to let my friends go."

"Oh, my dear. I'll let them go when you've finished explaining why the immateria is calling to you so loudly." Ilsabet smiled. "Shall we begin?"

Behind them, in the depths of the cavern, the pipes began to sing.

## 09. Gunpowder Rendezvous

The bar was called the Rusted Bell, and it deserved the name. Smoke hung under the low ceiling like a second, grimier sky. A broken jukebox in the corner played something tinny and out of tune, and the bartender, a heavy-set man with one eye and a towel over his shoulder, had the expression of someone who had stopped being surprised by anything in 1997.

Spike Spiegel sits at the far end of the counter with a cigarette in his mouth he has not lit, staring into a glass of cheap bourbon as though it has personally insulted him. He has a bounty poster folded in his jacket pocket, a photograph of a man with a crooked grin and a price on his head that would keep a family in Mars City for a decade. He has been in the bar for forty minutes. He has been here for ninety seconds too long.

The door bangs open.

Revy walks in like she owns the place, which she probably would if she could afford it. Black crop top, dragon tattoo winding up her right arm like a living thing, shorts so short they barely qualify as an opinion. Two pistols sit in holsters at her hips, and she does not bother to hide them. Her eyes sweep the room once, flat and quick, and land on Spike with the faint, dangerous amusement of a cat who has found a mouse already caught in a trap.

"Well, fuck me," she says, loud enough for the whole bar to hear. "The cowboy."

Spike sighs, lights the cigarette at last, and exhales slowly. "Revy."

"You look like shit."

"You always say that."

"Because you always look like shit." She drops onto the stool beside him without asking, signals the bartender with two fingers, and says, "Whatever's cheapest and strongest. Make it a double. He's paying."

"Am I?"

"You've been drinking the same glass for forty minutes, you broke bum." She grins. "Sit up straight. You're making it obvious you're waiting for someone."

"I am."

"Yeah?" Her eyes narrow. "Who?"

Spike taps the folded poster. Revy unfolds it, and her grin broadens into something dangerous and delighted. "Oh, that's _good_. The Brazilian. The one who sold his own mother for a shipment of cocaine. Reward's a hundred grand."

"You want it?"

"I always want the money." She drains her glass. "Question is, do you?"

"I want to get some sleep without anyone shooting at me."

"That's the most boring answer I've ever heard, Spiegel."

"I'm a boring man."

"You're a _liar_." She leans in, close enough that he can smell the whiskey and gun oil on her. "You've been chasing this guy for three weeks. You've got a dead partner on your conscience, and a prosthetic eye that makes you miss shots at the wrong angle, and you're still sitting here like you're gonna win."

Spike turns his head slowly, and for a moment the easy, lazy look drops from his face. His real eye, dark and tired, fixes on hers. "You know about the eye."

"I know about everything." Revy's mouth quirks. "It's a small town, cowboy."

"This isn't a small town. It's a bar in a city full of people trying to kill each other."

"Same thing." She finishes the double the bartender slides across, and her knuckles are white around the glass. "So. Partners or competitors?"

"Neither."

"Wrong answer." She sets the glass down with a click. "We're gonna be competitors. Two bounty hunters, same target, same goddamn bar. I'll get him first, and you'll be standing in a ditch crying about your eye."

"And if I get him first?"

"Then I'll shoot you in the leg and take the money anyway."

Spike laughs, short and rough, and the sound surprises both of them. "You're insane."

"You're dying."

"Already died once. Doesn't stick."

"Then you're just a ghost with bad taste in bars." She stands, tucks a fresh cigarette into her mouth, and slaps a crumpled note on the counter. "Motel on Fifth. Room twelve. Ten o'clock. If you're not there, I'll assume you ran off to become a cowboy in a movie and I'll tell everyone you're dead."

"I'm not going to be there."

"You'll be there," Revy says, already walking, "because you're too stubborn to let me win."

* * *

Room twelve smells like mildew and stale cigarettes. The television flickers with a black-and-white western, a lonely piano soundtrack rising and falling under a gunfight that nobody is watching.

Revy is sitting on the edge of the bed with both pistols on the nightstand and a bottle of whiskey between her knees. She looks up when the door opens, and her eyes follow Spike across the room with an intensity that makes him want to check his collar for blood.

"You came," she says.

"Figured you'd just shoot the lock."

"I thought about it." She tilts the bottle toward him. He takes it, and their fingers brush, and neither of them looks away. "You're not supposed to be this easy to find, you know."

"I'm not easy to find. You're just good at looking."

"Shut up." She takes the bottle back and drinks. "You know what the problem is with you?"

"Several things, I'm told."

"You don't care if you live." Her voice drops. "You walk into every fight like it's the last one you'll ever have, and you don't even flinch. It makes people nervous. It makes _me_ nervous."

Spike stares at her. He takes a slow breath. "I care about one thing."

"Yeah? What?"

"That I'm not going to die before I get to know what it's like to not be running."

Revy's expression changes, the mockery draining out of it. Her hand tightens around the bottle. For a long moment, neither of them moves.

Then the door behind Spike crashes open, and the room erupts.

* * *

Three men pour in, guns raised, their faces ugly with the Brazilian's money and the Brazilian's temper. Spike is already moving, his body flowing into the familiar rhythm of Jeet Kune Do, a kick sweeping the first gunman's legs out from under him. The second man fires; the bullet punches a hole in the wall an inch from Revy's head, and she is off the bed in a single motion, both pistols coming up in a blur.

"You shot my _motel_," she snarls, and fires.

The third man goes down clutching his shoulder. The second is still on his feet, staggering toward Spike with a knife in his hand, and Spike catches his wrist, turns, and slams the man's arm against the doorframe until the knife clatters to the floor.

They stand back to back in the wreckage of the room, breathing hard, guns steady, the television still playing its lonely piano music over three groaning bodies.

"Well," Spike says. "That was excessive."

"Nah," Revy says. "That was Tuesday."

He laughs again, and this time she laughs too, a jagged, exhausted sound. She turns, and their faces are inches apart, and the gunsmoke still hangs thick between them.

"Ten o'clock," she murmurs.

"You're late."

"I'm always late." She tilts her head, and her lips are very close to his. "Now. Who's gonna shoot first?"

He doesn't answer. Neither does she. The pistols stay where they are, aimed at nothing, and the television goes to static, and for one suspended, impossible moment there is no bounty, no Brazilian, no dead partner, no past at all. Only two people who have never learned how to stop.

## 10. Útiseta

I still don't know whether I was scared of the mound or of Astrid, and I suspect that's the honest answer to most of what happened that summer.

We moved to Vaxholm — no, not Vaxholm, somewhere north of it, a village in Norrbotten so small the bus only stopped twice a day — in June of 1995. My father had a new job at the timber mill, and my mother had decided that the fresh air would be good for me. I was fifteen. I had never seen a moose outside a zoo. I thought the midnight sun was a cruel joke. I spent the first month with my nose in a paperback and my earphones jammed in, hating everything.

Astrid found me at the river. She was sitting on a flat rock with her bare feet in the water, eating blueberries from a tin, and she didn't look up when I came down the bank.

"You're the girl from Stockholm," she said. "You look like someone who's been crying in a car."

"I wasn't crying."

"Mm. Good. Here." She held out the tin. "They're from the hill behind the church. Nobody picks them but me."

I took one. It was sweet and sour and tasted like the cold. We sat there for an hour, and by the time her grandmother called her home, I had agreed to meet her again the next night.

* * *

Astrid's grandmother lived at the edge of the forest in a red cottage with white window frames and a garden that was mostly nettles and blackcurrant bushes. Her name was Ingrid, and she was ninety, and she had a way of looking at you that made you feel like she had already read your diary and was too polite to mention it.

"You're the Stockholm girl," she said, when I came up the path. "Astrid says you're curious."

"I'm not curious."

"She says you want to know things."

I didn't answer. Ingrid studied me for a long moment, then smiled, showing a gap where a tooth had been. "Good. Curiosity is the only honest thing people have. The rest is just manners."

She didn't say much else. But Astrid began to teach me things, quietly, over the next weeks. The midnight games, she called them. Little rituals to learn what your classmates were thinking, or to make the boy at the petrol station look at you twice. You tied a red thread around your wrist and whispered a name into the wind. You left a coin under a stone at the crossroads. You made a wish on the first star after the sun went down, and you never, ever said it aloud.

They were stupid. I knew they were stupid. But they were also fun, and for the first time since we'd moved, I felt like someone was paying attention to me.

* * *

The night of the útiseta was the first of August. Astrid came to my window at eleven, in a black jumper and borrowed wellies, with a rucksack over her shoulder and a look on her face I'd never seen before. It wasn't mischief. It was something older and quieter, like a girl who knows she's about to step off a ledge.

"Are you coming?" she whispered.

"Where?"

"To the mound." She shifted the rucksack. "Grandmother says it's the right time. The moon's dark. The old ones listen better when nobody's watching."

"That's not real."

"Then you don't have anything to be scared of." She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "Come on, Linnea. You wanted to know things."

I went.

* * *

The burial mound sat on a low ridge a kilometre past the church, its grass grown thick and wild, its top ringed with a circle of grey stones so old they looked more like bone than rock. Astrid had brought a bundle of juniper, a bottle of water, two candles and a box of matches, and a small brass bowl that she set carefully in the centre of the stones.

"You have to sit in the middle," she said. "Facing north. Don't speak until the sun comes up. If you feel something touch you, don't move. Whatever happens, don't get up until it's gone."

"What if it doesn't go?"

"Then you'll find out what you're made of."

She lit the candles. The flames leaned sideways in a breeze I couldn't feel. Astrid sat cross-legged on the other side of the bowl and began to hum, a low, wordless tune that made the hairs on my arms stand up.

I sat. The grass was damp through my jeans. The stones were cold.

For a long time, nothing happened. Then I heard it: a faint rustling, like someone walking through dry leaves a long way off. Astrid's humming didn't change. I kept my eyes on the candle and tried to breathe slowly.

The rustling got closer. It circled the mound. Then it stopped right behind my left shoulder, and I felt something brush the back of my neck, light as a spider's leg, cold as a river stone.

I didn't move. My heart was going so fast I thought I might pass out.

"Astrid," I whispered.

She didn't answer. She was staring past me, her face pale in the candlelight, her lips still moving around the tune. Her eyes were wide and fixed, and for the first time I understood that she was not, in fact, in control of this.

The candle flames went out, all at once, with a soft exhalation.

In the total dark, a voice spoke from the stones. It was soft and old and did not sound like anything human, and it said a single word I couldn't understand, but which filled my chest with a grief so sudden and total that I started crying without knowing why.

When the sun came up, two hours later, the mound was just a mound. The grass was flattened in a rough circle around us. Astrid was asleep against a stone, her cheek pressed to the cold rock. The brass bowl was empty. Nothing else.

* * *

I've spent a long time since then trying to decide what I saw. Whether it was the cold, or the juniper smoke, or two girls scaring each other half to death in a field. Whether the whisper was a trick of the wind or the wind itself. Astrid never talked about it again, and her grandmother only looked at me when I came to the cottage that autumn and said, quietly, _you went up the mound. Did it answer?_

I didn't know what to say. I still don't.

We moved away the next spring. I never saw Astrid again. But sometimes, when the sun doesn't quite set in the long northern summer and the air goes thick and blue, I find myself humming a tune I can't remember learning, and I wonder what I was listening for, and whether it's still listening back.

## 11. Therapeutic Boundaries

Okay, so the first thing you should know is that my left hamstring is a liar and always has been. It tells you it's fine right up until the moment you put weight on it, and then it screams like a banshee in a hospital corridor. I learned that at nineteen, when I tore it for the third time in a single season, and I learned it again at thirty-one, when the surgeon told me my career was over and I should probably start thinking about "lifestyle management."

Lifestyle management. I wanted to throw my coffee at him.

So now I'm lying on a treatment table in a bright, slightly bleached clinic in Leeds, wearing shorts and a T-shirt I should've thrown out ten years ago, staring at a ceiling tile with a water stain shaped like a bird, and waiting for Dani Okafor to walk in and put her hands on me.

She's not Dani, of course. She's Dr. Danielle Okafor, Chartered Physiotherapist, MSc Sports Rehabilitation. But I knew her when she was a twenty-year-old middle-distance runner with a terrible haircut and a laugh that carried across the whole track. We ran together for two seasons at the national centre. She was the one who pushed me to do the extra sets. She was also the one who cried with me when I got the call about the selection squad, and then held my hand while I threw up in a bin behind the physio room.

She's the reason I'm here. She's also the reason I can't stop thinking about the way her hair looks in the strip lights.

The door clicks open.

"Right, Marcus," she says, flipping a clipboard against her hip. "Let's see what the hamstring's been up to."

"Hello to you too."

"You're in a mood."

"I'm always in a mood."

"Yeah, well." She sets the clipboard down and pulls on a pair of blue nitrile gloves with a snap that makes me flinch. "Today you're going to be a mood with a range of motion. Roll onto your front. Slowly."

* * *

I roll. The paper sheet crinkles under me. Her hands settle on my calf, cool through the gloves, and I force my breathing to stay even. This is the part I hate most: the first touch, when the body remembers what it used to be and starts writing the list of everything it can't do anymore.

"Okay," she says, and I feel her thumbs find the muscle belly just above the knee. "Tell me when it hurts."

"Everything hurts."

"Be specific."

"The back of the thigh. Halfway up. There's a knot like a walnut."

"Good." Her thumbs press in, slow and deliberate, working outward from the centre of the knot. Pain flares, bright and sour, and I grit my teeth. "That's the adhesion. It's from the scar tissue. You didn't stretch it properly after the last repair."

"Because I was busy being a professional athlete."

"Because you were busy being a stubborn idiot." Her voice is level, unbothered. "Now bend the knee for me. Slowly. Heel toward your backside. Stop when you feel the pull."

I bend. The hamstring tightens, a steel cable under my skin. Pain blooms like an ink stain, and I hiss through my teeth.

"There," she says. "That's the edge. Hold it."

Her hand moves to my ankle. Her other hand settles flat on the back of my thigh, just above the knot, and she applies a gentle, steady pressure that pushes the leg a few degrees further. The sensation is intense and nearly unbearable, a dense, electric ache that spreads from the muscle into the bone.

I can feel her breathing. I can feel the faint shift of her weight as she leans in. I can feel how carefully she's not looking at my face.

"Breathe out," she says quietly. "Let it go."

I breathe out. The ache dulls by a fraction. She eases the pressure, then adds a little more, and I feel my body respond, the muscle fibres loosening one by one like the strings of an old guitar.

"Good," she murmurs. "You're doing well."

"You say that to everyone."

"I don't. I say it to people who actually listen." Her thumbs find the knot again. "Like you used to."

* * *

The next thing she does is harder: a passive straight-leg raise, her hand cupped under my heel, lifting the leg in a slow arc until the hamstring starts to sing. I can feel the tension in my pelvis, in the small muscles of my lower back, in every stubborn fibre that refuses to let go.

"Stop," I say, when the pain climbs too high.

She stops immediately. "Okay?"

"Yeah."

"Good. We'll come back to it." She lowers the leg with the same care she used lifting it, cradling the heel until the foot touches the table without a jolt. "That was about forty degrees. Your baseline is around sixty. We've got a long way to go, but you're already more mobile than you were last month."

"That's not much of a compliment."

"It's not supposed to be a compliment. It's a fact." She glances up, and for the first time today our eyes meet. Her gaze is steady and unsentimental, but there's something under it that makes my stomach tighten. "And you're not allowed to hate it so much."

"I hate it because it's you doing it."

Her mouth tightens. "Marcus."

"I'm sorry." I stare at the ceiling. "It's just weird. Being on this side of the table."

"I know." She picks up her clipboard again, and the moment passes, carefully, like a glass set down before it can break. "Let's do the glute bridges. Then we'll talk about your insurance paperwork, because I know you've been avoiding it."

* * *

By the time she finishes, my leg feels like it has been turned inside out and carefully stitched back into place. She stands at the foot of the table, pulling off her gloves with a snap, and I sit up slowly, wincing.

"Next week, same time," she says. "And Marcus?"

"Yeah?"

"You're going to be okay. Not the same. But okay." She hesitates, and then, very quietly: "I've always thought you'd find something else. Something that makes you as happy as running used to."

"I don't think I'm capable of being happy about anything that doesn't involve breaking a tape at the end of a race."

"Yeah," she says softly. "That was always your problem."

She turns to leave. At the door, she stops, one hand on the frame, and looks back at me with a expression I can't read.

"For what it's worth," she says. "I still think about those two seasons. More than I should."

The door swings shut behind her.

I lie back on the table and stare at the water stain shaped like a bird, and my hamstring throbs in time with my heartbeat, and I feel, for the first time in years, the old familiar ache of wanting something I can't have.

## 12. High Seas and Low Vices

The Canton waterfront in the monsoon season smelt of wet timber, rotting fruit, and the sweet, cloying smoke that rose from every doorway like a prayer nobody meant. Lieutenant James Merrick had long since stopped noticing it. He noticed the dragon, though. The dragon was always there, curled behind his ribs, waiting for the first bell of the morning.

"You're late, Merrick," said Captain Fenwick, from the quarterdeck of the _Thalia_, not troubling to look up from his ledgers. "The Admiral wants the stores counted by noon."

"I'm sure the stores will survive another hour, sir."

"Will you?"

Merrick touched his cap, smiled as pleasantly as he could manage, and walked down the gangway with his hands in his pockets and his face arranged into the expression of a man with nowhere in particular to be. The guards at the dock nodded him through. They knew him. Everyone in Canton knew the handsome young lieutenant with the sunburnt nose and the unsteady hands, and nobody asked where he went when he wasn't on duty.

I was a decorated officer once, he thought. I had a letter from the Admiralty and a medal from Navarino, and my mother wrote to me every fortnight about the roses. Now I count stores that nobody checks, and I drink with men I wouldn't have spoken to at Harrow, and I buy my peace from the Hong merchants by the ounce.

* * *

The first stop was Ah Lam's teahouse on the Thirteen Factories road, where the windows were papered over with yellowing advertisements for English sherry, and the back room smelt of sandalwood and something sour underneath.

"Lieutenant." Ah Lam bowed, a thin, neat man with a pipe stuck behind one ear. "You are early today."

"I'm always early, Lam. It's the only thing I'm good at."

"You are good at many things," Lam said, not unkindly. "You simply do not enjoy them."

He led Merrick through a curtain of beaded bamboo into a room with three cushioned couches, a low lacquered table, and a lamp burning in a sealed glass box. A man lay on the nearest couch, eyes closed, face slack. Merrick felt the familiar tug in his chest, half hunger and half shame.

"Not today," he said quietly. "I have business with the Hoppo's clerk."

"Business," Lam repeated, with a thin smile. "Yes. The clerk is also in need of... patience."

"Then I'll be patient. But I'm not paying for the bill until tomorrow."

"Of course not." Lam poured him a cup of green tea. "The bill is always tomorrow, Lieutenant. That is why we are such good friends."

* * *

By noon Merrick had sold three navy-issue lanterns to a Portuguese chandler, a crate of salt pork to a boatman who swore he would deliver it to a Macanese widow, and a letter of introduction he had forged himself to a rich silk factor who did not read English. The money went into his pocket with a satisfying weight. It would buy him four hours of nothing.

He found Yuen Sheng in the warehouse near the old Dutch factory, sitting on a stack of tea chests and eating sugared plums from a paper cone.

"Merrick!" Yuen called, bright and cheerful. "You look terrible. Eat a plum."

"I'll take the plum. Not the lecture."

"The lecture is free, Merrick. Only the poppy is expensive." Yuen grinned, showing a row of gold-capped teeth. He was a clever, cruel little man, Merrick thought, but honest in his way: he did not pretend the trade was anything but what it was. "You owe me forty-two dollars. And a favour."

"What favour?"

"A man in the Hong Merchants' Guild wants a ship's manifest. Your ship's manifest. The one that lists the guns."

Merrick stopped chewing. The plum turned to ash on his tongue. "You're mad."

"I'm practical. The Guild pays three hundred. You pay me forty-two, and the rest I keep for my trouble." Yuen spread his hands. "It's not treason, Merrick. It's commerce. The British sell Lin the opium, Lin sells it to the mandarins, and nobody in London cares until the war starts. Then it's all very dramatic."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll be standing in the dock in front of your Admiral explaining why you've been selling lanterns to the Portuguese." Yuen's smile did not waver. "Think about it, my friend. Think about your mother's roses."

* * *

The afternoon was slow and hot and smelt of diesel from the steamers moored in the harbour. Merrick walked along the river wall with the sun on his neck and the sweat soaking through his shirt, and he thought, not for the first time, about the night at Navarino when the cannon fire had split the sky open and he had felt, for one bright, terrible moment, entirely alive.

Now he could barely feel his hands.

He met a girl near the Fat Man's Tavern, one of the Macanese women who worked the back rooms for British officers with money and no sense. She was perhaps seventeen, with a face like a moon and a laugh like a bell.

"You're the Lieutenant with the roses," she said, in careful English. "My friend says you talk about flowers when you are drunk."

"I talk about many things when I'm drunk."

"Do you want to talk now?"

"No." He gave her a coin, and she took it without looking, the way she took everything. "Just tell Yuen the forty-two dollars will be ready by tomorrow."

"He will say it is always tomorrow."

"He's right," Merrick said. "It always is."

* * *

By dusk he was sitting in Ah Lam's back room again, a thin lacquer tray balanced on his knees, the lamp burning low. The dragon had uncurled at last, and its breath was warm and heavy and entirely merciful. He closed his eyes.

Through the bamboo curtain, he heard Captain Fenwick's voice, faint and far away, reading the list of stores aloud to the clerk on the quarterdeck of the _Thalia_. Stores counted. Lieutenant Merrick, absent. Lieutenant Merrick, as always.

_Tomorrow,_ he thought. _It's always tomorrow._

He opened his eyes and reached for the pipe.

## 13. Sisyphus Gives Advice

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_My husband and I have been married nineteen years. For the last three, we have been having the same argument about whose turn it is to empty the dishwasher. I know this is ridiculous. He knows it's ridiculous. Neither of us will stop. How do I make it stop without losing?_

_— Dishwasher Dilemma, Leeds_

* * *

_Dear Dishwasher,_

_I've had a fair bit of time to think about arguments that don't end, so I'll tell you what I've noticed. The dishwasher is never really about the dishwasher. It's a small, safe place to keep a bigger feeling in: "I feel like I'm carrying more than you," or "I don't feel seen," or just "I'm tired." Those are much harder to say out loud, so you say the dishes instead._

_Try this. Next time the argument starts, don't defend your turn. Ask him what he thinks the argument is about. Then listen to the answer for longer than feels comfortable. Nineteen years is a long time to be right about a dishwasher. You can afford to be wrong about it once._

_— S._

_P.S. Crow says to tell you the dishwasher in our kitchen has been broken since 1987, and we just pile things on top and hope._

* * *

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_I've been offered a promotion that would mean moving to a city where I don't know anyone. My mum is ill and I'm her only child. I don't know whether to take it. Everyone says I should go for it. I feel sick._

_— Torn in Glasgow_

* * *

_Dear Torn,_

_Everyone who tells you to go for it is talking about the promotion. You're talking about your mum. Those are different conversations, and the second one is the one you need to have first._

_Ring her tonight. Don't ask her about the job. Ask her what she's afraid of. Then ask her what she'd want you to do if you were the one who was ill. You may find she's already thought about this more carefully than you have, and she's been waiting for you to bring it up._

_Whatever you decide, the feeling of sickness is telling you something true. Listen to it, but don't let it make the choice for you. You're allowed to take the promotion and still visit every other weekend. You're also allowed to say no. Both are a kind of love._

_— S._

_P.S. I push a boulder up a hill for a living. I have been told by a great many people that I'm doing it wrong. I've found the only thing that helps is to keep pushing and let them talk._

* * *

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_My manager takes credit for my work in every meeting. I've tried raising it, but she just smiles and says she "loves how we work as a team." I'm exhausted. Do I quit? Do I fight? Am I being paranoid?_

_— Invisible in Reading_

* * *

_Dear Invisible,_

_You are probably not being paranoid. You are, however, probably making the mistake of raising it in the wrong room._

_Put it in writing. Not an accusation, just a record: "Following up on the Q3 report, I wanted to confirm that the revised forecast model was my work, as discussed." Send it after the meeting, not during it. Copy her manager if you have one. Most people who take credit for things are counting on the fact that nobody will ever ask who did what. A calm email makes that harder._

_And start building a folder. Dates, drafts, emails. It's not for a fight. It's so that when you do decide to leave, you leave with your own name on the work._

_— S._

_P.S. Crow wants you to know that the best way to get credit for something is to be the one who keeps the minutes._

* * *

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_I'm 34 and I've been asking myself what my life is for. I'm good at my job, I have friends, I'm not unhappy, but I keep thinking that I'm just going through the motions. Is this normal? Or is something wrong with me?_

_— Motions, Cardiff_

* * *

_Dear Motions,_

_Nothing is wrong with you. Most people spend a lot of their lives going through motions. The question isn't whether you're doing it; it's whether any of the motions are yours._

_Look at your week. Find one small thing you do that you'd do even if nobody were watching. It might be cooking a particular dinner, walking a particular route, reading a particular kind of book. Do more of that. Not as a grand project, just as a few extra minutes here and there._

_Purpose, in my experience, is rarely a single bright thing you discover in a flash. It's usually a series of ordinary choices that you keep making until you look up and realise you've been living in the same direction for years._

_— S._

_P.S. I've been rolling the same rock for three thousand years. Some days it's exhausting. Some days I stop halfway up and look at the view, and it's the best part of my week._

* * *

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_My teenage son has stopped talking to me. He spends all his time in his room. I'm terrified I've done something wrong. How do I get him back?_

_— Worried Mum, Bristol_

* * *

_Dear Worried Mum,_

_You probably haven't done something wrong. Teenagers go quiet for lots of reasons, most of them having nothing to do with you. What he needs is not for you to fix his silence but to make it safe for him to come out of it on his own schedule._

_Stop asking him how he is. Start doing something alongside him instead. Drive him somewhere. Cook a meal together. Sit in the same room while you read. When he does talk, don't rush to respond with a solution. Just say, "Hm. That sounds hard," and wait._

_And if the silence goes on for a long time, or if you're worried about his safety, talk to his school or his GP. You don't have to carry this by yourself._

_— S._

_P.S. Crow says teenage boys are like hedgehogs. They look prickly, but if you sit very still and keep the biscuits out, they'll come close eventually._

* * *

_Dear Sisyphus,_

_I think my neighbour is stealing my post. Should I confront her?_

_— Suspicious in Swindon_

* * *

_Dear Suspicious,_

_Before you confront anyone, check whether the postman is delivering to the wrong house. It's surprisingly common, and it's much less awkward to discover that over a cup of tea than in a doorway with your arms folded._

_If it's still happening after that, have a friendly chat. "Hi, I think a few of my letters might have ended up at yours. Have you noticed?" Give her the benefit of the doubt. People are very rarely villains. Usually they're just absent-minded, or lonely, or both._

_— S._

_P.S. Crow is currently wearing a lost cardigan belonging to a Mrs. Patel from number nine. We're returning it this afternoon. He is not sorry._

* * *

_\[Interjection: Sisyphus pauses, wipes his brow, and eyes the boulder at the bottom of the hill. It has rolled back a few feet during the post-lunch letter session. Crow flaps down, perches on the stone, and waits.\]_

_"Right," Sisyphus says. "Back to work."_

_He sets his shoulder against the rock. It's cold and rough and very heavy. He takes a breath and begins to push._

_Behind him, the first letter of the afternoon is already waiting, folded neatly on Crow's back._

## 14. Lost & Found in Osaka

"Is that a Roland? The SH-101?"

The question came out of Mio in a rush, too loud, and she immediately wanted to crawl into the nearest bin of second-hand cables. She was standing in front of a glass display case on the third floor of a shop in Den Den Town, pressed so close to the glass that her breath fogged it, and a girl with a battered Strat slung across her back was staring at her as if she had just sneezed a chord.

"Yeah," the girl said. "You know the SH-101?"

"I — yes. Sorry. I didn't mean to stare."

"You're not staring. You're drooling." The girl's grin was quick and crooked. "It's okay. I drool at Rolands too. Want to hear it?"

"Hear what?"

"The Roland. I'm going to plug into the amp by the register and play something. Shop owner hates it. Totally worth it." She was already moving, shoving past a shelf of cheap keyboard covers.

Mio followed, mostly because her feet were moving before her brain caught up.

* * *

The amp was a tangle of old black cables and cracked tolex. The girl plugged in the Strat, tweaked a knob with the confidence of someone who had done this a hundred times, and then hit a chord so loud and thick that the entire floor seemed to tilt.

Mio flinched and grabbed the edge of the display case.

"Sorry!" The girl was laughing, her shoulders shaking. "I should've warned you. I'm Haruka, by the way. Or Haru. Nobody calls me Haruka except my mum when she's angry."

"Mio," Mio said, very quietly. "I'm — I'm sorry, I'm not very good at talking to people."

"You're doing fine. You just said a full sentence."

"That's a low bar."

"Yeah, but you cleared it, so you get a prize." Haru picked up a stray plectrum from the floor and held it out. "Here. Lucky charm. Now tell me what you were looking at, because your face was doing something very serious."

Mio stared at the plectrum. Then she pointed at the SH-101, sitting on a shelf behind the glass like a sleeping bird. "That synth. It's from 1982. It's got a sawtooth wave that sounds like a cat being gently mugged by a bee. I've been saving up for a year to buy one, but they're so expensive, and I don't even have a proper amp, and —"

She stopped, horrified. She had talked for almost ten seconds without pausing. Her cheeks were burning.

Haru's eyes went wide. "Oh my god. You're a nerd."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. I love this. I've been trying to find someone who knows what a sawtooth is for three months." Haru leaned against the counter. "Do you know what a Jupiter-8 sounds like? Because I think it's the best thing ever made, and everyone I know just looks at me like I'm weird."

Mio's throat tightened. "The Jupiter-8 is perfect," she said, very softly. "It's warm. Like being wrapped in a blanket that's also on fire."

Haru laughed, loud and delighted. "Yes! Exactly! Oh, I like you. You're going to be really annoying to hang out with, and I'm going to love it."

* * *

They stayed in the shop for an hour, sampling keyboards and arguing about filter resonance and whether the Juno-106 had been overrated. Mio ran out of things to say about twice and had to stop herself from apologising every time she spoke. Haru kept telling her not to, and kept asking more questions, and every time Mio answered one, Haru's face lit up like she'd just won a prize at a fair.

"You know what I want?" Haru said eventually, leaning on the counter and drumming her fingers on the glass. "I want to play a riff so heavy that it sounds like a building collapsing in slow motion, and then I want to play a lullaby on the same guitar, and I want nobody to be able to tell the difference."

"That's impossible."

"Yeah. That's why I'm doing it."

Mio looked at her. Haru's face was open and fierce and entirely unselfconscious, and something in Mio's chest that had been clenched for months loosened by a fraction.

"I think you'd be good at it," Mio said.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. You play like you're not scared of anything."

"I'm scared of everything," Haru said, and grinned. "But that's a secret. Don't tell the shop owner."

* * *

When the shop lights began to dim and the owner started pointedly sweeping near their feet, Haru stretched and looked at her watch.

"I'm starving," she said. "You hungry?"

"I — I should get home. My mum will —"

"Sushi." Haru tapped her nose. "There's a kaitenzushi around the corner. The plates go round on a conveyor belt, and you can grab whatever looks good. It's the best thing ever invented, next to the Jupiter-8. Come on. My treat."

Mio hesitated. Her instinct screamed at her to say no, to walk away, to go back to her room and her headphones and her careful, quiet life. But Haru was already at the door, holding it open, waiting with that same crooked grin.

"Okay," Mio said, and her voice came out small and surprised. "But I'm not very good at sushi."

"Nobody is. That's why we're going to eat it wrong together."

* * *

The restaurant was loud and bright and smelled of vinegar and green tea. Plates of salmon and tuna and egg rolled past on the belt, and Haru reached out and snatched three at once, grinning like a thief.

"Try the eel," she said. "It's the only good thing on this belt."

Mio tried the eel. It was sweet and soft and perfect, and she said so, and Haru punched the air like a footballer who'd just scored.

They talked through three plates, then five, then eight. Haru told her about a band she'd been in with two boys from her school who had both quit the moment a music teacher said the word "jazz." Mio told her about the synth she'd built out of a broken toy keyboard when she was twelve. Haru laughed so hard at that story that she choked on her green tea, and Mio, horrified, started patting her back.

"I'm fine," Haru wheezed, wiping her eyes. "I'm fine. That's the best thing I've ever heard."

"It wasn't meant to be funny."

"That's what makes it funny."

Mio grinned, and it felt strange on her face, and then it felt right.

Later, on the train back toward Umeda, Haru leaned her head against the window, eyes half-closed, her Strat case propped between her knees.

"Hey, Mio?"

"Yeah?"

"Can I come and see your synth? The broken-toy one?"

Mio's heart did something strange and warm. "It's not very good."

"I don't care. I want to hear what a sawtooth sounds like when it's been through a lot."

Mio looked out at the lights of the city, sliding past like sparks. For the first time in months, she was not thinking about what she should have said. She was thinking about what she wanted to say next.

"Yes," she said. "Come on Saturday."

"Deal," said Haru, and closed her eyes with a small, satisfied sigh, and the train carried them both on through the gold and violet dusk of Osaka.

## 15. The Watch That Remembered

Ollie Park had wanted a smartwatch for nearly two years, and when his aunt finally bought him one for his twelfth birthday, he wore it everywhere, even in the shower, which his mum said was disgusting. It was silver with a blue face and a button on the side that made a faint click when you pressed it. The box said _ChronoMate 3: Sync Your Life_, and the instructions had a picture of a clock running backwards.

"It's a joke," said his friend Dev, peering at the screen during lunch. "Nobody can rewind time."

"It says it can," Ollie said. "Look. There's a setting called Recall. Hold the button for three seconds and it goes back thirty minutes."

"Prove it."

So Ollie did. He held the button. The screen flickered, the blue face blinked, and suddenly the cafeteria clock was showing 12:14 again instead of 12:44, and Dev was reaching for a crisp he'd already eaten.

"Okay," Dev said slowly. "That's weird."

* * *

For a week, it was the best thing that had ever happened to Ollie. He rewound the time before a pop quiz and aced it. He rewound before a fight with his older sister and told her exactly the right thing to make her laugh. He rewound after he accidentally knocked over a glass of orange juice on the school library's only copy of _The Hobbit_, and the book came out perfectly dry.

But by the second Thursday, he started noticing things.

He was taller. Not much, but enough that his trousers were too short at the ankle. His voice had dropped, cracking on the word "hello" in the corridor and making Dev snort. There was a patch of hair on his chin that hadn't been there at breakfast. His mum looked at him across the dinner table with a funny expression, like she was trying to remember which boy he'd been last week.

"Ollie," she said. "How old are you?"

"Twelve. Obviously."

"Hm. You look older."

"I've been eating a lot of protein."

She didn't laugh.

* * *

He checked the watch's settings that night, in bed, under his duvet with a torch. The Recall function had a little counter at the bottom of the screen. It said _Uses: 31_. He stared at it. He'd used it maybe twelve times in total. Thirty-one didn't make sense.

Then he saw the second line, tiny and grey: _Each Recall advances biological clock by 2 days._

Ollie's stomach dropped through the mattress.

He tried to remember the last time he'd rewound. Then he tried to remember what he'd done during the rewinds. And he realised, with a jolt that made his hands shake, that he'd been using the watch to fix every small mistake he'd ever made for the last month. Forgetting homework. Being rude to the dinner lady. Losing the tennis match.

He'd been growing up in the wrong direction, two days at a time.

* * *

The next morning, Dev met him at the bus stop and nearly walked straight past him.

"Ollie? Oh my god. You look like my cousin's dad."

"I know."

"Are you ill?"

"I'm — no. It's the watch." Ollie pulled his sleeve up, showing the screen. "It's making me older. Every time I use it."

Dev stared. "That's insane."

"I know. And I can't stop, because when I don't rewind, I feel like I'm going to say something stupid."

"Ollie, you're twelve."

"I know. That's the problem."

* * *

At school, something else had changed. Nobody looked at him the way they used to. A girl from his class, Priya, smiled at him in the corridor in a slightly flustered way that made his cheeks burn. The PE teacher called him "sir" by accident. The lunch lady gave him an extra slice of pizza without being asked.

He hated it. He hated that people were treating him like someone he wasn't. And he hated the watch for making him this person, even though some tiny part of him liked the feeling of being taller, of being noticed.

At break, he went to the science block to find Mr. Ramsay, the only teacher who had ever seemed to understand that Ollie was thinking about things other people weren't.

Ramsay listened, grey-eyed and silent, while Ollie explained the whole thing. When he was done, the teacher picked up the watch, turned it over, and frowned at the tiny print.

"Hm," he said. "This looks like a time-series algorithm. Someone's really built a thing into the core. It's not just rewinding your watch — it's rewinding _you_." He handed it back. "Ollie. Did you rewind anything that mattered? Something big?"

Ollie's mouth went dry. "Yesterday. At the bus stop. Dev and I had a fight. I rewound so I wouldn't have to say sorry."

"And did you?"

"No. I just rewound so I wouldn't have to think about it."

Ramsay nodded slowly. "Then you've changed the timeline a little. Small things, probably. But you can't skip the hard bits and expect the world to stay the same."

* * *

That was the night it went wrong.

Ollie lay awake, the watch on the bedside table, its screen dark. He couldn't stop thinking about the fight with Dev. About how Dev had said _you're not even trying anymore_, and how Ollie had said something cruel back, and how neither of them had known what to do after that.

He pressed the button.

The room blurred. His stomach lurched. The clock on the wall jumped backward, and suddenly it was Tuesday morning, the sun was coming through the curtains in the wrong direction, and his mum was standing in the kitchen doorway, holding a cup of tea and staring at him with a face he didn't recognise.

"Ollie," she said. "Who are you?"

He looked down. His hands were smaller. His voice came out high and childish, the way it had been a month ago.

And beside him, on the kitchen table, sat a photo frame he'd never seen before. In it, his mum was holding a baby. Not Ollie. A baby girl with dark hair and a gap-toothed grin, and a name written underneath in careful pencil: _Maya, 2009._

He had a sister, he realised with a sick lurch. He'd always had a sister. And somewhere in the last thirty rewinds, she had quietly stopped existing.

* * *

He spent the rest of the day tearing through the house, looking for proof. Maya's school photo was gone from the fridge. Her bedroom was a guest room with a single bed and a stack of boxes. His dad, who had been fine that morning, was now a stranger on the phone, asking why Ollie was calling from a number that didn't exist.

By dusk, Ollie was sitting on the bathroom floor with the watch in his hands and tears on his face, staring at the Recall button.

There was only one thing left to do. Go back. Undo the rewind that had erased her. Undo every rewind that had made him older and stranger and lonelier than he'd ever wanted to be.

He pressed the button, three seconds, the same as always.

The room shimmered. The clock ran backward. The light turned gold and then grey, and then Ollie was standing in the hallway, his mum's voice floating up from the kitchen.

"Ollie! Maya! Dinner's ready!"

A girl came thundering down the stairs in a pair of pink wellies and nearly knocked him over. "Ollie!" she yelled, grinning with a gap in her front teeth. "You owe me a game of Snap!"

He caught her and hugged her, very hard. She squeaked in protest.

"Ow! You're weird today."

"I know," Ollie said, into her hair. "I'm sorry."

He didn't touch the watch again that night. He put it in a drawer, under his socks, and didn't look at it for a long time.

Some things, he decided, were better learned slowly.

## 16. Carnival of Dangerous Intentions

Coney Island, July 1937

She stopped just before reaching the shooting gallery, where the boardwalk planks gave way to a pool of trodden sawdust and the lights of the Steeplechase had begun to buzz overhead like a swarm of bright, impatient bees. The air was thick with salt and fried dough and the sweet, sticky scent of candy floss turning to syrup in the heat.

_His locks._

The words came to her again, unbidden, the way an intruder might slip under a half-open door. An old-fashioned phrase, the sort her grandmother would have used. She had heard him say it on the telephone three nights ago, low and amused, when she'd asked him what he thought about her hair. _Your locks,_ he'd said, _are a matter of some interest to me._ She had laughed at the time. She was not laughing now.

She pressed a gloved hand to her throat and made herself look at the crowd.

It was Saturday, and the boardwalk was full of sailors on shore leave, girls in cotton dresses with pinned-up curls, a pair of nervous young men arguing over the price of a stuffed bear. A brass band was playing somewhere near the carousel, the notes bright and brittle. A barker in a striped vest called out the odds on the Wheel of Fortune in a voice like a cracked bell. And at the edge of the crowd, half in the shadow of the gallery's painted sign, a man in a dark suit stood very still and watched her.

She did not look at him. She knew what she would find if she did.

* * *

Her name was Eleanor Hart. She was twenty-three, a secretary at a shipping firm in Manhattan with a fondness for silk stockings and a mother who believed she would marry a bank clerk and live in Queens. She had told her mother she was visiting her aunt in Flatbush. She had not told anyone about the letter, the one that had arrived three days earlier, typed on thick cream paper, promising that the stranger she had met at the Club Savoy would be waiting by the shooting gallery at dusk, _should she wish to know the truth about her family._

The truth about her family. She had laughed at that too, until she realised the letter had been right about the name of her father's firm, and the street of her childhood home, and the colour of the ribbon she had worn at her confirmation.

Now the carnival lights were coming on, and the crowd was thickening, and her pulse was beating a slow, dangerous rhythm in her wrists.

"You came," said a low voice behind her.

She turned.

He was younger than she had imagined, though not much. Perhaps twenty-seven. His hair was a dark, unruly mass of curls, black as ink, falling across a pale brow. His eyes were darker still, the colour of the sea at midnight, and when they found hers, something in her chest folded quietly in on itself.

"I said I would," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had expected.

"You did." He stepped closer, and she caught the faint scent of cedar and cigarettes and something older, like pressed paper in a locked drawer. "I half expected you to bring your mother."

"My mother doesn't know where I am."

"Good." His smile was slow and lazy, and it did not quite reach his eyes. "Some secrets are best kept between two people who understand what they cost."

"And what do they cost?" she asked.

He tilted his head, considering her. "Everything, Miss Hart. Usually the most interesting things do."

* * *

They walked along the boardwalk together, the sea wind pulling at her skirt, the band's music fading behind them into the roar of the rides. He did not offer his arm, and she did not take it. They moved in a careful silence, a foot apart, while the crowd flowed around them like water around stones.

"You're nervous," he observed.

"I'm curious."

"Mm. Curiosity is a dangerous thing in a woman." He glanced sideways at her, eyes glinting. "It led Eve into a great deal of trouble."

"Eve was given an apple. I've been given an envelope."

"Ah. A more practical temptation." He reached into his jacket and withdrew a small, plain photograph, face down. He held it out, between two fingers, but did not let her take it. "Do you know this house?"

She turned it over.

The photograph showed a narrow clapboard house with a green door and a cracked front step, the kind of house that looked very much like the one she had grown up in, though she had not seen it in fifteen years. In the window, half-hidden by a lace curtain, a woman with dark hair was staring out at the camera with an expression of pure, undisguised terror.

Eleanor's breath caught.

"That's my mother," she whispered. "She died when I was seven."

"Did she?" His voice was soft. "I wonder."

"What do you mean?"

He took the photograph back, slipped it into his jacket, and leaned in until his lips were almost at her ear. The warmth of him, the faint scent of cedar, made her knees go loose.

"I mean," he murmured, "that you're not the first girl in your family to come looking for the truth at a carnival. And you won't be the last to pay for it."

She pulled back sharply, her heart thundering. The man was watching her with an expression she could not read, a mixture of hunger and something dangerously close to sorrow.

"Who are you?" she demanded.

"A man who knows what you are." He smiled, slow and secret, and reached out to tuck a loose strand of her hair behind her ear, his fingers grazing her cheek with a touch as light as frost. "And a man who would very much like to know what you intend to do next, Eleanor Hart."

* * *

Behind them, the carousel began to turn. Its painted horses rose and fell in stately, mechanical rhythm, and the brass organ played a waltz so sweet it seemed to belong to another century entirely. Somewhere in the crowd, a woman laughed, high and brittle, and the sound was swallowed by the sea.

Eleanor looked at the stranger, at the dark curls and the midnight eyes, and felt the familiar, reckless pull of a door swinging open in a house she had been told was locked.

"Tell me the truth," she said.

He smiled, and it was the most dangerous thing she had ever seen.

"Gladly," he said. "But you'll have to stay for the waltz."

## 17. Caribou Season at the Lodge

JT knew something was wrong the moment the truck crested the last rise and the lodge came into view below them. It looked exactly as it always had: cedar shingles bleached silver by forty winters, the porch sagging a little on the left where the frost had heaved the footings, the chimney leaning like an old man trying to hear a conversation. But the air coming through the cracked window felt thick, like the woods had leaned in closer overnight, the way a crowd does when something is about to happen.

"You're quiet," said Mike, beside him in the cab, his elbow out the window.

"Tired."

"Yeah. Me too." Mike didn't look over. He was twenty-six and built like a fence post, and he had the kind of easy certainty that JT had envied since he was small. "Dad's already got the stove going. Bet the coffee's terrible."

"Bet it is."

In the back of the truck, under a tarp, the rifles lay wrapped in oily canvas, rattling faintly every time the wheels hit a rut. JT could hear them even over the engine. A small, stubborn sound, like something knocking to be let out.

* * *

The first caribou came on the second morning.

They had walked out before dawn, the frost crunching under their boots, the sky the colour of wet slate. JT was trailing, as usual. He'd been trailing since he was nine, when his father had told him to keep up and he had simply never managed it. Dad and Mike were ahead, their breath clouding in the cold, their silhouettes sharp against the pale light.

The animal stood at the edge of a stand of spruce, grey-brown and enormous, its antlers spreading like a crown of bare branches. It didn't run when Dad raised the rifle. It didn't run when Mike whispered, "Okay, steady." It just turned its head slowly and looked at them, its ears flicking lazily, as though it had been waiting all morning for someone to finally show up.

"Take it," Dad said quietly.

The shot cracked across the clearing. The caribou folded at the knees, almost gracefully, and sank into the snow with a long, soft exhale.

JT felt something in his gut turn over, slow and cold, like a fish rolling in deep water. The way it had fallen. Not thrashing. Not frightened. Dignified, almost. As if it had wanted to lie down there all along.

Nobody else seemed to notice. Dad clapped Mike on the shoulder and started talking about the butchering. JT kept his mouth shut. It was always safer that way.

* * *

The clearing was the third day.

He had fallen behind again. His bootlaces had frozen stiff overnight, and by the time he'd fought them loose and stood up, the others had already vanished into the trees. JT moved quickly, instinctively, his breath coming in short, sharp bursts. Then he slowed, because something in the air had changed.

The sound was gone. The wind had stopped. Even the birds had gone quiet.

He crept through a tangle of brush and looked out into the open space beyond.

A buck stood there, utterly still. It was the biggest animal he had ever seen, its antlers wide and pale as bone, its flanks rising and falling in slow, deep breaths. It didn't look at JT. It looked past him, toward the spot where his father and brother were standing at the edge of the clearing, rifles hanging forgotten at their sides.

The buck's eyes were dark and flat and very calm. They made the back of JT's neck prickle with cold.

His father raised one hand. Slowly, almost reverently, like someone approaching a sleeping child, he reached toward the buck's muzzle.

JT's breath stopped.

His father's face was wrong. The eyes were too bright, the lips parted as if on the verge of a whisper. Mike stood beside him with the same dazed expression, swaying faintly, as if he were listening to music nobody else could hear.

_Look away,_ JT's mind screamed. _Turn around. Run._

He didn't move.

Then the buck flinched. It shuddered, its great head snapping around toward the trees, and in one fluid motion it leapt into the brush and vanished without a sound.

The clearing was empty. The wind came back, rattling the spruce.

His father stood with his hand still raised, staring at the empty snow. Then he dropped it to his side, shook his head like a man waking from a bad dream, and turned to Mike.

"Did you see that?" Dad said.

"See what?" said Mike.

"Nothing." Dad's voice was rough. "Let's go back."

* * *

JT waited until their footsteps faded before he stepped out of the brush. His knees were shaking. He stared at the spot where the buck had stood, at the flattened snow and the faint, dark trail of something that might have been a drop of blood or might have been shadow.

Had it happened? He didn't know. He couldn't tell anymore whether he'd seen a miracle, or a hallucination, or simply a long, quiet morning in the woods that had got inside his head.

That night, he lay awake in the narrow bunk under the eaves, listening to the wind worry at the shingles. Downstairs, his father was laughing at something Mike had said. The sound was warm and familiar, and JT felt, suddenly and sharply, that he had never actually known either of them at all.

Something scratched at the window. Gently, patiently. Like antlers against glass.

JT didn't get up to look.

In the morning, the frost had cleared from the glass, and the snow outside was unmarked all the way to the treeline. Except for one set of tracks, broad and deep, leading from the porch steps to the edge of the woods, and stopping there.

Nobody mentioned them. Nobody ever mentioned anything.

But when the coffee was poured, Dad looked up at JT across the kitchen table with a strange, bright, faraway expression, and smiled very softly, and said, "Good boy. You kept quiet."

JT didn't answer. He took his cup and held it in both hands, and tried very hard not to notice that it was warm.

## 18. Hell Is Other Demons

The alarm in Lucifer's study went off at eleven, which was the only way anyone in Hell had ever been able to tell morning from evening. It was a small brass bell that had once rung in the cathedral of Notre-Dame and now hung beside the cocktail cabinet, next to a framed photograph of a dog that had died in 1622.

"Fourteen minutes past eleven," said Lucifer, to nobody. "Right on schedule."

He lay in the wreckage of a bed the size of a small country, his black silk pyjamas twisted around one leg, one wing draped over the side like a dropped umbrella. The other wing had been caught in the bedsheet and was now stuck fast. He sighed and tugged it free with a grunt.

Beside him, a shape stirred beneath the duvet. A long, pale arm slid out, followed by a dark curtain of hair and the faint, unmistakable scent of brimstone and expensive cologne.

"You're awake," said Lilith.

"I am always awake. It's my worst feature."

"It's your only feature, darling. The rest is just bad posture." She sat up, yawned, and reached for a cigarette from the bedside table. "Did you sleep at all?"

"Two hours. I dreamed about the Mariana Trench."

"How revolting. Who was in it?"

"Nobody. It was very quiet and very cold and I was looking for my keys." He sat up, painfully aware that his left wing was now entirely trapped under the bedframe. "I think it means I'm lonely."

Lilith raised one perfect eyebrow. "You're the Lord of Lies. Do you really think it's a dream?"

"It's a sign," he said. "I have a sign on my desk that says _Abandon all hope._ I think it's time someone took it seriously."

* * *

The day began, as always, with the paperwork.

The Department of Eternal Damnation had sent up nine thousand three hundred and twelve pleas for clemency, twelve complaints about the quality of the lava in Circle Five, and a formal letter from the Board of Torment requesting permission to install a new rack in the Hall of Sloth.

"Permission denied," Lucifer muttered, scrawling his signature across the bottom of the letter. "The Sloth need to suffer in comfort. It's the whole point."

"You can't deny a rack," said Lilith, reading over his shoulder while she painted her nails a deep and vaguely menacing shade of plum. "It's the Board's jurisdiction. You know how they get."

"They're fallen angels with a passion for upholstery. I'm the bloody Devil. I deny what I like." He signed another form. "Also I've been meaning to ask. Did you enjoy the thing with the Duke of Venice last night?"

Lilith smiled slowly. "The Duke was a bore. He wanted to talk about his cathedral. I took his soul and his cufflinks and left him in a cellar full of rats. It was a lovely evening."

"That's disgusting."

"It's _efficient_." She blew on her nails. "You'd know, if you ever came to a party."

"I hate parties."

"You hate everyone, darling. It's one of your more endearing traits." She set the brush down and looked at him, her expression unreadable. "Are we still doing the thing where you pretend you didn't miss me?"

"I didn't miss you."

"Liar."

"Fine." He stared at the paperwork. "I missed you. It's very annoying. I've been wandering around the Ninth Circle every evening for a month, waiting for you to show up and throw something at me."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

* * *

The afternoon brought the usual parade: a mid-level sinner from Wall Street who wanted to negotiate a discount on his eternal sentence, a poet from the seventeenth century who had been trying to get published in the Inferno since 1651, and a small, timid demon named Gorrath who had accidentally set fire to the Accounts Department and was asking, very politely, whether he could keep his job.

"You can keep the job," said Lucifer wearily. "You cannot keep the fire extinguisher. Give it to Beelzebub. He'll put it in the broom cupboard with the other things that don't work."

Gorrath bowed three times and fled.

"You're too soft," said Lilith, leaning in the doorway with a glass of something red and syrupy. "You'll be out of a job by Tuesday."

"I'm the Devil. I can't be fired."

"No, but you can be bored to death." She sipped her drink. "Come on. Take a break. I have a surprise."

"I hate surprises."

"You'll like this one."

* * *

The surprise turned out to be a small, rickety boat on the banks of the Styx, its paint peeling in long grey curls. Lilith had found it in a junk shop in the Eighth Circle, she said, and had paid for it with a single bad poem and the soul of an arms dealer who had wanted to be remembered.

"It's perfect," she said, grinning. "We can row across to the Fields of Asphodel and watch the souls pretend they're happy."

"That's not a surprise. That's a Tuesday."

"Then you're welcome to row yourself back."

He rowed. She lounged in the stern with her feet up on the gunwale and a bottle of sweet black wine balanced on one knee, and the river slid past them, black and silent and thick with drowned leaves. Off to one side, a line of shivering souls stood in the shallows, waiting for a ferry that would never come.

"Do you think they know?" Lilith said suddenly.

"Know what?"

"That they're going to be here forever. That there's no getting out."

"They know. That's the point." He pulled on the oars. "Forever is a lot easier to bear when you've got someone to share it with."

Lilith was silent for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "Is that what you think this is?"

He didn't answer. He kept rowing, the oars dipping and rising, the river whispering against the hull. The silence stretched between them, thick and warm and uncomfortable, like a blanket that had been left out in the rain.

"Lucifer," she said at last.

"Hm?"

"Ask me to stay."

He stopped rowing. The boat drifted slowly toward the bank, and the drowned leaves whispered against the wood.

"Stay," he said.

"Mm." She smiled, sharp and mocking, and drank deeply from the bottle. "Was that so hard?"

"Excruciating," he said. "Please don't make me do it again."

* * *

They returned to the palace after dark, when the sky over Hell turned the colour of a bruise and the lava in the Fourth Circle glowed a sullen orange. Lucifer stripped off his coat, hung it on a hook shaped like a crooked spear, and stood at the window of his study looking out at the endless, burning city.

"Do you ever think about leaving?" Lilith asked, from the doorway.

"Every day."

"Where would you go?"

"Somewhere with a decent bookshop and a dog that doesn't die in 1622."

She laughed, low and warm, and crossed the room to stand beside him. Her hand found his, and for a moment they simply looked out at the damned, the flames, the vast and terrible machinery of eternity grinding quietly on.

"I suppose we could take the dog with us," she said.

"We'd need a bigger boat."

"We'd need a very much bigger boat."

Outside, the bell in the cathedral of Notre-Dame, hung on a chain across the Styx, rang once for midnight. Lucifer did not let go of her hand.

## 19. The Sand and the Sword

The smell reached me before the light did: sweat, wet sand, ox-blood from the butchers' stalls by the Porta Libitinensis, and beneath it all the sharp sweetness of the lime-wash they used on the cells. I lay on the straw of my cubiculum with my eyes shut and tried to remember the name of my father's vineyard. Nothing came. That frightened me more than the noise from the stands.

"Marcus," said Felix, from the next cell. He was a Thracian, broad and taciturn, and he had been in the ludus longer than I had. "Get up. The lanista wants us to see the new bronzes."

I got up. My knees cracked like old wood. I was twenty-six, and in the arena that was practically ancient.

* * *

The ludus of Scaurus stood on the Caelian, a warren of brick and stone behind high walls, its training yard always crowded with men who had been sold, or had sold themselves, or had been taken from their villages in the north with nothing but the clothes on their backs. We were not supposed to be happy. We were supposed to be useful. A dead gladiator was a loss on the account books; a living one was a profit that could be sold again.

I had been born free, in a town near Ravenna, to a shipwright and his wife. I had been sold for debt when I was nineteen, after my father drowned and left the family with a warehouse full of unpaid timber. A praetor's man had come with a purse of gold and a tally-stick, and by the end of the week I was wearing the leather belt of a retiarius and learning to throw a net at a man's throat.

That was seven years ago. I was still alive. It was, by the standards of the ludus, a kind of miracle.

* * *

The bronzes were waiting in the central courtyard, fresh from the armourers: greaves and helmets, each polished to a sullen gleam, the crests freshly oiled. The lanista Scaurus walked among us like a farmer inspecting livestock, his toga hitched up over one shoulder, his wrinkled face set in a permanent expression of disappointed calculation.

"The Emperor's games are in ten days," he said. "The crowd is restless. There has been talk of bread riots in the Subura, and the Praetorians are nervous. The people want blood, and the Emperor wants them to think he has given it to them." He tapped a helmet with his stick. "You will make them think it, gentlemen. That is your job."

Felix grunted beside me. "And if we die?"

"Then the Emperor has spent less on feeding you," said Scaurus, without a flicker of expression. "Do not waste my time with sentiment, Thracian."

I thought about the grain dole that had been cut again last winter, and the empty bread-ovens on the Aventine, and the widow in the next insula who had been dragged off by the watch for stealing a loaf. The city was boiling beneath its marble, and we were the scum on top.

* * *

At midday they let us out into the streets, under guard, for the market near the Forum Boarium. The air there was thick with the stink of the Tiber, fish and tar and crushed herbs, and the noise was a living thing: hawkers bellowing the prices of figs, sandal-makers arguing with their wives, a crowd of Syrian beggars singing something mournful in the shade of a portico.

I bought a wedge of cheese with a copper coin Scaurus had given me as a bonus after the last fight. The vendor, an old woman with a face like a walnut, looked at my belt and spat on the ground.

"Gladiator," she said, as if naming a disease. "My son died in the arena at Capua."

I didn't know what to say. I paid her anyway, and she took the coin without thanks, and I walked on with the cheese in my hand and the taste of ash in my mouth.

Felix found me sitting on a step near the temple of Hercules, watching the water.

"You're thinking about the old woman," he said.

"Yes."

"Don't. It does nothing." He sat down beside me and tore into his own cheese. "The Republic's gone. The Emperor's got the bread and the circus. The rest of us are just sand for the crowd to sweep up afterward."

"Do you believe that?"

Felix was silent a long time. Then he said, quietly, "I believe the crowd doesn't care whose blood it is, as long as it's red."

* * *

The afternoon sun was hot on the stones when we returned to the ludus. Scaurus was waiting at the gate with a sealed letter in his hand and an expression I had never seen on his face before: something close to fear.

"A man came from the palace," he said. "He wanted to know which of my men were Roman citizens. I told him none. He seemed disappointed."

I felt the cold seep into my bones. "Why would he ask that?"

"Because the new law says a citizen cannot be forced into the arena without a trial." Scaurus folded the letter. "The Emperor is angry. Someone in the senate has been talking about the men we bury. About the ones who never should have been here."

My hands went numb. For a moment I was back on the beach at Ravenna, listening to the sea and my father's last laugh, and I wondered whether any of it had ever mattered.

"Get some rest," Scaurus said, softer than I had ever heard him speak. "You fight at dawn on the day after tomorrow. Try to dream of something that isn't the sand."

* * *

That night I lay awake in the dark, listening to the ludus breathe around me: snores, coughs, the soft creak of straw as men turned in their sleep. Somewhere beyond the walls, a dog barked and was answered by another. Far off, from the direction of the Palatine, came the faint, mournful note of a horn.

I thought about the old woman, and the widow, and my father's warehouse full of timber that nobody would ever buy again. I thought about the bronze helmets gleaming in the courtyard, and the crowd roaring for blood, and the Emperor smiling from his high box with bread in one hand and a knife in the other.

Then I closed my eyes and tried, one more time, to remember the name of the vineyard. The sea, I thought. The sea, and my father's voice, and something green and bright and very far away.

Tomorrow I would fight. The day after, I would fight again. But for now, in the dark, I was a shipwright's son from Ravenna, and that was enough to keep me company until morning.

## 20. Letters from the Rock

_Dunmore Light, Sound of Mull_  
_14th September 1889_

Dear Mam,

I got your letter on the boat yesterday, the one with the tin of shortbread and the knitted socks. The socks are very fine and I am wearing them now, though it is not yet cold. I want you to know the shortbread was eaten by Mr. Gillies before I had a chance to sit down, so you may tell Aunt Jean she has a rival in the baking line.

The light is working well. The new lens came up from Edinburgh in August and it is the finest thing I have ever seen, all brass and prisms, and at night it throws beams across the water so bright you could read a psalm by them, though nobody does. Mr. Gillies says the Commissioners will be pleased. I have not yet decided whether I am pleased.

The rock is quiet. Too quiet, Mam. I did not know silence could have a weight to it until I came out here.

Your son,  
Alasdair

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_2nd October 1889_

Dear Alasdair,

Your letter came with the mail boat on Tuesday and your father read it twice at the table before he said anything. He said you sounded well. I said you sounded like a man sitting in a cold kitchen waiting for the kettle, and he said that was the point of being a lighthouse keeper.

I am sorry for it, son. I was never sorry to see you go, only frightened of what the rock would do to you. You were always a boy who needed noise.

There is news. The Macleans' Ruth is to be married at Martinmas, to the Oban boy who works at the ferry. You will remember her. She asked after you in the shop and blushed like a rose.

Write when the weather allows,

Mam

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_21st October 1889_

Dear Mam,

I did remember Ruth. I was not aware she had ever noticed me, but I am glad she is happy. Give her my good wishes if you see her.

There was a storm on Saturday. I will not write the whole of it, because you would only fret. The wind came from the south-west and drove the spray so high against the tower that the glass of the lantern shook in its frame. Mr. Gillies went out to check the chains and was nearly carried into the sea. I held the rope. I had never held a rope like that before, Mam, with a man's whole weight hanging off the end of it and the water trying to pull us both under. When we got him back in, he laughed. He said it was the first time in eleven years he had felt properly alive.

I did not laugh. I sat on the stairs for an hour after and could not stop shaking.

Your son,  
Alasdair

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_18th November 1889_

Dear Alasdair,

I am glad you are alive, and I am glad Mr. Gillies is alive, and I am sorry it had to be so close.

Your father has been very quiet since your letter. He went down to the harbour yesterday to speak to the Oban boat's captain, and came back with a face like thunder and said nothing at all. I think he means to write to the Commissioners, though I have not asked him why.

Are you eating? The tin of oatcakes is nearly empty. Let me know what else you need and I will send it on the next boat.

Mam

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_2nd December 1889_

Dear Mam,

Please don't send anything else. I have plenty. The stores are full and the lamp oil is coming on the first of the month, and I am sleeping better now that the weather has settled.

I have been thinking about what you said, that I was a boy who needed noise. I think you were right. I went to the rock because the Commissioners said it was a post of honour, and I was flattered, and I thought I could be useful. I did not think about the silence. I thought I would sit in it and it would make me wise.

It has not. It has only made me talk to the gulls. I have named two of them. Hamish and the Duchess. The Duchess is very rude.

I am not unhappy, Mam. I think I am only lonely in a way that has no name yet. I do not know how to say it in a letter.

Your son,  
Alasdair

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_10th January 1890_

Dear Alasdair,

You have said it in a letter, and I have read it, and that is enough for now.

When I was young, before I married your father, I went to live with my aunt at the far end of Lewis for a winter. I was seventeen and I thought I would die of boredom. Every morning I walked to the shore and every morning there was nothing but grey water and grey sky. I hated it. I wrote to my sister every week and told her I had been abandoned by God.

And then one day in February, a seal came up in the shallows, very close, and looked at me with the most ancient eyes I have ever seen, and I stood there for an hour without moving. Nobody else saw. It was the loneliest and happiest hour of my life.

I do not know whether there is a seal on the rock, son. But there is something out there that will look back at you if you let it. Look for it.

Mam

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_27th January 1890_

Dear Mam,

I have looked. There are no seals, but there is a porpoise that comes into the bay most mornings at first light. It rolls its back out of the water like a man stretching after sleep. I have started writing down the times it comes, in the logbook where I am meant to note the weather. Mr. Gillies thinks I have gone soft in the head.

Yesterday I rowed out to the skerry at low tide and found a stone with a hole straight through its middle, the size of a thumb. I brought it back and put it on the windowsill of the watch-room. When the sun comes through it, it throws a little ring of light on the wall.

I think I shall keep it.

Your son,  
Alasdair

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_14th March 1890_

Dear Alasdair,

Your father wants you to know he is proud of you. He did not say it in those words, but he read your last letter aloud at supper and then went out to the byre for a long time, and when he came back his eyes were red and he would not explain.

Ruth had a boy in February, and she has named him Alasdair, after you, because she says you were the only one who ever listened to her talk about the ferry. I think that is the sweetest thing I have ever heard.

Come home at midsummer if the Commissioners will let you. The house is quiet without you, but I think I am beginning to like the quiet.

Mam

* * *

_Dunmore Light_  
_2nd April 1890_

Dear Mam,

Alasdair

## 21. The Hacker Mindset

The menu says the bread is sourdough, which is good, because sourdough is the only thing in this restaurant I have not already mentally mapped. The waiter is named Tomás, according to the brass pin on his shirt. He has a habit of touching the back of his neck when he's about to lie about the specials. Ninety percent confidence he's going to recommend the sea bass. Fine. I'll eat the sea bass.

Across the table, Priya is laughing at something on her phone. She has a laugh like someone accidentally dropping a tray of cutlery in a very nice way. I like her. I've liked her for twenty minutes, and that is itself a problem, because liking people is the one thing my brain never seems to do without also taking an inventory.

_Stop it,_ I tell myself. _Be a person. People have dinner with other people. It's allowed._

The bread arrives. I break a piece off with my right hand. The dinner table is an old one, slightly warped, and the second bolt on the chair leg is loose; I can see the rust-rimmed hole where it used to sit. If someone leaned back too hard, the whole thing would tip. I make a mental note of that and say nothing about it.

"So you were saying," Priya says, "you used to play Magic competitively?"

"Yeah. Back in uni. Mostly Legacy. I was terrible at drafting but I was dangerous with a good deck."

"Oh my god, I _love_ Legacy. What did you play?"

And here it is. The thing that makes first dates bearable. Shared nerd-fandom is a handshake that works without either person having to say anything about their life, and I have spent a good eleven minutes talking about Force of Will and mana bases with a woman who clearly knows what she's doing. Her Oath deck is annoyingly good. I tell her so.

"You've played Oath?" She leans forward, eyes bright.

"Once. Against a guy in Shoreditch who kept telling me he'd invented the archetype."

"Oh, _no_. What happened?"

"I found the combo on turn two by reading his sleeves."

She stares at me. Then she laughs so hard she has to put her wine down.

"You can't say that on a first date," she says, wiping her eyes.

"I know. I'm working on it."

* * *

The sea bass arrives. Tomás touches his neck. It is, in fact, the special, and he is, in fact, lying about the lemon. I eat it anyway. Priya tells me about her job at a cartography firm and the time she accidentally used a satellite map of a sheep farm for a client's office move. We swap stories about bad Wizards tournaments. She has a good, dry sense of humour, the kind that sneaks up on you. I'm enjoying myself, which is a sensation I have to keep reminding myself is safe.

Then she reaches for her phone to check the time, and I see the screen light up under the table, and I see her thumb swipe three times in a pattern I recognise instantly: a Z-shape, left to right, then diagonal, then down.

Nine dots. Four strokes. The lock screen is a photo of a dog in a Santa hat.

The pattern is visible on the glass in the reflection of the window, a faint grease-smear that picks out the path under the light. It's the worst unlock pattern I have ever seen, and I have been trained to see unlock patterns the way some people see faces.

My brain files it away before I can stop it. _Top-left, middle-middle, bottom-right. Predictable. Smudge-visible. Two shoulder-surfers with a clear line of sight from the bar. Her phone probably has no PIN fallback. Her dog's photo is probably her lock screen on every device she owns._

_Don't say it._

"Sorry," she says, tucking the phone away. "Work thing. They never leave me alone."

"Sure," I say. I smile. I drink my water.

_Don't say it._

"You know," I hear myself say, lightly, "you should really change your phone pattern."

Priya looks up. "What?"

"Sorry. Ignore me. It's a habit."

"What habit?"

Fuck.

"Just that it's, uh, quite visible. From the bar. Reflection off the window." I gesture vaguely at the glass. "Anyone could see it. Not to be weird. Honestly, it's a very common mistake. I just notice things."

Her face does a quick, complicated shift. Amusement, then wariness, then something that looks very much like she's deciding whether to be offended. "You were looking at my phone?"

"I wasn't _looking_, exactly. It sort of happened."

"Wow." She doesn't laugh. "That's a very specific skill."

"Yeah. It's the one that got me fired from my last job. Well. Almost fired. They put me in a different team and gave me a stern look. Turns out nobody likes a guy who points out which fire exits are propped open by a bin."

"Did you have a reason for that?"

"A very good one. Someone had to tell them."

She is silent for a long beat. Her fingers drum once on the tablecloth. I can see her doing the same thing I do: counting exits, watching the bar, checking the door where the two shoulder-surfers are sitting, one of whom is still glancing over at our table like we're the most interesting thing in the room.

I could walk it back. Apologise. Pretend I'm just a bit odd, as she clearly suspects. Or I could do what I'd like to do, which is say something useful. Like _your password manager is probably not set up right, I'll check it for you_, and then spend the next hour explaining why that's the kindest possible response to a stranger with a dog in a Santa hat.

_Sabotage,_ whispers the voice in my head. _Just a little. Let her see it._

_Connection,_ says the other one. The quieter one. The one that wanted, very badly, to have a good night.

"You're doing it again," Priya says softly.

"I know."

"Are you going to tell me the rest?"

I look down at the sea bass. The lemon is definitely not fresh. "Yeah," I say. "But I'm going to need another glass of wine first. And I think I should apologise, before I go on, for the next ten minutes."

She picks up her glass, eyes narrowed. "Go on, then."

I take a breath. I start talking. Behind me, the loose bolt in the chair leg creaks, quietly, and I resist, with considerable effort, the urge to fix it.

## 22. The Quiet Girl

Okay. Okay. Deep breath. Three seconds in, two seconds out. Mrs. Ferreira said that in the calm-down corner last year, and it's stupid, but my brain keeps reaching for it anyway, like a hand going for a railing on a staircase that's already been fixed.

The science project is "Density and Buoyancy: Build a Boat." Our table has four people and a tub of tin foil. Mr. Oduya has paired me with Daniel Park, which is either the best thing that's ever happened to me or the single worst thing, and I can't decide which because I'm too busy trying not to look at him.

He's smiling at Sophie. Sophie is saying something about her hair. Daniel laughs, and the sound is warm and easy and nothing like the way I laugh, which is mostly a sort of cough with my shoulders up around my ears.

_Say something._

Okay. Let's do the rehearsal.

Hi, Daniel. Do you want to do the buoyancy calculations, or should I? Yeah, good. You're good at numbers, right? I saw your test score in the hall. Congratulations. That was a really impressive result. Or — no. That's weird. He'll think I've been checking his scores. Which I have. But he doesn't need to know that.

Hi, Daniel. What do you think about the foil? I think it's probably too thin. We could fold it twice. Is that a good idea? Yeah. Okay. Yes. Fine.

_Ugh._

It's too formal. It's too much. He'll think I'm a robot. I sound like the back of a cereal box. Maybe a joke? Hi, Daniel, I hear you're the boat person now. Respect. Except that's not a joke, it's just a statement with an awkward little tail on the end, and he'll look at me the way people look at a dog that's learned to say _hello_.

I could just say nothing. I'm very good at saying nothing. I've had years of practice. Nobody remembers the quiet girl who sits by the window and draws small, careful diagrams in the margins of her notebook. Nobody has to.

But then Mr. Oduya says, "Right, team, let's crack on," and everyone turns to look at me, and I realise I've been staring at Daniel's left shoe for about nine seconds.

"Hi," I say.

It comes out as a squeak. My face goes hot. My ears go hot. I can feel my neck going red in that patchy, humiliating way that makes Sophie tilt her head and say _are you okay?_ in the voice people use for toddlers.

"Hey," Daniel says, and smiles at me. A real smile. Not the polite one he gives teachers. "You're Mina, right? From the library?"

_He knows my name._ He knows my name, and he remembers the library. Oh no. What did I do in the library? Did I sneeze? Did I snort-laugh at the vending machine? I'm sure I snort-laughed at the vending machine.

"Yeah," I say. "Mina. Yes. Hi."

"You said something smart about the Archimedes thing last week," he says. "I was going to ask about it. You're the one who knows the physics stuff, right?"

I stare at him. _I did?_ I don't remember saying anything smart. I was too busy looking at the cover of his book. I was too busy hoping he'd look up. I hardly ever say anything out loud, not in class, not anywhere, and I definitely wouldn't have said something smart in front of Daniel Park, because if I had, I'd be replaying it right now, every single syllable, in the dark, with my face in the pillow.

Except I am replaying it. Right now. In my head. And I can't find it.

"Maybe," I manage.

"Cool." He pulls the foil toward him, unrolls a bit, and holds it up to the light. "Okay, so I was thinking we fold it into a bowl. Like, a really deep one. More surface area means more displaced water. So it floats, right? Even though it's metal."

_Oh._ Oh. That's the right answer. That's exactly the right answer. My brain lights up like a fuse box. I know this. I've known this since I was eight. Water displacement, mass over volume, the boat sits low because it's pushing against more of the sea.

"Yeah," I say, quieter now. "It's about the average density. If you spread the mass out, the average goes down. So the boat's less dense than water. It floats."

It's a complete sentence. It's a _good_ complete sentence. I hear myself say it, and it doesn't feel like falling down a staircase. It feels like stepping onto a stair that's exactly where I thought it would be.

Daniel is nodding slowly, his eyes on mine. "Wait, so the boat's not heavier, it's just spread out more?"

"Yeah. Like if you took the same foil and crumpled it into a ball, it would sink. Because the ball is denser. It's the same stuff, just squished."

"Huh." He grins. "That's actually really cool."

"Oh. Um. Thanks."

_Stop. Don't you dare say thanks like that. Like a little sad apology._

"Like, it's not impressive," I add, quickly. "It's just physics."

"It's not _just_ physics. It's a cool trick." He taps the foil. "Okay, you do the folding. I'll do the labels. We'll be the best boat team in the entire school."

And he goes back to his notebook, and I stare at the foil in my hands, and my heartbeat slowly comes back down from the ceiling.

* * *

Sophie leans over. "Did you two just become best friends?"

"No," I say, which is true, and then, because it feels wrong to be that flat about something so small: "Maybe. A little."

"He's nice," Sophie says. "He's always nice. It's kind of annoying."

I agree with her, mostly because it's the only safe thing to agree with. Inside my head, though, I'm already replaying the moment, rewinding it like a video. He said my name. He remembered the library. He thought I'd said something smart. I think I did say something smart. I think I might have been, for about thirty seconds, the kind of person who gets to be in a conversation.

Then the voice comes in, the one that always comes in, the loud, sneering one that lives in the back of my skull and never sleeps.

_He's being nice because he feels sorry for you. Everyone feels sorry for the quiet girl._

_He's going to tell everyone you're weird._

_Next time you won't have anything to say. And he'll see it. And he'll stop looking at you that way._

I fold the foil. I fold it again. The edges go soft and slightly crumpled under my fingers.

_Stop it,_ I tell myself. _He didn't say anything bad. He just said it was cool._

_He said it was cool because you're smart, idiot. And now he's going to find out you're not._

Mr. Oduya comes by and taps the table with one finger. "Looking good, team. Nice bowl shape, Mina. Keep it up."

I smile at him, and it's small and nervous and probably looks like I've been caught doing something wrong. But I feel, very faintly, the warmth of it, like a little lamp left on in the hall.

Daniel looks up from his notebook and catches my eye, and gives me a thumbs up, and goes back to labelling.

My chest does something complicated. I press the foil bowl against the table, very carefully, so it doesn't crease.

Three seconds in. Two seconds out.

_Okay,_ I think. _Okay. Maybe tomorrow I'll say something else._

I don't yet know that the most interesting thing about me is the thing I keep trying to hide. But when the bell rings and everyone packs up, Daniel leaves the foil bowl sitting on the table, its shape perfect, and says over his shoulder, "Bring that to the fair next week, Mina. It's the best one we've got."

And for the first time all year, I say, "Okay," out loud, without a single rehearsal.

## 23. Rehearsing Rejection

She texted at 4:12. _Hey, are we still on for tonight? I'm looking forward to it x._ Good. That's good. That's fine. People text things like that all the time. It's just a message. It doesn't mean anything except that she's looking forward to it, and she put a kiss on the end, which she has never done before, which means something, or it means nothing, and either way I'm going to spend the next four hours trying to work out which.

I read it eleven times. Then I read it again, looking for the cold bit. There isn't one. Which is somehow worse, because a cold bit would be easy. A cold bit would give me something to hold.

_Okay. Okay. Let's do this._

I've been sitting on the edge of the bath for twenty minutes with my phone face-down on the bathmat, and in my head the conversation has already happened maybe nine times. I know exactly how it goes, because I've written it. I've been writing it since Sunday.

* * *

_You've been distant._

_No, I haven't._

_Yes you have. You take three hours to reply. You cancelled on Thursday. You said "maybe" when I asked about the weekend, and nobody says "maybe" unless they're looking for the door._

_I was tired._

_You're always tired. It's a convenient word._

And there it is. The little flare of heat in my chest, the one that always comes right before the sentences start getting sharp. In the rehearsal, I'm already defending myself. I'm saying things like _you don't get to decide how I feel about you_ and _I'm not your ex, stop treating me like one_, and I'm saying them so loudly that the words crack. And she's looking at me with a face I've never seen before, and it's the face of someone who has finally seen through me, and she's leaving, and she's taking the good parts of her with her.

I hate this part. I hate that I can see myself doing it. I know it's not real. I know it's the brain's old machinery, humming away, building walls in case somebody walks through the door with a crowbar.

_You're doing it again,_ says the small, tired voice at the back of my head. _Notice it. Name it. You know what this is._

I do know what this is. I've read the books. I have a therapist with a poster of a rowing boat on the wall, and she has told me that these loops are my mind's way of trying to protect me from being hurt, and that if I rehearse the worst version enough times, I'll feel like I have some control over it.

But the loop doesn't care what the books say. It keeps going. It's a little theatre inside my skull, and I'm the only audience, and every ticket is a pain I've already paid for.

* * *

I get up. I put on the blue jumper she said she liked. I look at myself in the mirror and I think, _you look like someone who's been crying,_ and then I think, _no, you look like someone who's about to be hurt,_ and then I think about how those are the same thing if you stare at them long enough.

I breathe. I count. I don't text her back.

_Maybe she's just busy. Maybe she's got a lot on at work. Maybe she didn't mean the kiss._

_No, she meant the kiss, and she's going to end it, and you're going to be on your own again, and you'll have to explain to everyone why this one didn't work out either._

_Okay, but then why did she text you? Why would someone who's leaving send you a kiss?_

_Because people are cruel. Because they say things they don't mean. Because she's probably been talking to her friends about you, and they've told her you're too much._

_You are too much._

_No I'm not._

_Yes you are. Look at you._

I hold my breath until my lungs start complaining, then let it out.

_Stop._ I say it out loud, to the bathroom. My voice echoes off the tiles. It sounds thin and stupid.

_Stop,_ I say again. _This is the pattern. You're rehearsing. You're building the case before the trial starts. You know what you're doing._

The thing is, knowing what I'm doing doesn't always stop me from doing it. It just gives me a clear view of the train as it runs me over.

* * *

She's there when I arrive, sitting at a table by the window of the café on Clarence Street, with two coffees and a half-eaten cake and an expression I can't quite read. She looks up when I walk in, and her face lights up, and I feel my heart drop through the floor.

"Hey," she says. "You look nice."

_She's being polite. She always says that. It's a thing people say._

"Thanks," I say. "You too."

I sit. The chair is hard. The coffee is too hot. She talks about her sister's wedding, the bridesmaid dress that's the wrong shade of green, a dog that barked at her on the bus. Everything she says is light and warm and easy, and every time she laughs, I wait for the sentence where it all turns.

It doesn't come.

Instead, she reaches across the table and puts her hand over mine. Her fingers are warm. The café is loud, and nobody is looking at us, and I feel a rush of something so sudden and strong that my throat aches.

"I missed you this week," she says quietly. "I know I've been a bit absent. Work's been mad. But I was thinking about you the whole time."

The loop screams. _Lies. She's saying what you want to hear. She's going to say the other thing in a minute._

_She just said she missed you._ The quieter voice is very faint. _Why are you making it hard to believe?_

I want to believe it so badly that it hurts. I can feel my eyes stinging. I can feel my body wanting to lean across the table and fold itself into her, to say _I missed you too, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry I doubted you._

And then the other thought arrives, cold and fast, like a door slamming. _You're only saying this because you're sorry for me. Everyone's sorry for me. I'm the girl with the diagnosis and the messages and the stupid mood swings. She's been carrying this for weeks, and now she's going to ask me to be less._

"Are you okay?" she asks.

"Yeah." It comes out too quick. "Just tired."

She looks at me for a long moment, and I can see the question on her face, and I can see her deciding not to ask it.

"Okay," she says gently. "Do you want to walk along the river after?"

"Yes," I say, and that part is true, and it's the only part I'm sure of.

* * *

We walk. The river is dark and slow and smells of cold mud and diesel. Her arm brushes mine, once, twice. Then she takes my hand, and I let her, and the warmth of it sinks through my skin and into the hollow behind my ribs.

"You're quiet," she says.

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

I open my mouth to tell her everything. The whole theatre: the rehearsals, the walls, the voice that says she's leaving. My hand tightens around hers. For a second I think I might actually do it.

Then a bus goes past, loud and bright, and I flinch, and the moment shatters.

"Nothing," I say. "Just you."

She squeezes my hand. "Good."

We stop under a streetlamp. She turns to face me, and her eyes are soft and steady, and for the first time all day, I don't feel like I'm waiting for the blow. I feel like I'm standing in a warm room with the door open and the rain outside.

"I'm glad we're doing this," she says.

And I can feel the sentence forming in my head, already: _Thanks. I'm glad too. But I know you're going to get tired of me eventually. Everyone does._

I don't say it. I don't say anything. I just kiss her.

It's clumsy and tentative, and her hand comes up to my cheek, and I think, with a strange, clear sort of wonder: _she's still here._

_For now,_ says the voice.

_For now,_ I think back, and I let myself have it anyway.

## 24. Blood Debt

The ruins of the Sanjo-in temple stood on a rise above the river, its roof long collapsed, its tiled eaves split open to the sky. Moss clung to the stone steps. Inside, where the altar had once stood, someone had cleared the rubble and laid a ring of old lanterns around a packed-earth floor, and it was there, under the thin amber light, that the crowd had gathered.

Three hundred of them, maybe more. Samurai's sons in threadbare dark silk, their swords hidden under their coats. Dock-workers with broad shoulders and cropped hair. Two Chinese stevedores who had come up from the wharves with a bag of silver between them. A pair of Dutch sailors betting in Japanese they only half understood. They pressed close to the ring, murmuring, wagering with cups of rice wine and gold scraped from purses, and every few seconds a roar went up as someone lost a bet.

At the far side of the ring, behind a rope of braided straw, a boy of perhaps eighteen stood with his feet planted wide, his hands loose at his sides. Kenji Mizuno wore the plain black gi of the Jigen-ryū dojo in Kagoshima, his hair cropped to the scalp, his face pale and fierce in the lantern light. His breath came slow and deep. His eyes were fixed on the opposite side of the circle, where a second figure waited.

Lin Wei was barefoot, wearing loose blue cotton rolled to the elbows. His black hair was tied in a tight knot at the nape of his neck. He had no weapon. He was rolling his shoulders, very gently, almost lazily, the way a cat stretches before it decides to chase something.

"You came," Kenji said.

"You invited me." Lin's voice was soft and carried clearly in the hush. "Twice. Your letter was very polite. Your master would have been appalled."

"My master is an old fool who still thinks the Mori clan will come back for their land."

"And mine thinks the same about the bells of the Yamanaka shrine. We all have ghosts." Lin's gaze was steady. "Shall we begin?"

The crowd fell silent.

* * *

Kenji moved first.

He did not waste a breath. He came in low and fast from the left, feet sliding across the packed earth, his right hand already drawing back for a straight punch aimed at Lin's solar plexus. It was the classic opening of his style: explosive forward momentum, a single decisive strike, the whole body driven behind one point like a stone from a sling. Jigen-ryū masters called it _the first blow is the last blow_. Kenji had practised it ten thousand times.

Lin did not step back. He stepped _into_ the punch, angling his body a fraction to the right, so that Kenji's fist sailed past his ribs with a whisper of cloth. At the same instant Lin's left arm whipped out, wrapping around Kenji's extended wrist like a loose rope, and his hips turned in a tight, circular sweep.

Kenji's momentum carried him forward, off balance. He twisted, trying to free his arm, but Lin's grip followed the motion, rolling his wrist so that the joint bent the wrong way. Kenji hissed through his teeth and drove his left elbow backward, a short, vicious strike toward Lin's throat.

Lin was already gone. He had pivoted on his right heel, dropping his centre of gravity, and the elbow passed over his shoulder with a hair's breadth to spare. At the same moment he released Kenji's wrist, snapped his arm forward and down, and brought the heel of his palm up under Kenji's chin.

Kenji's head snapped back. He staggered, catching himself on one foot, and the crowd roared.

"That's Piguaquan," someone shouted. "The whip-hand! Watch the whip-hand!"

Kenji shook his head to clear it. His eyes were narrow now, sharp and calculating. "Commoner's trick," he spat.

"It's a trick that keeps commoners alive," Lin said, and his voice was almost gentle. "Do you want to try again?"

* * *

They circled.

The packed earth was uneven, and Kenji's feet found the dips and rises with the discipline of a man who had trained on this ground since he was a child. He was faster than Lin, and he knew it. He began to press, stepping in with short, sharp feints: a jab to the face, a kick to the thigh, a rapid chain of punches that cut the air like hail. Each one forced Lin back a half step, then another, toward the edge of the lanterns.

Lin did not meet the force directly. He flowed. His arms moved in tight, spiralling arcs, deflecting each punch with his forearm, the back of his wrist, the flat of his palm. When Kenji's kick came in, Lin caught the shin on the outside of his forearm and let the momentum carry it past, then stepped in close, one knee lifting toward Kenji's ribs.

Kenji twisted at the waist to avoid the knee. The movement brought his shoulder into range, and Lin's free hand struck him across the upper arm with a crack like a whip. Kenji grunted and stumbled, his balance wavering for a precious half second.

Lin's left foot slid forward, hooking behind Kenji's ankle, and the boy went down hard on one hip.

He rolled instantly, coming up in a crouch, but Lin was already standing over him, arms relaxed, weight evenly balanced on both feet, the lanterns casting long shadows across both their faces.

"Enough?" Lin asked.

Kenji's jaw tightened. He came up off the earth in one explosive motion, both hands raised, and drove forward with a flurry of strikes that were faster and more precise than anything he had shown before. Lin's arms blurred, deflecting each blow. A fist grazed his cheek. A palm slapped against his collarbone. Lin gave ground, step by step, until his back was almost against the rope.

Then Kenji made his mistake.

He committed everything to a single downward chop aimed at Lin's collarbone, his weight pitched entirely forward, his hips rotating into the blow the way the old masters had taught. It was perfect, technically. It was also a single line, and Lin had been waiting for a single line all night.

Lin dropped under the chop, rolling his body to the left, and his right hand came up in a rising arc, the back of the wrist striking Kenji's jaw with a sound like a rifle shot. Kenji's head rocked back. His knees buckled.

Lin stepped in, his left hand catching the boy's shoulder, and held him upright for a long, breathless moment, as if he were guiding a drunk friend home.

"I told you," Lin murmured, close to his ear. "You rely too much on the first strike."

Kenji tried to speak. Blood ran from one corner of his mouth. He laughed weakly, a sound like a dying bell.

"Then finish it," he rasped.

"No." Lin let him go, and Kenji swayed and caught his balance. "Your blood is no debt of mine. Keep your pride. It's the only thing you have that's worth anything."

* * *

The crowd erupted into arguments. The stevedores were shouting for Lin to finish the boy, while the samurai's sons were already muttering about a foreign trick and a fixed match. The Dutch sailors had lost their bet and were laughing uproariously. Someone threw a rice cup into the lantern ring, and it shattered.

Kenji stood in the centre of the packed earth, swaying slightly, one hand pressed to his jaw. He was breathing hard, and there was something new in his eyes. Not defeat, exactly. Something more like respect.

"Your masters," he said quietly, "will hear of this."

"Yes," Lin said. "And mine will say I should have broken your arm."

"Would you have?"

Lin considered. "Probably not. You're too stubborn to heal properly if I did."

Kenji's laugh came out as a cough, and he winced at the pain in his ribs. "Same time next month?"

"Only if you bring better tea."

The crowd began to thin, the lanterns guttering one by one, the samurai's sons gathering their coats and the dockworkers sliding their silver back into their purses. Above them, through the broken roof, the stars came out over Kyoto, cold and bright and indifferent to the blood on the packed earth.

## 25. Breaking Formation

The studio is three floors up in a building in Gangnam that smells of floor wax and expensive cologne. The mirrors cover one whole wall, floor to ceiling, and they make the room look twice as big and twice as hostile. Seventeen trainees stand in four neat rows, every foot angled exactly at forty-five degrees, every spine straight as a pole. I stand at the back, third row from the left, in a grey tank top from a thrift shop in Daegu, and I feel like a fly that has walked into a surgery.

My name is Jiwoo Park. I'm nineteen. I grew up in Bongcheon-dong, where the buildings have graffiti on every wall and the sound of basslines leaks out of car windows at three in the morning. I learned to dance on a cracked concrete rooftop, with my cousins yelling beats from the stairwell and a single speaker that could barely hold a note. I've never been taught a single count. I've just _moved_, and when I move, everything else goes quiet.

The instructor's name is Lee Han-sol. Everyone calls him Director Lee. He walks slowly down the row, his shoes clicking on the floor like a metronome, and he doesn't speak. He doesn't have to. Every trainee in this room has heard the stories. He broke the last group of girls to make a debut line. He made one of them cry until she vomited. He made another stand in the corridor for six hours because she was half a beat late.

He stops in front of me.

"Park Jiwoo," he says. His voice is dry and flat, like paper.

"Yes, sir."

"You're the street one."

"Yes."

"Show me your basic box step."

I hesitate. There's no basic box step in my body. There's a rhythm, a bounce, a slide of the hips that comes from something older than counts. But the room is silent, and seventeen pairs of eyes are watching, and Director Lee's expression hasn't changed.

"Okay," I say, and I start.

* * *

The move I know is the one I was born into: a body roll that starts at the chest and ripples down through the spine, the knees dropping, the weight sinking into the heels. Then a stomp, hard enough to shake the floor, followed by a sharp pivot that sends my right arm flying out in a whip-crack line. The whole thing is loose and dirty and full of attitude. My cousins used to say it looked like someone had cut the strings on a marionette and let it run wild.

Director Lee watches with his arms folded.

"No," he says.

I stop. "Sir?"

"You are dancing like a drunk on a bus." He doesn't raise his voice. "Start again. Five counts. Eight beats on each count. Feet flat, knees soft, spine straight. Arms move on the second count, head on the fourth, hips on the sixth. You do not move until the count tells you."

I try. Five, six, seven, eight. I shift my weight on the wrong beat. My shoulders lift half a second early. The arm comes out too wide, the wrist too loose. The whole thing feels like wearing someone else's shoes.

"Again," says Director Lee.

Again.

Again.

By the twelfth time, my calves are burning and my lungs are aching. My head is buzzing with numbers, and my body keeps running ahead of them, instinct overriding the count, the old rhythm pulling me sideways like a current in a river.

"Again," he says.

* * *

The trainee next to me, a girl named Hana with a neat ponytail and sharp eyes, gives me a sympathetic look during the break. She wipes her face with a towel, watching me in the mirror.

"You're really good, you know," she whispers. "Your rolls are so... clean. But you're fighting the count."

"I'm not fighting it. I just can't hear it."

"You have to hear it. It's the only thing in here that matters." She sighs. "He doesn't want you to be good. He wants you to be _the same_ as us."

I want to say that's the most stupid thing I've ever heard. But the rhythm in my chest feels like it's trying to bang its way out of a locked cupboard, and I don't know how to tell it to be quiet.

* * *

The second rehearsal is the choreography for the title track. Director Lee demonstrates it himself, once, without music. His body moves through the sequence like a machine built by someone who has never made a mistake: sharp lines, perfect angles, each formation crossing and reforming at precisely the right beat. A quarter turn. A step back. Arms rising in a unified arc. The entire studio seems to vibrate with the discipline of it.

It is beautiful, in the way that a sword is beautiful. It is also completely closed.

"Now," he says, "you."

We form up. I'm placed at the end of the second row. My mark is a strip of tape on the floor, two metres to the left of Hana. The music starts, an insistent electronic beat with a pulse like a heart at a full sprint. Seventeen bodies move as one.

Count one. Step left. Arms up. Count two. Pivot. Head snaps. Count three. Sweep the arm across the face in a diagonal slash. Count four. Drop the hips, hold, release.

I hit the first three counts. On the fourth, my hips want to ripple, to slide and flow the way they always do, and my knees go loose, and the whole line tilts toward me like a row of dominoes with one piece pushed out of place.

Hana's shoulder bumps mine. The girl on my other side shoots me a furious look. In the mirror, I see the gap in the formation, a ragged hole where my body should be, and my stomach drops through the floor.

"Stop!"

The music cuts out. The silence rings.

Director Lee walks toward me. His face is still and cold, but there's a faint tightening at his jaw that I didn't see before.

"Park Jiwoo," he says. "Explain."

"I —" My throat is dry. "I lost the count on four."

"You did not lose the count. You chose not to hear it."

"That's not —"

"You were dancing for yourself." His voice is low now, almost intimate. "I could see it. The arms, the rolls, the hips. It is very beautiful, and it is wrong. Here, you are not one body. You are a single note in a chord, and you are singing a different song."

I stare at him. Something hot is burning under my ribs. "Sir, with respect. Music is supposed to be felt. If you tell me to just count like a machine, then what's the point of dancing at all?"

The room goes very still. Hana's eyes widen. Somebody coughs.

Director Lee studies me for a long, cool moment. Then he smiles, very slightly, and I realise, with a jolt of cold dread, that he's pleased.

"Good," he says softly. "That is the first honest thing you have said since you walked in. Now you must decide what it is worth."

* * *

After the session, I sit alone on the bench in the changing room, my hands wrapped around a bottle of water I haven't opened. My reflection in the locker door looks like a stranger's: flushed, sweaty, eyes too bright. My phone buzzes. A message from my cousin Jae-won: _how's the Seoul prince? did they make you bow to the mirror?_ I don't answer.

The truth is, I'm terrified. Not of Director Lee, or the counts, or the cutthroat ladder of trainees fighting for a spot in the debut line. I'm terrified of what I'll be if I stay. I'll be taught to fold my hips into a clean, perfect line. I'll learn to make my smile land on the eighth count. I'll be famous, probably, in a year or two, and every video of me will be flawless, and nobody who watches will ever know the wild, crooked, half-broken girl from the concrete rooftop who used to make the whole block stop and watch.

Hana sits down beside me. "You okay?"

"No."

"Yeah." She holds out a granola bar. "Hey, for what it's worth, I think you're going to get kicked out."

"Thanks."

"Or you're going to make it." She shrugs, staring at the wall. "I can't tell which is worse."

I laugh, short and sharp. It hurts a little.

* * *

Later, I stand alone in the studio. The lights are dimmed, and the mirrors give back a dim, shadowy image of a girl in grey. I put on my own playlist, a mix of old funk and hip-hop beats I used to play on that cracked speaker in Bongcheon-dong, and I let the first bassline roll out across the floor.

My body wakes up immediately. The rolls come first, slow and wide, the chest leading and the spine following like a wave. Then the stomp, sharp enough to shake the mirror. The arm snaps out, the whip that's been in me since I was twelve. My hips slide, free and unhurried, and for a moment the whole room melts away, and I'm back on the rooftop with the city lights flickering below.

I don't count. I don't need to. The music is inside me.

Then I hear the door open.

Director Lee stands in the doorway, arms folded, silhouetted against the corridor light. He doesn't come in. He watches me for a long time, his expression unreadable.

I stop, breathing hard, sweat dripping into my eyes.

"Do it again," he says.

I hesitate. The music is still playing, quiet and insistent. Then I lift my arms, and I start again, slower this time. Five counts. Eight beats. I feel the rhythm sliding under my feet, and I don't resist it. I let it guide me, and I let my body fall into the cracks between the beats where my own voice lives.

When I finish, the silence is huge.

Director Lee nods, very slowly.

"Tomorrow," he says, "you will do it with the others. And you will do it in formation, exactly on the count. And then, after everyone else has gone home, you will come back here, and you will do it again, the way you did it just now."

"Why?"

"Because the audience will never see the difference unless you show them." He turns, pauses in the doorway. "That is the only lesson I have to teach you, Park Jiwoo. Not how to follow. How to hide something beautiful in plain sight."

The door clicks shut.

I stand in the empty studio, the bass still thumping faintly through the speakers, and I laugh. It's a surprised, breathless sound, and it echoes off the mirrors, and for once, it doesn't feel like breaking anything at all.

## 26. The Court of the Dreaming, Session in Dispute

The Court sat in the Hall of Unfinished Things, where the ceiling was a sky that could not decide whether it was dusk or dawn and the floor was made of loose sheets of forgotten lullabies. Dream sat at the high bench in his usual black, his face unreadable, one long finger tapping the armrest of a chair that had been carved from a single, sleeping moth.

He had not wanted to hear this case. He had said so at the start of the session, in a voice that carried like a cold draught through a cathedral. He had said so twice more since.

"Mora," said the Clerk of the Court, a small, bald figure with ink-stained fingers and an air of permanent apology, "appears before the Court on the matter of the Maxwell Boy's dreamscape, which is held in fee by the Entity known as First Love."

A woman rose from the defendant's bench.

She was tall, pale, and lovely in the way that a cliff is lovely in a storm. Her hair was long and black and threaded with silver, and her eyes were a deep, clear grey that seemed to swallow the light. She wore a plain dark gown that clung to her hips, and she sat down again slowly, folding her hands in her lap with the composure of a woman who had sat on far more distressing chests than this one.

"Morning," said Mora pleasantly.

"It is not morning," said Dream.

"It's always morning somewhere."

"Not in here."

Mora smiled, slow and sly. "Well. I'm sorry you've been up all night. Must be exhausting."

* * *

First Love was called next.

It came in like a blush. A soft, rosy shape that seemed to bow every time it moved, its edges blurred and shy, its voice so earnest it made the nearest courtroom windows fog. It had been creating dreams for longer than the species had been able to remember them. It was, by every reasonable measure, the most harmless thing in the Dreaming.

"Your Honour," it said, twisting its hands. "The boy Max has been dreaming of the wildflower meadow since he was eleven. He lies in the grass with his crush, Sophie. She laughs at him for being clumsy. He tries to kiss her and she turns her head, and it's always just a little muddled, because he isn't sure yet, and that's all right. That's what first love _is_. The uncertainty is the point."

"Yes," said Dream, sounding deeply bored. "I'm aware of what first love is."

"And then Mora came," First Love said, its voice trembling. "She changed the scene. Sophie climbs on top of him, very playfully, and then —" It shuddered. "She turns into _that_."

The gallery gasped. A small, tinkling sound, like teaspoons dropped onto a tray.

"What does _that_ look like?" asked Dream, flatly.

First Love didn't answer. It simply rippled a little, and the courtroom's far wall went briefly, deliberately dark. When the light came back, the sleeping Max was lying in the centre of the floor, his face slack and grey, a thin line of mist rising from his open mouth into the thing that was now crouched on his chest.

It had Sophie's shape, almost. But the arms were too long, the mouth too wide, the skin mottled like wet bark. And yet, when it turned to look at the bench, its eyes were soft and dark and startlingly beautiful, and the whole gallery shifted uncomfortably in their seats, a little ashamed of the warmth stirring low in their stomachs.

"Yeah, okay," said Dream. "I can see the problem."

* * *

Mora was called to speak.

"Your Honour," she said, rising, "I'd like to make a point about property rights." She spread her hands. "This dream was created by a bashful little entity who doesn't know what it's doing. It built a meadow with no walls, no doors, no warnings. Anybody could walk into it. Anybody could be taken over. I was simply the first to do it with some enthusiasm."

"You were not invited," said Dream.

"Neither was First Love, if we're honest. Nobody sends an invitation to the boy's subconscious. It just happens." She smiled. "I'm not stealing the dream. I'm _repurposing_ it."

"You're terrifying a child."

"I'm feeding a child." Mora's voice was gentle, almost wistful. "Look, it's not my fault. I'm a nightmare spirit. I sit on people's chests. That's the job. It's what I do, and I'm good at it, and I have to eat too."

The gallery stirred. Someone in the back row muttered, "A girl's gotta eat, you know?" and then, quickly, "Sorry."

Dream closed his eyes. A tiny muscle jumped in his jaw.

"Mora," he said, after a long silence. "Do you understand that the boy has been waking up at four in the morning for six months, unable to breathe, and his mother has taken him to three different physicians?"

"Oh, that's just the chest thing," Mora said cheerfully. "Doctors never listen. They'll give him an inhaler and tell him it's asthma."

"It's not asthma."

"No. It's a lot more fun."

* * *

The adjudication dragged on through the afternoon. Witnesses were called. A snail who had been present at the scene testified that the meadow had smelled of clover and that the girl had been very gentle with him, which First Love found deeply touching. A goose from the periphery of the Dreaming claimed the whole affair had been staged for ratings and asked whether it would be eligible for compensation. The Clerk of the Court, who had hoped for a quiet day, sighed deeply and added the goose to the list.

Dream listened without moving. Every so often his gaze drifted to Mora, who sat with her legs crossed at the ankle, humming under her breath and looking entirely at home.

At last, Dream spoke.

"The dreamscape," he said, "is hereby returned to First Love, with the following conditions. The meadow remains, but Sophie stays a girl. She climbs on the boy's back, if she must, and they laugh about it. She does not eat. First Love will install a locked gate at the edge of the field."

First Love burst into delighted, shimmering tears.

"Mora," said Dream, "you are forbidden from entering any dream featuring Max or any child under sixteen for a period of one hundred years. You will report to the Lighthouse Sector twice weekly for supervised dream-work. And you will pay the boy's family a sum in the form of their most cherished memories."

"Fine," said Mora, looking delighted. "I'll take the grandmother's first kiss. It's very nice."

"You will not take the grandmother's first kiss."

"Oh, but I'd _love_ to," she purred, leaning forward, her grey eyes glinting. "Can't I just have a taste?"

Dream stared at her across the length of the bench. Then, very slowly, a faint, dangerous smile crept across his face, the kind that made the sky above the courtroom flicker briefly toward night.

"You may," he said, "have one taste of something I think you'll enjoy a great deal more than a grandmother's kiss."

Mora's eyes widened. The gallery stopped breathing.

"And what's that?" she asked, very softly.

"A dreamless sleep," said Dream pleasantly, "for exactly one hundred years. In a room with no windows and a ceiling that never stops falling. And a very long, very quiet afternoon to think about the meaning of the word _appetite_."

Mora sat very still for a long moment. Then she laughed, a wild, delighted peal that rang up through the vaulted ceiling and made the sky above the Hall go briefly and entirely pink.

"Oh, you're _dreadful_," she said.

"I know," said Dream, and rose, and left the bench. "Clerk, adjourn the Court. I need a cup of tea and a lie down."

## 27. Blood Moon

The moon over the Carpathian foothills was a bruised, swollen thing, and the village of Varnsdorf had locked its doors against it. Shutters clacked shut in every window. Candles guttered behind sealed glass. A dog howled once, far off, and then the night was silent except for the wind combing through the black pines.

Alucard stood at the edge of the woods with his coat flapping about his knees, his wide-brimmed hat tipped low, and his orange sunglasses catching the moonlight like twin coins. He was not hungry. He was never hungry for the villagers. They were too weak, too dull, too eager to die for their priest's comfort. He had come here for something else.

He smelled her before he saw her: lilac and gooseberries, cutting through the damp pine like a blade through silk.

"You're late," said a voice from the shadows. "I was beginning to think you had lost your nerve."

"Nerve?" Alucard turned slowly. "I have never once lost my nerve, little sorceress. Only my patience, and that was centuries ago."

Yennefer stepped out from behind a split oak, her violet eyes gleaming in the moonlight. Her dark hair spilled down her back in a loose, careless cascade, and her black velvet cloak was fastened at the throat with a silver brooch shaped like a serpent eating its own tail. She looked young, almost painfully so, and she held herself like a queen who had never once in her life been told no.

"You're the one they call the Hellsing dog," she said.

"And you're the one who makes fools of kings." He smiled, showing a row of teeth that were a little too long. "I've heard stories. The Duke of Aedirn, who woke up with his own shadow in his bed. The mage in Kaer Morhen who tried to bind you and lost three fingers and his left testicle."

"He had it coming." She lifted one shoulder in a shrug. "He was a fool, and fools are my favourite target. They're so easy to rearrange."

"And you? Are you a fool?"

"Not for a single moment of my life." Her gaze swept over him, from the crimson coat to the long, pale hands, the silver-chased pistols at his hips, the sunglasses hiding eyes she had not yet seen. "You're a monster, Alucard. I can smell the rot under the cologne."

"You can smell the blood." He tilted his head. "You're fascinated, though. I can see it. You've been circling me since you crossed the valley. You want to know what I am."

"I want to know how you survived the Ottoman siege with a hole through your chest the size of a cannonball." Her voice was cool, but there was a hard, bright edge to it. "No mortal man can do that. You regenerated. I felt it from three leagues away, the way the air buckled when you healed."

"Ah. So you were watching."

"I'm always watching." Her smile was thin and dangerous. "It's the only way to survive in a world full of men who want to put you in a sack and drown you."

* * *

Alucard circled her slowly, boots whispering on the frost-crusted leaves. Yennefer turned with him, always keeping her body angled, her hands loose at her sides. Her fingers were already moving, faint blue sparks flickering across her knuckles.

"Careful," he said. "You don't want to hurt yourself."

"I'm not going to hurt myself. I'm going to hurt you, and then I'm going to ask you a question."

"What question?"

"Whether you can bleed for me." She raised her right hand. The sparks flared into a sudden, roaring blue fire, and the air crackled with the smell of ozone and lilac. "Or whether you're just a corpse that moves."

The fire leapt from her palm, a lance of pure cerulean light, and struck Alucard square in the chest.

He did not move. The flames burst across his coat, tore through the crimson cloth, and sank into the flesh beneath with a sound like wet paper crumpling. The light flared bright enough to whiteout the trees. For one terrible instant, the smell of cooking meat filled the clearing.

Then the flames guttered and died.

Alucard stood exactly where he had been, his coat smouldering in ragged black holes, his chest a ruin of blistered skin. As Yennefer watched, the blisters began to close. The skin knit itself together, flushing from raw pink to healthy white, and within ten seconds there was not a mark on him except the scorch on his coat.

"Oh," said Yennefer, very softly.

"Interesting, isn't it?" Alucard grinned, his sunglasses glinting. "I've been burned by better. Frozen, drowned, dissolved in acid. You'll have to try harder, my dear."

Yennefer's breath came quicker. Her fingers curled into a fist. Then, slowly, she let her hand drop.

"I didn't come here to kill you," she said.

"No?"

"No. I came because I need to know if it's true." Her voice had lost its edge. "The thing they say about you. That you're the only vampire in Europe who cannot be killed by anything except silver and the will of the one who created you."

"Who created me?" His grin faded slightly. "That is a very old story, sorceress, and a very dull one."

"Tell me anyway."

He was silent for a long moment. Then he stepped closer, until she could feel the cold radiating from him, the faint scent of gun oil and old blood and the bitter, mineral smell of the grave.

"I was a soldier once," he said quietly. "A Wallachian lord's son, sold to a Hungarian count when I was sixteen. The count was a sadist. He liked to bleed his servants slowly while he played the lute. One night he bled me too far, and I died, and when I woke up I was no longer a man. I was something that wanted to bleed everyone else."

Yennefer stared at him. Her violet eyes were unreadable.

"And the silver?" she asked.

"Silver burns. The cross is an insult. The sun is a nuisance. But only one thing can truly end me." His gaze lifted to the bruised moon overhead. "A person who is strong enough to make me afraid."

The silence between them stretched, thick and taut.

"Then I suppose," Yennefer said at last, very carefully, "you should be glad I'm not afraid of you."

"Oh, I'm very glad." His smile was genuine now, wide and sharp. "I've been waiting centuries for someone who wouldn't flinch."

"I flinched a little."

"Only with your eyes."

She laughed, a surprised, rough sound that seemed to startle her as much as him. The tension in the clearing cracked, just a little.

"You're insane," she said.

"And you're _fascinating_, Yennefer of Vengerberg." He took one more step, bringing them so close that she could feel the faint tug of his breath on her cheek. "I think I'd like to keep you around. Just to see what you do next."

"That's a terrible idea."

"I know." He leaned down, until his mouth was an inch from her ear, his voice a low, velvet threat. "But the most interesting things always are."

* * *

The moon sank slowly behind the pines. The first grey light began to bleed across the eastern hills. Somewhere in the village below, a rooster crowed, thin and indignant, and the shutters began to lift one by one as the villagers cautiously emerged to face another day.

Yennefer did not move away. Neither did he.

"Next time," she murmured, "you'll tell me your real name."

"Next time," said Alucard, "you'll try to kill me again."

She smiled, slow and dangerous, and stepped back into the shadows. "Oh, I intend to."

When she was gone, Alucard stood alone in the clearing, his ruined coat fluttering in the dawn wind, and touched the place on his chest where the fire had burned. His skin was perfect. Smooth and cool and entirely unmarked.

But somewhere beneath it, something was stirring for the first time in four hundred years. Something he had long since stopped expecting to feel.

He laughed softly, and the sound was swallowed by the wind.

## 28. Prison Strings

The Shelby Facility for the Incarcerated Supernatural smelled of disinfectant and burnt copper, and the corridor lights hummed at a pitch that set Jolyne's teeth on edge. She walked behind the guard with her wrists unshackled but her ankles still bound, the chains dragging soft sounds across the tiles, her hair pulled into its usual two tight buns and her butterfly tattoo still bright under the fluorescent glare.

"You're going to like it here," said the guard, not turning around. "The inmates are mostly quiet. The devils are very polite."

"I've been in prison for two years, babe. I know what polite means in here." Jolyne snorted. "It means somebody's gonna stab you in the back with a smile on their face."

"Charming."

"It's the truth, and you're a liar if you say otherwise."

They rounded a corner, and the guard stopped at a steel door with a window of thick, scratched glass. Beyond it, a woman sat in a plain white chair, hands folded neatly on the table, her auburn hair falling to her shoulders in soft waves. She wore a simple grey uniform, pressed and spotless. When she looked up, her eyes were ringed with yellow, bright and steady, and she smiled.

It was the sweetest smile Jolyne had ever seen. It made her skin crawl.

"Oh, you must be Jolyne," the woman said, her voice gentle as warm milk. "I'm Makima. I've been looking forward to meeting you."

* * *

Jolyne's first instinct was to step back. Her second was to lean forward and stare, very hard, into those yellow eyes. Her Stand, Stone Free, stirred under her skin, a faint itch in the threads that ran through her body like invisible wires. She could feel the pull of them, the way they wanted to unravel, to spill out and dance.

"You're a devil," Jolyne said flatly.

"Am I?" Makima tilted her head, amused. "Whatever gave you that idea?"

"The eyes. The smile. The way the air goes cold when you talk." Jolyne folded her arms. "I've met a few freaks in my time, sweetheart. You're the creepiest one I've seen since my dad's tenth stalker."

"Your father is Jotaro Kujo, isn't he?" Makima's voice was soft, almost fond. "He was very kind to me once, long ago. Before I knew better."

"He's never been kind to anyone who messes with his family."

"Then he and I have something in common." Makima gestured to the empty chair opposite her. "Please, sit. The guards are very nervous today, and I'd hate for them to do something rash."

"I'm not here to sit."

"Oh, but you are." Makima's smile widened, just slightly. "Your name has come up in my records. A murder you didn't commit. A frame job by a man with a very ugly family. You were sentenced to ten years, and you've only served two. That must be very frustrating."

Jolyne's jaw tightened. "Who the hell told you that?"

"A friend." Makima's fingers curled around an invisible thread on the table, so faint that Jolyne almost missed it. "Someone who wants you out of here. Someone who has a very great deal of influence in this facility."

"Yeah? And what do you want?"

Makima's eyes glinted. "Oh, nothing, Jolyne. Just a little conversation. And perhaps, if you're a good girl, I'll help you get home."

* * *

Jolyne sat.

She didn't want to. Every instinct she had was screaming at her to walk out, to call the guards, to get as far from this woman as possible. But something about the way Makima spoke, soft and sure, made her feel like she was already tangled in something she couldn't see.

"What's in it for you?" Jolyne asked.

"Ah. That's the question everyone asks." Makima leaned forward, her elbows on the table, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial murmur. "I'm very interested in people who have a special kind of power. Your Stand, for example. Stone Free. It's beautiful. You can unravel your own body into string and control it perfectly. Do you know how rare that is?"

"I get it. I'm special. Congratulations."

"You're _useful_." Makima's smile sharpened. "There's a man I'm very interested in. He's a devil, not unlike myself. Powerful. Dangerous. He has a very great deal of interest in people like you, and I'd like to make sure he has the right kind of interest."

"Sounds like a bad time."

"It is, for him." Makima's eyes swept over Jolyne's arms, her hands, the faint, fine threads that ran under the skin like silver veins. "Would you like to see what I can do?"

Before Jolyne could answer, the lights went out.

* * *

The darkness was total for three full seconds. Then the emergency lamps flickered on, dim and red, and Jolyne was on her feet, chains rattling, her Stand flaring through her body in a roar of silver threads.

Makima had not moved. She sat exactly where she had been, her hands still folded, her face serene. But the air around her was thick with something invisible, and Jolyne could feel it pressing against her skin like a fist. Her threads recoiled, twitching, afraid.

"You're not very good at hiding it," Makima said pleasantly.

"Hiding what?"

"That you're afraid of me." Makima's eyes glowed faintly in the red light. "It's sweet. Most people try to be brave. You're honest about it. I respect that."

"I'm not afraid of you, lady."

"No?" Makima stood, slowly, and walked around the table. She moved with the grace of a cat, every step silent, every motion perfectly controlled. When she reached Jolyne, she lifted one slender hand and gently brushed a strand of hair from Jolyne's cheek.

Jolyne flinched, her body going rigid. Her threads lashed out instinctively, a dozen silver lines snapping toward Makima's throat.

They stopped an inch from her skin. Trembling. Unable to move.

Makima smiled. "You see? You can't control everything. Not even yourself."

Jolyne's breath came ragged and hot. "What did you do?"

"Nothing." Makima stepped back, her fingers still curled in the air. "I simply asked your threads to stop. And they listened. Because they're yours, Jolyne, and you're mine now, in a very small way."

Jolyne's heart hammered. Her hands were numb. She could feel something cold settle on her shoulders, like a leash being drawn tight.

"Let me go," she growled.

"Of course." Makima's voice was warm, almost tender. "In a moment. First, I want you to understand something. There are people in this facility who would sell their own children for the chance to be like me. And there are devils who would tear the world apart to have me on a chain. You are very useful to them, Jolyne. But you are much more useful to me."

"Why?"

Makima's smile turned sad. "Because you're the only one who has ever fought me and lost, and kept fighting anyway. That's very rare. It makes you so much easier to keep."

* * *

The threads released with a snap. Jolyne staggered back, catching herself on the wall, gasping for breath.

Makima was already at the door, her hand on the handle, her back to the room.

"We'll talk again soon," she said over her shoulder. "Think about what I've offered. Your freedom. Your father's safety. A world where you never have to be afraid again."

"And what do I have to give?"

Makima turned. The red light caught her face, and for the first time, Jolyne saw something beneath the sweetness. Something cold and patient and vast, like the bottom of a very deep well.

"Obedience, dear," Makima said, very softly. "Just a little. Only at first."

The door clicked shut behind her.

Jolyne stood alone in the dim corridor, her chest heaving, the threads of Stone Free humming faintly along her arms. She touched her tattoo, the butterfly that had been her mother's last gift, and whispered, "No way in hell, lady."

But as the emergency lights buzzed overhead, she felt it again: the faint, thin, unmistakable tug of a string tied around her heart, leading back through the corridor toward a room where a woman with ringed yellow eyes was smiling in the dark.

## 29. Incident Report: Temporal Correspondence, Site-19 Research Staff

**Report Filed By:** Dr. Hannah Veil, Senior Researcher, Anomalous Cognition Division  
**Clearance:** Level 3  
**Date of Entry:** 14 March 2026  
**Subject:** Recurring handwritten communications attributed to self-authorship from a future date

* * *

I should start by saying that I have never been good at keeping a diary. My mother used to say I'd forget my own name if it weren't printed on my hospital wristband. So when I first found the note in the pocket of my lab coat, I assumed it was a joke from one of the junior technicians. They do that sometimes: slip cartoons into your desk drawer, leave biscuits with googly eyes stuck to the wrapping.

The note was written on the back of a Site-19 supply requisition form. The handwriting was mine. I'm certain of that. The same looping _g_, the same habit of crossing my sevens, the same way of pressing too hard on the downstrokes so the paper creases. The ink was blue-black, a Parker fountain pen, the one I haven't used since my PhD.

It said:

> _Don't go into Containment Bay C on the 19th. Tell Marcus to close the north vent. Trust nobody on the 17th who wears the grey scarf._

I laughed out loud. Then I realised the 19th was in five days, and I didn't own a grey scarf.

* * *

I reported it to my supervisor, Dr. Samuel Okoro, who listened with his usual polite scepticism and then, after a long pause, asked me to bring him the note. He read it twice, turned it over, held it up to the light, and said nothing for almost a minute.

"Hannah," he said finally. "Have you been sleeping?"

"Not much."

"Are you taking the prescribed medication?"

"Yes. Mostly."

"Okay." He placed the note in an evidence sleeve and sealed it. "We're going to have this looked at properly. In the meantime, no one goes into Bay C on the 19th. That's standard protocol for any temporal flag."

I didn't say anything. I was thinking about the grey scarf.

* * *

The second note appeared three days later, tucked inside the cover of my copy of _The Structure of Scientific Revolutions_, which I had left on the windowsill of my office. It was written on a yellow sticky note, in the same handwriting, but the letters were shaky and the lines ran uphill.

> _They won't believe you. They'll say you're doing it to yourself. They're wrong, but they'll be right about the pills._

I stared at it for an hour. Then I took it to Okoro, and he took it to the Anomalies Review Board, and by the end of the week I had been placed on mandatory leave pending psychological evaluation.

* * *

The Board's findings came back on the 9th. I'm copying the relevant section here, because I think it matters:

> _Analysis of the physical documents indicates consistent paper stock, ink composition, and handwriting matching the subject with 97.3% confidence. Stylometric analysis of the text shows deviation from subject's baseline language patterns in two respects: (1) increased use of present-tense future constructions, and (2) apparent knowledge of internal Site-19 scheduling data that is not accessible through standard clearance. The subject does not recall authoring these documents. Neither the subject nor the Board can rule out either a temporal artifact or a psychological event. Recommendation: continued observation._

I read that paragraph six times. I didn't know whether to be terrified or relieved. Either I was losing my mind, or something was reaching back to me through the years, using my own hand.

* * *

I returned to the facility on the 17th, against medical advice, because I could not sit at home one more day with the notes folding in my head like origami.

I was in the corridor outside the Bay C observation room when I saw him.

He was walking toward the stairwell, a tall man in a grey wool coat with a scarf wound twice around his throat. The scarf was grey. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

I didn't recognise him at first. Then he turned, and I saw his face, and my heart stopped.

It was my face. Older, sharper around the eyes, with a scar across the bridge of the nose that I didn't remember getting. He looked at me for a long moment, and his expression was not surprised. It was something closer to grief.

"You shouldn't be here," he said quietly.

"Who are you?"

"You already know." He glanced down the corridor. "Don't go into Bay C tomorrow. And when the alarms go, don't let them tell you it's a containment breach. It isn't. It's a door, and they're going to open it from the wrong side."

Then he was gone, down the stairs, his grey scarf trailing behind him like a banner of smoke.

I found Marcus Teague in the security office twenty minutes later. He was reviewing the camera footage from the corridor. He shook his head before I finished speaking.

"There's nobody on the feed," he said. "Just you, standing alone. Talking to the wall."

* * *

The breach happened on the 19th, at 14:52. Bay C's north vent failed, flooding the observation room with a yellow, sweetly scented vapour that made four technicians collapse before the seals engaged. The Site's containment protocols classified the event as a "minor atmospheric anomaly" and no fatalities were recorded.

I was not in Bay C. I was in the security office, with Marcus, watching the vent on the monitor. He had closed it, at my insistence, two minutes before the failure.

I still don't know how I knew to tell him.

* * *

The Foundation's official position, as of this report, is that the notes constitute either a temporal anomaly of unknown origin or a sophisticated psychological operation mounted by an entity contained somewhere in the lower levels, which has access to my handwriting and my personal history. The Board's review is ongoing. I have been assigned a new psychologist, and I'm taking the pills.

I have also been asked to keep a log of any further communications, which I am doing now.

I don't know if the notes are the future trying to warn me, or the past trying to shape me, or simply some entity in the dark enjoying the game. I only know that when I went to my desk this morning, I found a new note waiting under my pen. It is still sealed in its envelope. I have not opened it.

The handwriting on the envelope is shaking.

It is my handwriting, and it is not, and the last line I can read through the paper, before I put it down, says:

> _Please, Hannah. Stop trying to be brave. We don't have time for it._

I am placing this document in the Archive under seal. Whatever happens, I want someone to know I wrote it first.

* * *

_\[Addendum, appended by the Board:\]_

_Document received. Subject's note remains unopened in the evidence locker. Requests for access are denied pending review. Subject is advised to continue medication and observation. Do not, under any circumstances, open the envelope._

_Note: Subject's handwriting on this addendum does not match the Board's examiners. Please advise._

## 30. SCP-████ — "The Quiet Archive"

**Item #:** SCP-████  
**Object Class:** Euclid (provisional)  
**Special Containment Procedures:** SCP-████ instances are to be located, logged, and sealed by Mobile Task Force Epsilon-11 ("Night Shift"). Entry is restricted to authorized personnel. Recovered documents are to be catalogued in Site-43 Archive, sub-level 6.

**Description:** SCP-████ refers to a set of unmarked, identical wooden doors, approximately 2.1 m tall, found in abandoned structures worldwide. Doors are uniformly painted a matte grey, with no visible hinges, handles, or lock mechanisms. Upon opening, each door reveals a corridor leading to SCP-████-1, an architecturally impossible archive. The archive's interior volume has been estimated at roughly 3.8 × 10⁴ cubic metres, though measurements change between visits. Shelving extends in all directions beyond visual range, with ladders that are self-repairing.

* * *

**Exploration Log 17-B (Night Shift, Team Lead: Sgt. Ruth Adeyemi)**

_Entry 0214 hrs._ Team entered via Door 7 (Lowell Mill, Massachusetts). Corridor was cold and dry. Air smelled of lamp oil and old paper. Lighting is provided by fixtures on the ceiling that appear to be functional, though no power source has been identified.

_Entry 0231 hrs._ Reached first junction. Signage in a Latin-script language (not Latin) reads roughly "Department of Continuance." Signage appears to be a mix of English and an unrecognised script. Cataloguing begun.

_Entry 0248 hrs._ Recovered first document (see Recovered Materials, Item 17-B-001).

_Entry 0309 hrs._ Team encountered a sealed section of shelving approximately 40 m in length, with several dozen crates labelled in red. Crates were being removed by figures in sealed grey coveralls with no visible faces. Figures appeared to be using a pneumatic hoist to lower crates into a floor hatch. Upon sighting the team, figures immediately ceased all activity and retreated down the hatch. Team did not pursue per protocol. Hatch has since sealed itself.

_Entry 0327 hrs._ Recovered crate contents appear to be sealed paper records, sealed in wax, bearing a stamp that matches the Foundation's own emblem but with the triangle reversed.

_Entry 0352 hrs._ Team Member Pvt. Dana Kessler reported a sensation of "being read" while walking past Section C-14. Withdrew to safe distance. Kessler reports no further effect.

_Entry 0418 hrs._ Withdrawal. Door 7 closed behind team at 0422 hrs.

Result: several minutes are missing. Others appear to have been written in our own hand, dated after the current meeting. We have not yet determined whether we authorised this visit or whether it authorised us._

## 31. The Envoy and the Minister

The Minister's quarters were on the fourth terrace of the Old Ministry, above the saltwater cisterns and below the glass dome where the orbital lights came through in pale, slow bands. The corridor leading up to them smelled of dust and cold metal. Ieva Serrano had counted the steps. Two hundred and nineteen, not counting the switchback, not counting the stop she'd made at the balcony to look down at the city.

She had not been told to look. She had looked anyway, because the rule that said she must come straight up from the reception hall was a rule written by men who had never once walked the lower streets of Halim.

Her escort, a young Command officer named Pell with a face like a freshly ironed shirt, had stopped at the top step and cleared his throat.

"The Minister is waiting, Envoy."

"Yes. I'm aware of how long I've kept him."

"You were not lost, were you?"

Ieva smiled. "No. I was looking at the sea."

* * *

She had been reprimanded at six that evening by the Director of Command, a broad, pale woman named Oksana Ruhl, who had sat behind a desk the colour of old bone and spoken in the level, unhurried tones of someone who has rehearsed a speech to the bathroom mirror.

"You went out," Ruhl had said. "Alone. Without an escort. Against protocol, against the terms of your briefing, and against the explicit advice of the Halim Liaison."

"I went out to walk."

"You went out to _wander_. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a diplomat and a tourist." Ruhl had tapped a stylus against the desk, once. "You were in the Tanner's Market at four. You were seen in the Hollow Quarter at five. You stopped to talk with a fishwife in the Lower Canal for twenty minutes, and you bought a loaf of bread from a child who should not have been out after curfew."

"The bread was very good."

"Envoy."

Ieva had folded her hands in her lap. "I was told the Halimite people were a single people, divided by a single, shared grievance. I was told that the Ferryfolk were a violent and backward faction who had refused every offer of integration for three generations. I was told the Deep-Born were bred for labour and had no interest in the public square."

"And?"

"The fishwife was Ferryfolk. Her eyes are a shade of grey I haven't seen anywhere else in the system. She told me her daughter goes to school with the Deep-Born children, and that they have their own debate club, and that they argue about history in a way that makes her laugh. She said the Command men who come through the canal every week never ask her name." Ieva looked up. "I didn't see any violence, Director. I saw a city of people who have decided to be annoyed with one another in ways that are, I think, rather healthy."

Ruhl's stylus stopped. For a long moment her face was entirely still.

"Your report will be reviewed," she said at last. "You are instructed to attend the Minister of Science at nine tomorrow. He has requested the meeting personally."

"Dr. Halvorsen?"

"Minister Halvorsen is a political appointment, Envoy. He is not a scientist. Remember that."

"I'll try."

"You will do more than try." Ruhl looked down at her papers. "He will test you. That is what he does. He has been testing Command envoys for eleven years. Four of them have gone home early. Do not be the fifth."

* * *

The Minister's study was a long, low room lined with books that had clearly never been read. The shelves were arranged by colour, not subject. A single window looked out over the glass dome, where a slow, luminous arc of Halim's sister moon was rising. Minister Aurel Halvorsen stood beside the window with his hands clasped behind his back. He was a thin, elegant man in his sixties, with silver hair brushed back from a high forehead and a manner so relaxed it felt like a threat.

"Ms. Serrano," he said, without turning. "You're late."

"Two minutes, Minister. I stopped to look at the sea."

"Of course you did." He turned at last, and his eyes were pale and sharp, like the edge of a broken shell. "Sit down. The chair is comfortable. I had it imported from Earth, at great expense, and I'd hate for you to waste it by standing."

Ieva sat. The chair was indeed comfortable. She suspected that was the point.

"You went down to the markets yesterday," Halvorsen said, pouring two glasses of pale amber liquid from a crystal decanter. "Walked the Hollow Quarter. Spoke to the Ferry women. Bought bread." He handed her a glass. "Tell me. What did you think?"

"That the bread was good."

"That's the answer of a tourist, Envoy."

"Then I'm a tourist, Minister. But I'd like to think I'm an honest one."

Halvorsen's mouth twitched. "Honesty is a luxury, my dear. We cannot afford it in a place like this. Do you know how many people died in the Lower Canal riots of 2119? Eleven hundred and four. Do you know how many of them were Ferryfolk? Eighty. The rest were Command. Do you know which of those numbers the Central Council prefers to cite?"

"I imagine the one that serves the most people."

"Good. You do have a brain." He sipped his drink. "So tell me, Envoy. Why are you here?"

"To address the tensions on Halim, Minister. To assess whether the current integration policies are functioning and to recommend changes to Command."

"Yes, yes, the briefing. I read it. Very thorough. Very dull." Halvorsen set his glass down. "What I want to know is what you _believe_."

Ieva held his gaze. "I believe that the Ferryfolk are not a faction. They are a people with their own history, their own languages, and their own reasons for distrusting us. I believe the Deep-Born are not bred for labour; they are simply the children of people who were told where they were allowed to live. I believe that the people of this city want the same things every people want: safety, a fair price for bread, and the right to argue about their own future."

"And the Command?"

"The Command wants order. I understand that. But order without consent is only a quieter kind of war."

Halvorsen was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed, short and genuinely amused.

"You remind me of a woman I knew at university. She was going to fix the entire political system with a single hand-written pamphlet. She is now the Director of Command's Eastern Division, and she has learned to say very little in very many words." He leaned back in his chair. "You have not learned that yet. I like that."

"Is that why I'm here?"

"Partly." He studied her, his fingers steepled beneath his chin. "The Central Council is considering a proposal to reclassify the Hollow Quarter as a Security Zone. Curfews, checkpoints, the full apparatus. The Command would like to implement it by the spring. I, personally, think it would be a catastrophe. But I am not a scientist, and I am not Command, and so my opinion counts for rather less than I would like."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because you are going back to the Central Council in three days, Envoy. And because I want to know whether you will repeat what I said, or whether you will simply report that the Minister of Science is a dull old man who likes his chair."

Ieva considered that. The window behind him glowed faintly with the rising moonlight, and she thought of the fishwife's grey eyes and the child with the bread.

"I'll report that the Minister of Science has a very comfortable chair," she said slowly, "and that he is one of the few people on Halim who seems to understand what he is protecting."

Halvorsen's expression did not change, but something in his shoulders eased, very slightly.

"That," he said, "is the first useful thing anyone has said to me in six months."

* * *

He walked her to the door himself. At the threshold, he paused, one hand on the frame.

"Envoy."

"Minister?"

"The bread." His eyes glinted. "Was it really good?"

Ieva smiled. "Yes. Dense and sour. A little burnt on the bottom. The girl who sold it said her grandmother made the starter. She said it had been alive longer than the Command, and would outlast it."

Halvorsen laughed again, and this time the sound was warm, almost boyish.

"Then I shall buy a loaf tomorrow," he said, "and see whether it's still true."

She left him standing in the doorway, looking out at the slow, pale arc of the moon, and walked back down the two hundred and nineteen steps into the city.

## 32. Polymerase Chain Reaction

The lab had been a dentist's surgery once. Kestrel could still see the ghost of the old sign on the wall, a tooth in a cartoon grin with the letters chipped away, and the reclining chair had been stripped to its steel frame and bolted under a bench of salvaged centrifuges. The bench was wobbling. Everything in Ashwell Commons wobbled now, because everything had been built out of parts scavenged from places that had been abandoned for good reason.

Kestrel had a ring of fingers' width of solder burn on the back of her left hand and a fever that she had decided, that morning, was just the heat of the lamp.

Her PCR thermocycler was a Frankenstein of parts: a Peltier plate from a broken fridge, a microcontroller pulled from a dead tractor's dashboard, and a temperature sensor she'd rewired herself with the wrong-coloured wire. It sang a thin, tinny whine each time it ramped to ninety-four degrees, like a kettle on the verge of boiling. Kestrel had learned to hear the difference between the whine that meant _fine_ and the whine that meant _about to melt my samples into glue_.

It was the second whine she heard now.

"Shit," she said, and reached for the power switch, and missed.

* * *

The contract had arrived on the back of a dead man's ID card, delivered by a boy from the north road with a bruised cheek and the sort of smile that made you want to check your pockets. It was written in a shorthand that only the black-market brokers used, which meant it was from the Halcyon cartel, which meant it was going to kill someone, and possibly Kestrel.

_Verify viability of Strain 9-K in seed vault samples. Deliver proof of expression in three days. Payment: forty grams of clean antibiotic, two litres of water filtered to 0.2 microns, and a standing offer of safe passage through the Halcyon zone._

She'd read it four times before she believed it. Then she'd gone to the seed vault in the basement of the old library, carried in two crates of the last frozen samples in the settlement, and spent a whole night staring at the labels.

Because the labels said _Gen-7 Lot 3_, and Gen-7 was the thing that had been supposed to feed the world.

* * *

Corporate genetic engineering in the twenty-first century had never been about feeding anyone. Kestrel's grandmother had told her that when she was small, sitting on the cold kitchen floor while the power went out and came back again. _They built the seeds to last one season. Then they sold you the next bag. Then the bag after that. The world ran on subscriptions, love, and bad maths, until one day the maths ran out._

Gen-7 had been the company's pride. Drought-tolerant wheat, high-yield rice, a potato that was meant to grow in salt water. Its DNA was stitched with suicide sequences, self-degrading telomeres, a biological expiry date set to a single generation. Plant it, harvest it, and the second planting would simply fail, leaving the farmer to buy again.

When the supply chains collapsed, the company's warehouses had burned. The seeds that survived were in the wrong hands, or none, and nobody alive could reliably sequence the original constructs anymore.

Kestrel could. Or at least, she could try.

* * *

The thermocycler finally stopped whining. Kestrel slumped on her stool and pressed her palms into her eyes. The fever made the room tilt when she stood up too quickly, a warm sway in the bones, and she had a sour taste in her mouth that she was almost certain was not from the coffee.

She'd been taking the untested stuff for three weeks. Her own homebrew. A cocktail of partially degraded plasmids she'd lifted from a pack of the Gen-7 wheat, reassembled with a crude CRISPR kit found in a school chemistry cupboard in Harlow, and spliced into a bacterial vector she grew on agar she'd made from rotted potato peel. The first dose had given her a rash and a dizzy spell. The second had left her knees aching for two days. The third had done something she still didn't understand.

She could see better in the dark now. A faint, ghostly shimmer around the edges of objects, like the air above a road in summer. Her wounds closed faster. The scar on her shin, from a barbed fence in March, had faded almost to nothing overnight. Her periods had stopped, which the settlement midwife had called "a miracle" and Kestrel privately called "a warning."

She didn't tell anyone about the warm, sweet voice she sometimes heard at the edge of sleep, murmuring instructions in the language of codons and amino acids.

* * *

The knock came at dusk. Three short raps, then two long ones. The boy from the north road was back, his bruised cheek healed to a faint yellow shadow.

"Tell me," he said, stepping inside. "Are they viable?"

Kestrel didn't answer immediately. She was looking at the tiny tubes lined up on the bench under the lamp. Each one contained a cloudy, pale-green fluid that glowed faintly when she tilted it toward the light.

"Some of them," she said. "Lot 3 is mostly degraded. But the sequence in the wheat seeds from the eastern field is holding. It's expressing the drought gene. The salt-tolerance one, too." She swallowed. "It's still got the kill switch in the telomeres. It'll last one season, then it'll die. Same as always."

The boy's face didn't change. "Can you fix it?"

"Maybe." Kestrel picked up a tube, turned it over in her fingers. "But fixing it means synthesising new constructs from scratch. I'd need a proper sequencer, clean reagents, a lot more time than three days. And I'd have to make sure the new seeds don't die after one generation."

"Will they?"

"If I do it right? No." She paused. "They'll pass it to the next generation. Forever, if the code holds."

The boy's eyes narrowed. "Is that what the Halcyon wants?"

"I don't know what the Halcyon wants." Kestrel's voice was flat. "I know what Ashwell needs. We've had two failed harvests. The wheat from the eastern field is the only thing keeping sixty kids from being hungry next winter."

The boy was quiet for a long moment. Then he took a small brown envelope from his coat and set it on the bench.

"Forty grams of clean antibiotic," he said. "Two litres of filtered water. And a promise that nobody from the Halcyon zone will come through Ashwell's gates this year."

"And if I say no?"

He smiled, and the bruise on his cheek seemed to darken. "Then someone else in the settlement is going to find out what you've been taking, Kestrel. And they're going to want to know what it does to them."

* * *

Kestrel did not sleep that night.

She sat at the bench while the thermocycler ran its slow cycles, and the lamp burned low, and the wind moaned through the broken windows of the old dental surgery. The fever came and went in waves. Each time it peaked, she saw the faint, shimmering outline of the lab walls, and the sweet voice at the edge of her mind grew louder, almost fond.

_Good,_ it whispered. _Keep going. The next strand is easier._

She picked up the pipette. Her hand was steady.

Outside, somewhere beyond the settlement walls, a dog barked, and was answered by another. The sky over the broken city was clear and cold, and the stars were very bright. Kestrel thought about the sixty children in the Ashwell nursery, and the eastern field with its ragged rows of pale green wheat, and her grandmother's voice on the cold kitchen floor, saying _the world ran on subscriptions, love, and bad maths._

She'd spent her life learning to read the maths. Maybe it was time she wrote something better.

She pressed the start button. The Peltier plate hummed. The thermocycler began to sing, a thin, bright note climbing toward ninety-four degrees.

And for the first time in months, Kestrel listened to it without flinching.
