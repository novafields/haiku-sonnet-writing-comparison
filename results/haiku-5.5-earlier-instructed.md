## 01. The Azra Gambit

The transfer shuttle smelled of hot wire and somebody's overpriced cologne. Arthur Neegan sat with his knees against the seat in front, a paper cup of recycled coffee cooling in his hands, and watched Mars shrink through the porthole. Red, dusty, and indifferent to him, as it had been for thirty-one years.

Across the aisle, the two men in grey suits had not stopped looking at him since Ceres Dock.

"You're thinking about your mother," said the older one, not unkindly.

"I'm thinking about the lease," Arthur said. "Nobody gets an armed escort to talk about a lease."

"Nobody gets an armed escort to talk about most things," said the younger one, smiling. He had the mild, forgettable face of a clerk. "But you're right, Mr. Neegan. We aren't here to discuss your lease."

"Then what are you here for?"

"To take you to someone who wants to meet you. That is all we're permitted to say."

Arthur looked down at the insignia stitched on the younger man's sleeve: a stylized star, gold on blue, surrounded by a ring of thin silver lines. The Antares Diplomatic Corps. He had seen the emblem on import tariffs and the occasional stamped letter. Nobody he knew had ever seen one standing in his kitchen.

The ship lurched as the transfer drive engaged. Somewhere under the deck plates a generator hummed, low and reassuring.

"Do you know what you're sitting on, Mr. Neegan?" the older man asked.

"Azra," said Arthur. "A vein of it. Under forty meters of basalt and my compost heap."

"Under forty meters of basalt and a vein so rich that three survey companies have already tried to buy it from the Martian Authority. Two of them, I understand, failed to buy it for reasons that were unfortunate for their directors."

Arthur set the cup down on the tray table. "Are you threatening me?"

"No. We are, however, clearing the air." The man folded his hands. "A decay accelerator this potent is worth more than a navy. You understand what it does to a drive plate? Ten years of fuel in ten minutes of thrust. Every shipyard in the Belt wants it. Every war office wants it more."

"I'm a mining engineer," Arthur said. "I dig holes. I don't understand what you people do with the rocks."

"Then you're the perfect man to be here." The younger one leaned forward. "Because the people waiting for you do not care what you understand. They care what you can do with the vein."

Through the port, a dull silver station swelled into view, its spokes turning slowly against the stars. Beyond it, a long shape hung in the dark: a diplomatic transport, sleek as a dart, its hull painted the same gold and blue as the sleeve.

"Antares," Arthur murmured.

"Antares is a name," the older man said. "The people you're about to meet are the reason it means anything."

The shuttle docked with a soft, final clunk. Arthur stood, stiff from the long seat, and felt the weight of the gravity change beneath him as the ship's spin took hold. Behind him the clerk gathered his case.

"One more thing, Mr. Neegan," the older man said. "The gentleman waiting inside has a standing offer. He will make it once, very politely. You will have a short time to consider it."

"And if I say no?"

The man looked at him for a long moment, the way a chess player looks at a board he has already won.

"Then," he said, "you will discover that a politely made offer has a very long list of alternatives, and most of them are not polite at all."

The airlock hissed open. Warm air rolled out, sweetened with something Arthur couldn't place: ozone and dry flowers. Beyond it, a corridor of white panelling ran away into a distance that seemed slightly too long for the hull that held it.

Arthur stepped through, and the hatch sealed behind him.

## 02. Darkroom Revelations

The red safelight made everything look like the inside of an eyelid. I'd stopped noticing it after the first month. Now it was just the colour of the room, and the colour of Elliot's hands when he lifted a print from the developer tray, dripping and careful, like it might wake up.

"Is it working?" he asked.

"You tell me. You're the one who spent three hours on the exposure."

"It's a portrait of a bus shelter." He tilted the tray toward the light. "Nobody's going to care about a bus shelter."

"I care about it. The guy in the corner looks like he's about to lose a fight with a pigeon." I leaned over his shoulder, close enough to smell the stop-bath vinegar and the cheap oil from his hair. "That's the good kind of miserable."

He laughed, quick and surprised. Then he put the print on the drying line and didn't say anything for a while.

It was after midnight. Our project was due in four days, and we'd been pairing up in the darkroom every night since Professor Haas told us to work with a partner whose visual language we didn't understand. Most of the class had paired with people they already knew. I'd ended up with Elliot, who wore thrift-store waistcoats over black mesh shirts and silver rings on three fingers, and who said maybe six words in the first week, all of them careful.

Now he was saying more.

"Can I tell you something?" he asked. "You can say no."

"You're going to tell me something." I leaned against the counter, arms folded, trying to look relaxed instead of like I'd been waiting all semester for this. "Go on."

He picked at the edge of the counter with his thumbnail. "My mum found a binder in my room. Last spring. It had... things I'd drawn. Not drawings. Notes. Lists of names I'd thought about using." He swallowed. "She asked if I was trying to be a girl. I said I didn't know. I still don't."

I didn't say anything for a second. My instinct was to fill the silence, to say something clever and sure. That's what I do. I take the lead, I make the call, I'm the one who knows where we're going. I felt the old reflex rise, and then I felt it drop, because his face was turned away from the light and his shoulders were up near his ears, and he looked about twelve.

"Thank you for telling me," I said. "That sounds like a lot to carry."

"It's fine." He shrugged. "It's just... I haven't said it to anyone who isn't my therapist."

"Then I'm honoured. Even if I'm not sure what I'm supposed to do with it."

"You don't have to do anything."

"Okay." I nodded. "Okay. Then I won't."

We stood in the red light, the timer ticking on the wall, the prints drying in a row behind us. I was aware of how close he was, and how I didn't want to move away. I was aware of something else too: the question that kept rising at the back of my mind, the one I'd been ignoring since October. I'd always known exactly who I was attracted to. Tall, certain, a bit dangerous, people who looked like they knew what they were doing. Elliot did not look like any of that. And I still couldn't stop looking at him.

_What does that make you?_ the voice in my head asked. _Are you changing, or just finally noticing?_

"What are you thinking about?" he asked quietly.

"Honestly? I'm trying to figure out what kind of person I am when I'm not in charge."

He smiled, a small crooked thing that made my chest tighten. "I think you'd be fine."

"Would I?"

"You're the only person in this building who's ever told me my work was good without making it sound like a favour."

I laughed, a little shakily. "Well. Your bus shelter is genuinely good."

"Thanks." He hesitated, then reached out, not for me, but for the nearest print. He turned it over and pressed his thumb to the corner where the emulsion had lifted. "Dani?"

"Yeah?"

"Can you hold the light for me? Just for a minute. I want to try the other one."

I stepped back and picked up the safelight lamp. The red glow fell across both of us, and for the first time all semester, I didn't know what was going to happen next, and I didn't mind.

## 03. Stolen Moments in Roma

Alessandra came around the corner of Via della Stamperia at forty kilometres an hour, which was too fast for the street and about twenty too fast for the tourists who had spread across it like spilled sugar. Her Vespa leaned into the bend. A man in a linen jacket shouted. A pigeon gave up its place on a bench.

And a tall Englishman in a rumpled blue shirt stepped off the pavement with his eyes fixed on a guidebook, two metres from the front wheel.

She stood on the brakes. The scooter fishtailed, slid, and stopped with its front tyre nudging the sole of his left shoe.

"_Porca miseria_," Alessandra said. "Are you blind or just stupid?"

He looked up slowly, guidebook still open in one hand, face going pink from collar to hairline. "I'm so terribly sorry. Entirely my fault. I was reading about the fountain. Did you know it was designed in the eighteenth century, but the aqueduct it runs on is two thousand years old? I find that astonishing. Are you all right? Is your machine all right?"

"My machine is fine. You are standing on my front wheel."

He glanced down, horrified, and stepped back so quickly he almost fell into the gutter. "Oh God. Oh, I'm so sorry. _Mi dispiace. Molto mi dispiace._ Is that right? I've been practising."

Alessandra stared at him. Then she burst out laughing, a loud, unguarded sound that turned several heads on the pavement.

"You practise apologising in Italian?"

"I practise everything in Italian. It's my sabbatical project. I'm writing a paper on Baroque hydraulics and I've come to Rome to be humiliated by native speakers." He pushed his glasses up his nose. "So far it's going very well."

"You're hopeless."

"Yes. Quite hopeless. My name is Ben, by the way."

"Alessandra. And you should look where you're walking, Ben, because next time I might not be quick enough." She pulled off her helmet and shook out her dark hair, sharp-eyed and grinning. "Come on. You owe me a coffee."

"I'd be delighted."

"Good. But you pay, and you don't talk about aqueducts."

* * *

The café was a narrow room with a wooden counter worn to a dull shine. Alessandra ordered two espressos without asking him, then leaned against the bar and watched him fumble for his wallet.

"You're going to tell me your whole life story in the next five minutes, aren't you?" she said.

"Only the interesting bits. Lecturer in architectural history at a university in Leeds. Divorced, amicably. Here on sabbatical because my ex-wife said I was 'vanishing into my own footnotes.' She was right, which was infuriating." He sipped the coffee and winced. "This is extraordinary."

"It's a coffee. Not a sculpture."

"In Leeds, it would be a mistake."

She shook her head, but she was smiling. Then her gaze slipped to the till, where a small silver spoon sat by the register. Ben didn't see her fingers move. Neither did the barista, who was busy with the steam wand. One second the spoon was there, the next it was gone, sliding into the lining of Alessandra's jacket.

"What was that?" Ben asked, sharply.

"What was what?"

"Nothing." He frowned. "I thought I saw..."

"You're seeing things. Scholarly eyes are notoriously unreliable."

She laughed and finished her espresso, and Ben, who had spent nine years learning to trust the evidence of his senses, decided to let it go.

* * *

They went to the Trevi an hour later. Alessandra led him through the crowd with a hand flat in the small of his back, steering him with the authority of someone who had done this a thousand times. At the edge of the basin, she stopped.

"Throw a coin," she said.

"Over my shoulder?"

"Over your shoulder, with your right hand, and don't look."

He did. The coin flashed and sank into the turquoise water.

"Now what do I wish for?"

"You don't tell me."

"But what if it doesn't come true?"

"Then you didn't wish hard enough." Her eyes were bright, teasing. "Or maybe the fountain's a thief. Like me."

He looked at her sideways. "Are you a thief?"

"Only in the best possible way."

Her hand brushed his arm. He felt the pull of it, a current running through his chest, and told himself it was the heat.

* * *

By early afternoon, he had been dragged onto the back of a delivery scooter through the Monti district, handed a paper cone of fried artichoke by a shopkeeper who called him _professore_, and sent up a rickety flight of stairs to a rooftop terrace where Alessandra's cousin was hanging laundry above a view of the Colosseum. He had also, somehow, carried a crate of wine bottles for a restaurant owner who had never seen Alessandra before in his life and seemed to find her a force of nature.

"You do this often?" Ben asked, sitting on a low wall with a bruised shin and a grin he could not suppress.

"Only when someone needs a hand." She tilted her head. "Or when someone looks like they've never had fun in their life."

"I'm having fun."

"You're having a complicated time. Let's see if we can make it simple."

At the Quirinale she talked her way past a security guard with a story about a missing cat. At the Pantheon she swapped the sugar bowls on two café tables while the waiters argued. Ben watched it all, appalled and fascinated. He caught her once, in a shop near the Campo de' Fiori, tucking a little brass compass into her pocket, and when he raised an eyebrow she only shrugged, unrepentant.

"You're a criminal."

"I'm a _romantic_."

"Those can't be the same thing."

"They are in Rome."

* * *

At sunset they ended up on the Ponte Sisto, leaning on the railing with the river sliding gold beneath them. Ben was quiet for a long time. Alessandra watched him from the corner of her eye.

"You're going to leave tomorrow," she said.

"Yes. My flight's in the afternoon."

"And you'll go back to Leeds and write about fountains."

"I'll write about fountains." He turned toward her. "Alessandra, what were you doing today? Really?"

She said nothing for a moment. Then she smiled, small and private, the first unguarded thing he'd seen her do all day.

"Showing you that the city isn't a museum," she said. "It's a fight. And you have to decide if you want to be in it."

"I think I've been in it since you nearly ran me over."

"Good." She pushed off the railing. "Then you're not hopeless yet."

She walked away down the bridge, hands in her jacket pockets, the way she'd walked into the café that morning. Ben stood alone for a moment. Then he laughed out loud, and realised his heart was beating faster than the traffic on the Lungotevere.

When he looked down, the brass compass was in his own hand. She had palmed it onto him sometime in the last hour, and he had no idea when.

He stared at it. Then he ran after her.

## 04. The Last Outpost

The Last Outpost smells like spilled ale, ozone from the reclaimer vents, and the cinnamon thing Old Mo insists on frying on the grill in the back. I can smell it from behind the bar, which is why I know the new guy is a problem before he even opens his mouth.

Nobody comes in looking like that. Tentacled dreads, a grey-green coil of them, half-undone and dragging across one shoulder like a drunk snake. His jacket is torn at the left sleeve and there's a dark stain on the collar that is not, I'm pretty sure, ale. He drops onto the stool at the end of the bar as if someone just pulled his strings.

"Vega whisky," he says. "Neat. Better make that a double."

I reach for the good bottle, the one with the cracked label, and pour. Two fingers. Then three, because he looks like he needs them.

"You're not from the belt," I say.

"No." He knocks the glass back in one swallow, then pushes it toward me without looking. "Refill. Please. I'm told it's a pleasant town."

"It's a bar. The town's out there." I jerk my chin at the viewport, where the Andromeda rim hangs in a frost of cold stars. "What's your name?"

"Does it matter?"

"It does if you're going to run up a tab I won't see paid."

He finally looks at me. His eyes are a pale yellow, flecked with something darker that moves when he blinks. A tentacle twitches against his cheek, slow and deliberate, like it's listening.

"Corwin," he says. "Corwin Vale. I have money."

"Everyone has money. The question is whether it's yours."

His mouth thins. He doesn't answer, just pulls a credit chip from his jacket and sets it on the bar. The light catches the logo. A Ceres Mining Consortium stamp, and beneath it, scratched across the surface, a single word in someone's sloppy handwriting: _RUN_.

I don't touch it.

"You're in trouble," I say.

"I'm always in trouble." He drinks. "Usually I'm the one who causes it. This time I'm the one it's looking for."

"Who's looking?"

"The people I used to work for. Or the people who used to pay me to forget things." He sets his glass down very carefully, the way drunks do when they're trying not to show how shaky they are. "I was an auditor. For the consortium. I found the thing they didn't want found in the reclaimer ledgers. Then I found out the thing they didn't want found was me."

"That's a bad afternoon."

"That's a bad decade." He laughs, low and humourless. "Your reclaimer vents are very loud, by the way. Are they always like that?"

"Only when someone's lying next to them."

He snorts into his whisky. For a second something almost like warmth crosses his face. Then it's gone.

"You don't trust me," he says.

"I don't trust anybody who walks in here looking like they've been dragged through a recycler. It's not personal."

"Good. Then we understand each other." He leans in, lowering his voice. "I need a room for the night. Somewhere that doesn't keep records. And I need someone who knows the loading docks on the south ring."

I glance at the back door, where Mo is shouting at a crate of bean paste. "Why would I help you?"

"Because I'll pay you more than the whisky is worth." A pause. "And because the men coming here tonight aren't looking for an auditor. They're looking for a woman with a cracked ceramic mug and a very bad temper."

My hand freezes on the bottle. "They're looking for me?"

He doesn't answer right away. The tentacle on his cheek curls tight. Then he says, very quietly, "Nettle, isn't it? Your friends call you Nets. The ones who still remember your old name are the dangerous ones."

I set the bottle down. My heart is doing something uncomfortable.

"Mo," I call over my shoulder, not turning around. "Lock the back door. And get me the thing under the counter."

"What thing?" Mo shouts back.

"The thing you told me never to use."

Corwin raises his glass to me. "Good," he says. "I was hoping you'd still have it."

## 05. Gone South

The bus station in New Orleans smells like fried dough, diesel, and a sweet rot I can't name, and the first thing I learn is that nobody looks at you twice here if you're wearing a hoodie with the hood up. That's the good news. The bad news is that the hood keeps falling down because I'm too tired to keep it up, and every time it drops, my hair is out, and every time my hair is out, somebody notices.

I'm sitting on the curb outside the Greyhound terminal with my backpack between my knees and my phone at four percent. Two states. Two states since last night, when I climbed into the cab of a truck outside Memphis with a guy named Dale who called me "buddy" for six hours and never once asked my name. He dumped me on Canal Street at two in the morning because he said he had a schedule. I didn't argue.

Mom's texts are still stacked up on my lock screen. _Taylor please. Come home. We'll talk._ And under that, the one from Dad that's just a missed call, which is somehow worse.

I don't open them.

What I do open is the message from the number I saved last week, back when I was still pretending I might not need it. _You made it?_ Two words. Then: _Don't go into the Quarter alone after dark. Trust me._

I type back: _Who is this._

Nothing for a minute. Then: _Sable. From the shelter in Nashville. You gave me your sandwich in the bathroom line. Remember?_

I do remember. A girl with a shaved head and a bruise on her jaw, who looked at me like I was a dog that might bite and said thanks anyway. I'd thought she was going to rob me. She just wanted the sandwich.

_Where are you?_ I type.

_Bywater. Pinkberry's on the corner. Come get a coffee, I'll buy._

_Why would you buy me a coffee?_

_Because you look like you haven't eaten since Tennessee, and I know that look._

* * *

Pinkberry's turns out to be a rusted diner with pink neon in the window and a cat asleep on the counter. Sable is sitting in the back booth with her feet up on the bench. She's older than I thought, maybe nineteen, with silver hoops in both ears and a leather jacket that's too big for her.

"Sit," she says. "You look like a ghost."

I sit. The waitress brings two coffees without asking. I wrap my hands around the mug and try not to cry, which is stupid, because it's just coffee.

"So," Sable says. "Who's chasing you?"

"Nobody."

"Liar." She grins, showing a chipped front tooth. "Everybody's chasing somebody. What'd you do? Steal a car?"

"I didn't steal anything. I just... left."

"Left what?"

"My house. My family. My name." I stop. It sounds dramatic when I say it out loud, even though it isn't. "My mom doesn't know who I am. I told her last week, and she said it was a phase. Then she said it was a sin. Then she said she'd stop eating if I didn't change my mind about the name."

Sable's grin fades. "Shit."

"Yeah."

"You're trans?"

"Yeah."

She nods slowly, like she's filing it somewhere. "Okay. My dad kicked me out when I was fourteen for being queer, so I'm not gonna pretend I know your whole deal. But you can't stay near the Quarter. Too many guys looking for kids off the bus."

"I'm sixteen," I say, too loud. "I know what I am."

"Okay." She holds up both hands. "Okay. Sixteen and smart as hell, I can see that. But listen. There's a shelter on Dauphine that doesn't ask questions. They'll take you tonight. But don't tell them your real last name, and don't tell anyone where you came from."

I stare at her. "Why are you helping me?"

She looks out the window at the traffic. The cat stretches and falls asleep again.

"Because when I got here, the first person who helped me was a runaway who wouldn't tell me her name either," she says. "And I still think about her every day."

My throat aches. I drink the coffee, which is terrible, and let the warmth spread through my chest.

"What's your real name?" I ask.

Sable hesitates. Then she leans forward, very quiet. "Tell me yours first."

I open my mouth, and for the first time since last night, I feel something other than panic. It's small and uncertain, but it's mine.

"Taylor," I say. "Taylor's my name. The one I picked."

She smiles, and this time it doesn't look like a dare.

"Okay, Taylor," she says. "Welcome to New Orleans. Don't get eaten."

## 06. My Apocalypse Buddy

The apocalypse had been over for eleven years, and Midas still had the same problem it had always had: he was about to do something stupid and he knew it.

"Chewy," he said. "The plan is simple."

Chewy, the animo-tronic koala, sat on the kitchen table with one button eye slightly askew and a fur coat that had been patched with duct tape by someone who had clearly not been concentrating. He blinked once. His mouth opened on a hinge with a sound like a rusty gate.

"_Heeerrrk_," said Chewy.

"Exactly," said Midas. "Simple. We walk to the Old Tesco Superstore, we find the emergency generator, and we bring it home. Nobody dies. Nobody gets cursed by a regional council. We're back by lunch."

"_Heeerrrk_."

"What do you mean, that never works?"

"_Heeerrrk_. _Heeerrrk_."

Midas sighed and reached for his backpack, which contained a rusted tin of beans, a half-sharpened machete, three batteries of unknown vintage, and his lucky spanner, which had once saved his life in a crisis involving a tortoise. He checked the straps. He checked the spanner. He did not check the map, because it had been eaten by a goat in the spring.

"We're going," he said. "Get in the bag."

Chewy got in the bag with the dignity of someone who had been carried into a hundred bad decisions and expected a hundred more.

* * *

The Tesco car park had been a lake for six years and was now something closer to a marsh with ambitions. Midas waded through a foot of brown water, holding Chewy above his head, while frogs the size of dinner plates watched him with deep suspicion.

"Are those frogs, or are they just big and offended?" he muttered.

"_Heeerrrk_."

"Yes, I see the sign."

The sign said _DANGER: ELECTRIC FENCE_. It had been partially eaten by something. The fence, which had once been a neat line of galvanised posts, now sagged under a rusted tangle of razor wire and what looked suspiciously like a bicycle.

Midas stopped and considered it.

"We could go around."

"_Heeerrrk_."

"We could go over."

"_Heeerrrk_."

"We could go under, if we were small and sensible."

Chewy's ears twitched. Midas took that as agreement, which was a mistake, because the moment he crawled under the wire, the bicycle slid off the top of the fence and landed on his chest, and the entire structure collapsed with a noise like a dropped piano.

When the dust settled, Midas was lying flat on his back with a bicycle on his stomach and Chewy sitting calmly on his face.

"_Heeerrrk_," Chewy said, almost apologetically.

"I'm fine," Midas croaked. "Absolutely fine. This is the plan working. We're inside."

* * *

The superstore had been stripped to the walls years ago, but the staff room in the back still had a locked cage. Inside it, under a pile of cracked tiles, sat a grey metal box with a yellow sticker that read _GENERATOR, DO NOT DROP_.

Midas had clearly not read the sticker.

He had lifted the box halfway when the floor beneath it gave way with a long, sad creak, and the whole thing tipped sideways, pulling him after it into a hole filled with cold rain and an alarming number of tins of cat food. He surfaced spitting water. The generator bobbed next to him, its yellow sticker peeling away like a sunburn.

"Chewy," he said, grabbing the edge of the hole. "Are you still in the bag?"

Chewy's head poked out of the rucksack on the far side of the room, where it had apparently been thrown by the collapse. Both button eyes were wide.

"_Heeerrrk_."

"Yes, I'm also surprised we're alive."

* * *

They got home at dusk, which was not lunch. Midas came up the garden path dragging the generator on a length of rope, soaked to the skin, his left boot full of frog, his right hand bandaged in a sock. Chewy rode on top of the generator like a conquering general, one ear missing, a new strip of tape across his nose.

"Success," Midas announced to the empty kitchen.

Chewy blinked.

"Yes, all right, there were some problems. We found the generator, we nearly drowned, we discovered the cat food, we met a frog who wanted to fight me. But we're here, we have the generator, and nobody died." He paused. "The council will have to be dealt with, of course. But not today."

"_Heeerrrk_," said Chewy, with a tone that suggested the council had already been dealt with and had not enjoyed it.

Midas looked at the generator. He looked at his soggy boots. He looked at the koala, who looked back at him with the placid, mildly disappointed patience of a creature that had seen the end of the world and found it rather overrated.

"Tomorrow," Midas said, "we find a map."

"_Heeerrrk_."

"No, I don't know where the goat is."

## 07. Love in the Limelight

"Do you have a book about Dylan Thomas?"

The bell over the door had barely stopped jangling when the man said it. He was leaning against the poetry shelf with his back to the window, one hand raised to his face, a pair of enormous dark sunglasses pushed halfway down his nose. Outside, through the rain-streaked glass, a small crowd of camera phones and one very determined photographer were pressed against the pavement.

"We have three," said the owner, without looking up from the till. "One's in the window. One's for children. One's a biography that made Thomas sound like a horse-racing tipster."

"Which one do you recommend?"

"The one that doesn't sound like a horse-racing tipster."

"That's the one I'm not buying." He turned, flashing a grin that could have sold a car. "Hello. I'm Julian."

"I know who you are. Everyone on the Welsh coast knows who you are. You're in the window of the petrol station, on a bus shelter, and on the side of my neighbour's van."

"Ah. Yes. That's the campaign." He tilted his head, studying her. "And you're not impressed."

"I'm busy."

"You're reading a receipt."

"I'm adding it up."

He stepped toward the counter. "Would you be terribly offended if I hid behind your bookshelves until the photographers have wandered off? I'll buy something. I'll be very quiet. I'm an excellent hider. I was a child actor."

"You were a child actor in a soap opera set in a Yorkshire chip shop. I remember the episode where the shop burned down."

His mouth opened, then closed. A flicker of something crossed his face, quick and wounded, before the grin came back.

"Dear God. You're a fan."

"I'm not. I just have a long memory and no television." She finally looked up. Her name was Gwen, according to the embroidered badge on her cardigan, and her eyes were the grey-green of the sea beyond the town wall. "You can stay behind the shelves. Don't touch the first editions."

"Those are the ones I want."

"That's why I said it."

* * *

He wandered the aisles with his hands in his coat pockets, humming something under his breath. Every few minutes he would glance at her across the shop, and every time she would be looking at something else: the shelf labels, the stock list, a tiny spider working on the window frame. He made a show of pulling out a battered copy of _Under Milk Wood_ and holding it up like a trophy.

"Look," he said. "A book about a village. You'd like this. It's full of people who are wonderfully unimpressed by everything."

"That's why I stock it."

"You're very hard to charm, Gwen."

"You haven't tried."

"I've tried _everything_. I did a bit about the sheep in _Coronation Street_. I did a monologue from _Macbeth_ in a Welsh accent. The bus driver cried."

"The bus driver was probably having a bad day."

He laughed, genuinely this time, and it changed his whole face. For a second he looked younger, shabbier, more real, and Gwen felt an unwelcome flutter under her ribs.

She cleared her throat. "Do you want the book or not?"

"I want to know why you're not impressed by me."

"Because you're trying too hard to be liked."

The silence that followed was thick enough to cut. Julian stared at her. Then he slowly pulled off the sunglasses and folded them into his breast pocket.

"That's rude," he said.

"It's accurate."

"It's rude _and_ accurate, and nobody has ever said that to me in a bookshop before."

"Well, it's a small town."

"It's a very small town." He stepped closer, leaning on the counter, close enough that she could smell rain and expensive soap. "You know what I think?"

"I'm sure you'll tell me."

"I think you're hiding behind your receipts because you don't want to be impressed."

Her heart gave an uncomfortable thump. She put down the pencil. "And what do you think I'm impressed by?"

"Honestly? I think you'd prefer someone who gets the first edition's binding wrong on purpose so you'll correct them."

"That is an extremely specific fantasy."

"I'm a specific man."

She laughed, too quickly, and then immediately felt annoyed at herself for it. He grinned, pleased, and tapped the counter twice.

"I'll buy the Thomas," he said. "The one that sounds like a horse-racing tipster. For the sheer audacity of it."

"Fine."

She rang it up. Their fingers brushed as he handed her the note. Neither of them moved away for a moment too long.

"Gwen," he said quietly. "What time do you close?"

"Six."

"Would you let me come back at six? Without the sunglasses. Without the bus shelter. Just to talk about why you're really reading the receipt."

She looked at the door, at the crowd still gathered outside, at the shop with its dusty windows and the soft Welsh rain beading on the glass.

"You'll have to bring your own cup of tea," she said. "I'm not making you one."

"That," said Julian, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It's a cup of tea."

"In this town," he said, backing toward the door, "it's a marriage proposal."

She didn't answer. But when the bell jangled behind him and the cameras swung around to catch him in the rain, Gwen found herself smiling at the empty counter, and the Dylan Thomas biography was lying open on the desk, the title page facing up, as if she had been thinking about it for a long time.

## 08. The Morska Conspiracy

The underhold smelled of brass polish, lamp oil, and something older underneath, like wet stone in a church where the bells had stopped. The iron door swung open without a sound, and Aya stepped through first, because she had always been the one who went first, even when it would have been wiser not to.

Katra came after her, a shaved-headed shadow with a pistol held low and a face that said she would rather be anywhere else. Simeon brought up the rear, carrying the lantern and a satchel of brass tools, and muttering prayers under his breath in the old dialect of the docks.

"Quiet," Aya said, though no one had spoken. "Too quiet."

The corridor opened into a vaulted chamber lined with bronze cabinets, their doors standing ajar. The lamp light caught the rivets and the arcane sigils scratched on the floor, faded but still faintly glowing. At the far end, a plinth of black marble held a sealed box the size of a coffin. Its lid bore the carved shape of a curved blade, and the faint hum of trapped immateria prickled across Aya's skin like insects.

"The scythan relic," Simeon breathed. "It's real."

"Of course it's real," Katra hissed. "That's why we're here."

Aya stepped toward the plinth. She had taken three paces when the lamp flared, throwing their shadows up the wall. A voice spoke from the shadows, low and amused.

"That's close enough, little moth."

Aya froze.

From behind the far cabinets came a sound she knew very well: the click of an expensive pistol being cocked, followed by the soft, certain rhythm of boots on stone. Figures stepped into the light. Four bodyguards in black leather coats, brass knuckles glinting on their fists, rifles slung over their shoulders. And in front of them, wearing a tailored grey coat over a blood-red dress, a woman with silver-streaked hair and a face Aya had seen only once, on a wanted poster in the aristocratic quarter.

"Madame Velka," Aya said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"Mistress of the Morska guild, and the woman you were supposed to be feeding to the magistrates at dawn." Velka smiled, showing very white teeth. "I was told there was a rival in my house. I didn't expect her to come knocking in person."

Katra's pistol came up. Three rifles swung toward her.

"Don't," said Velka softly. "I've already had one very bad morning. I'd rather not have two."

Simeon's lantern trembled in his fist. "They said you were arrested," he whispered.

"They said a great many things." Velka's gaze slid to Aya's face. "They said there was a masked thief in the guild, a girl with an aristocrat's voice who called up creatures from the immateria and made my men weep. I didn't believe them until I saw the trick you played on the Cardigan Street warehouse."

Aya's stomach dropped.

"Take off the mask, child," Velka said. "Let me see whose face I'm going to remember."

Her hands rose to the silver filigree at her temples. She hesitated, one heartbeat, two. The chamber was silent except for the hum of the box and her own pulse thundering in her ears. Then she lifted the mask away.

The lamp light found her face, pale and fierce, her dark hair coming loose from its pins. Behind her, Katra made a small noise of despair.

"Lady Aya Vantrell," said Velka, delighted. "Your father sits on the Board of Brass. Your mother was hanged for immateria magick in the square at Morska Fall, I believe. Did you come here to avenge her, or to finish her work?"

"I came for the relic," Aya said. She could hear her own voice cracking. "That's all."

"And did you think I would simply let you walk off with it?"

"I thought you were in chains."

"I am never in chains," said Velka. "I am always in the room where the chains are made."

* * *

They were bound quickly and efficiently with iron cuffs that burned as they clamped shut. The bodyguards pushed them to their knees on the cold stone beside the plinth. Simeon's lantern was taken and set on a bronze shelf. Katra had blood on her lip where someone had struck her. Aya's wrists ached, and her mind raced through a hundred ways out and found none.

Velka walked slowly around them, her heels clicking on the flagstones.

"Do you know what the magistrates will do with you, Lady Vantrell?" she asked. "They'll tear your name from the guild registers and hang you from the Morska Bridge. Your father will be made to watch from the gallery. The Board will sing hymns about the purity of brass and steel. Men will sleep soundly, believing they've killed the poison in their city."

"I'm not poison," Aya said.

"No. You're a mirror." Velka crouched in front of her, face close enough to smell her perfume, something cold and sweet like lilies. "You show men what they fear most in themselves: that they are not the masters of the world they built."

Aya's breath caught. "Then let me bargain."

Velka laughed softly. "With what?"

"With the only thing you want." Aya lifted her chin, though her hands shook. "I'm the daughter of a Board member. I'm the only one who knows how to open that box without destroying it. And I can call the immateria to heel, which your men cannot."

Velka's gaze sharpened. "Go on."

"Keep me alive. Keep my friends alive. I'll open the relic for you, and you'll have the scythan blade, the guild's power, and the ability to bargain with the Board on your own terms. Or you can hand me to the magistrates, and watch your city learn what happens when a brass man meets the thing he fears."

The chamber fell quiet. Somewhere deep under the stone, something shifted, and the hum of the box rose, low and hungry.

Velka rose slowly. For a long moment she studied the girl kneeling at her feet, her face unreadable.

"You're bold," she said at last. "I like that. It's a pity your mother was not as clever."

"She was clever enough to be afraid of you."

Velka's smile did not reach her eyes.

"Simeon," she said, without looking away from Aya. "Get the girl's mask. Then open the box."

Aya's blood went cold. The box was humming louder now, a sound like a choir of distant bells. Behind her, Katra whispered her name in desperation. Simeon hesitated, then slowly rose, lantern gone, his trembling hands reaching for the brass tools at his belt.

"Yes, Madame," he said.

And somewhere in the dark, the immateria stirred, as though it had been waiting for her all along.

## 09. Gunpowder Rendezvous

The bar was called the Lucky Ninth, which was the first lie of the night. Spike Spiegel had seen the sign from the street and walked in anyway, because he was hungry and the Ninth had a rumoured kitchen, and because the woman sitting at the far end of the counter with two pistols on the bar in front of her was not a thing he could walk past.

Revy didn't look up when he sat down. She tipped her glass toward the bartender, then took a long pull from a bottle of cheap whisky and set it down with a clink.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early. You're just drunk."

"I'm _always_ drunk. Doesn't mean I'm not on time." Her voice was rough, amused. Her right arm was covered in a dragon tattoo from wrist to shoulder, its scaled tail curling over her collarbone. The black crop top was half-tucked into shorts that had no business being in a city that cold. "You're the guy from the Eastside bounty. Spike, right?"

"And you're the one who put a hole in the warden's car."

"He had it coming." She grinned, all teeth. "I'm Revy. Two Hands, if you're a dumbass."

Spike lit a cigarette and blew smoke toward the ceiling. "You're after Vance Holloway?"

"Everyone's after Vance Holloway. Twelve grand on his head, and he's hiding behind a dead man's name in the Ninth Street motel." Revy tapped one finger against the bottle. "I'm going in tonight. You can come, or you can go home and eat your pride with a fork."

"I'd rather eat the cigarette."

"Then you'll starve."

"I've been starving since I was twelve." He raised his glass to her. "Let's see who gets there first."

* * *

The argument started over the money and ended over the bullets. By the time the bartender had cleared the second round, they had established that Spike was broke, Revy was reckless, both were lying about their last kill, and neither trusted the other as far as they could throw a Coke can.

"You left a woman behind," Revy said, staring at him over the rim of her glass. "That's what the file says. Some dame in a bar on Ganymede. You just walked away and never looked back."

Spike's expression didn't change. But his fingers tightened on his glass, very slightly.

"File's wrong," he said. "I didn't walk away. I ran."

"From what?"

"From a life that gets people killed." He stubbed out his cigarette. "Whatever happens, happens. I've already died once. I don't plan on getting attached to a second one."

Revy laughed, harsh and surprised. "You're a fatalist."

"I'm a realist with bad luck."

"Yeah? Well, I'm not a good person. Not a bad person either. I'm just here." She threw back the rest of her whisky. "So you can stop looking at me like I'm the problem."

"You're definitely a problem."

"Good. Keeps you on your toes."

* * *

The Ninth Street motel was a squat two-storey box with peeling paint and a flickering vacancy sign. Spike parked his bike in the shadow of a dumpster. Revy strode up the stairs without checking for cover, a pistol in each hand and her boots making the metal walkway ring like a bell.

"You're going to get us killed," Spike said quietly, catching up.

"You're going to get us _paid_," she corrected. "Come on."

Room 14 had its door cracked open. Inside, the lamp was on, and a radio was playing something tinny and sad. The smell of cigarette smoke and cheap cologne hung in the air.

Revy kicked the door wide. Spike slid in low, his gun tracking left, while she took the right side of the room. The bathroom door was closed. The closet stood ajar, a shadow beyond it.

"Holloway!" Revy shouted. "Come out with your hands up!"

Silence. Then the bathroom door creaked.

Spike fired first, a single shot into the frame, and the door slammed shut again with a splintering crack. A voice swore. Revy was already moving, diving across the bed, both pistols firing into the closet door, the mattress jumping under her weight.

The closet exploded outward. A man in a torn suit came spilling out, a shotgun in his hands, face twisted in fear and fury. Revy rolled off the bed and came up with her pistols level, but the shotgun was swinging toward her, and she had nowhere to go.

Spike's bullet took the shotgun out of the man's hands. The second shot took him in the thigh, and he went down screaming into the carpet.

Revy stared at Spike across the room. Her chest was heaving. Her hair had come loose, black strands stuck to her sweaty cheek.

"You missed," she said.

"I didn't miss. I hit the gun."

"You meant to hit the gun."

"That was the plan."

"Liar." But she was grinning again, sharp and wild. She holstered one pistol and stepped toward the fallen man, boot heel landing hard on his wrist. "Vance Holloway, I presume?"

The man groaned. "Who are you?"

"Your worst nightmare. Two Hands, if you're a dumbass." Revy crouched and grabbed a fistful of his collar. "Now tell me where the rest of the money is, or I start shooting things that matter."

Spike watched her, and felt something tighten behind his ribs. It was not fear. It was not desire, exactly, though he could feel that too, sitting in his chest like a live coal. It was the uncomfortable recognition of someone who understood exactly what it cost to keep moving.

"Revy," he said.

She glanced up. Their eyes met across the wreckage of the room, across the spent casings glinting on the carpet and the radio still whispering its sad song.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do the thing with the eyes."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like you're going to say something smart and then you don't." She stood, slowly, and kept her left hand loose at her side, pistol still in her right. "Because I'm not gonna trust you, Spike. Not if you save my life. Not if you kiss me. You'd probably run anyway."

"And you'd probably shoot me before I got the chance."

"Yeah." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the sweat on her collarbone. "So what's it gonna be, cowboy? Are we partners, or are we just two idiots in a motel room with a dead man on the carpet?"

Spike took a long slow breath. Then he dropped his gun into his holster, and held up both hands.

"Partners," he said. "For tonight."

Revy considered him. Then she lowered her pistol too.

"Tonight," she agreed. "And tomorrow, we see who's still alive."

## 10. Útiseta

The mound at Vallstad was the only place in the village where the snow never settled. Astrid said that was because something underneath kept it warm. I said it was because the wind came straight off the lake and scoured the top clean. She rolled her eyes and handed me a torch.

It was a quarter to midnight. The sky had that thin, bruised blue it gets in late June, when the sun only dips below the trees for an hour and then climbs back up as though it can't be bothered to leave. Everything looked wrong in that light. The birch trunks were too pale. The fence around the church had turned silver, like a wire left out in the rain.

"Put your coat on the grass," Astrid said. "Not the stone. The stone is for her."

"Who is _her_?"

"The one who's buried under us. My grandmother says her name was Hild, and she sat out here every midsummer until she went mad." Astrid grinned, showing the little gap between her front teeth. "Don't look so frightened, Linnea. It's just a story."

I put my coat on the grass.

I'd moved to Vallstad in March, after Dad's transfer came through from the power company in Stockholm. Mum had cried in the car for the first hundred kilometres. I'd sat in the back with my earphones in and pretended not to hear. The house we rented smelled of damp and pine resin, and the school had twenty-three students across all grades, and everyone knew everyone's grandmother. Astrid was the first person who talked to me without asking where I was from in that tone people use when they mean _why would anyone move here_.

She'd shown me the mound in April. I'd thought it was just a hill. Then she'd told me what her grandmother did here.

_Útiseta_, Astrid called it. Sitting out. You sat alone on a burial mound at night, with nothing but a blanket and your own breath, and the dead came to talk to you. Knowledge came with them. Secrets. Who was lying to you at school. Who would fall in love with you by autumn. What would happen to your father's job.

It was a game, I'd told myself. A stupid, eerie game. I'd agreed to come anyway, because Astrid had looked at me in the cafeteria and said, _You don't have any friends here, do you?_ in a voice so kind it had made my throat hurt.

* * *

We sat on the grass, back to back, the torch between us switched off. The cold came up through my jeans. Astrid was humming something under her breath, a tune with notes that didn't quite fit the scale.

"You have to be quiet," she whispered. "Breathe slow. Don't speak unless they speak first."

"Who's _they_?"

"Shh."

Something rustled at the edge of the trees. A bird, probably. Or a deer. I told myself that very firmly, and then I heard it again: a soft, deliberate step, like someone walking barefoot on moss.

"Astrid."

"Don't talk."

"Astrid, there's someone out there."

"There's always someone out there," she said calmly. "That's the point."

The step came again, closer now, and I felt Astrid's shoulders tense against mine. The hum stopped. The silence that followed was so complete I could hear my own pulse in my ears.

Then, from the direction of the church, a voice spoke. Not Astrid's. Not any voice I knew. It was thin and dry, like a page turning, and it said my name.

I stopped breathing. Astrid's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it hard.

"Did you hear that?" she breathed.

"Yes."

"Good. That means it's working."

I wanted to tell her to stop, to walk back to the house, to forget the whole stupid night. But my mouth wouldn't open. My body felt heavy and far away, the way it did in the dentist's chair when the anaesthetic took hold.

The voice spoke again, closer. "_Linnea. Who do you want to know about?_"

I thought of the girls in the cafeteria who laughed when I sat down with my tray. I thought of my father's face when he came home from the office, pale and tired, and said the promotion had gone to someone else. I thought of Astrid, who had never once told me her surname.

"Nobody," I whispered. "I don't want to know anything."

Silence. Then a low chuckle, very near my left ear, so close I felt breath on my cheek. I jerked away.

Astrid was staring at me in the faint light. Her face had gone very still, her eyes wide and bright.

"You didn't ask the right question," she said softly.

"I didn't ask anything!"

"That's what Hild said, too." She swallowed. "She was the one who didn't ask. That's why they took her."

The grass at my back seemed suddenly damp, as if something had breathed on it. From somewhere far below the mound came a sound like a heavy door swinging shut, deep in the earth.

I scrambled to my feet. My coat slid away into the dark. Astrid stood up too, slowly, her face pale in the torchlight.

"We should go," she said. "Now."

"Yes."

But as we turned toward the path, my torch flickered once, twice, and died. In the sudden dark, I heard something behind us. A slow, unhurried crunch of footsteps in the grass, coming up from the church. And a soft, ordinary voice that sounded exactly like Astrid's grandmother, saying, _Come back tomorrow, girls. You'll be better at asking then._

We didn't look back. I still don't know if I want to know what we would have seen.

It's been thirty years. I've never sat out on a mound since. But sometimes, in the summer, when the sun barely sets and the birch trunks go pale against the dark, I hear a step on the moss, and a thin voice says my name, very gently, as though it's been waiting.

## 11. Therapeutic Boundaries

The first thing Dr. Maya Okonkwo-Reyes said to me, four weeks after the surgery, was, "Okay, Cal, let's see what your knee has to say about this."

And then I recognised her, and my knee said nothing at all. It just went hot and cold under my skin, the way it does when someone you haven't seen in six years walks into a room you're trying to disappear from.

We'd trained together in Sheffield when we were nineteen. Two sprinters at the same club, sharing a bench and a bag of cheap oranges in the warm-down. She'd gone to university for sports science. I'd gone to the national team, then to the Olympic trials, and then the hamstring went and then the knee went and then nobody was calling anymore. Now she had a clinic on Fulwood Road and a clipboard with my name on it, and I had a body that betrayed me in every direction.

"Hi, Maya," I said.

"Hi, Callum." She smiled, quick and professional, and I hated the sight of that smile. "Lie back. Let's get your leg flat."

* * *

She had her hair tied up under a clip. Her hands were cool and dry when she slid them under my heel. I lay on the padded table in shorts and a grey vest, staring at the ceiling tiles, and tried to focus on the water stain above my head, which had the shape of a badly drawn Scotland.

"Okay, we're doing passive knee flexion today," she said. "I'm going to take your heel up toward your glutes, and I need you to let me do the work. No pushing. Just tell me when it starts to pinch."

"It's going to pinch."

"That's the point. Pain is information, not an enemy." She rolled her eyes at herself. "I sound like a poster."

"You sound like a poster from the nineties."

"Shut up."

I laughed, and for a second it was nineteen again, and her hand was tight around my ankle on the warm-down track, and the oranges were rolling across the concrete.

Then she lifted my heel.

The knee resisted, the joint stiff as an old hinge. I felt the back of my thigh tighten, the hamstring fibres stretching over the healing graft, and a sharp electric twinge flared at the inside of the kneecap. My breath hissed out through my teeth.

"Tell me," she said quietly.

"Pinch. Just there. At the front."

"Okay. Hold." Her thumb pressed gently against the tendon below my patella, testing the give. "Breathe out. Let the muscle soften. Good. That's it."

I breathed out. The pinch eased, just a little. Her fingers stayed where they were, warm now, the pads of her thumb moving in small circles over the scar tissue. The contact was precise and clinical, and it made my stomach knot.

_She's not thinking about you._ The thought came sharp, unwelcome. _She's counting degrees on a goniometer._

Except she was looking at me, not the knee. Her eyes flicked up and held mine for a second, then dropped away, and a faint flush rose on her throat.

Neither of us said anything.

"Okay," she said at last, her voice a fraction too brisk. "Let's do the lateral stretch. Roll onto your side."

* * *

I rolled. She moved to the other side of the table and slid one hand under my hip, the other flat across my shoulder, guiding me over. Her palm pressed into the bony ridge of my pelvis, and I felt her fingers spread to take my weight. Her breath was warm on the back of my neck.

"Relax your shoulder," she murmured. "Let me take it."

I didn't relax. I couldn't. My whole body was a locked gate, every muscle braced against the thing I'd been running from since I was nineteen. The thing wasn't pain. It was her, close enough to touch, close enough that I could smell the faint soap on her wrists and feel the tremor in her fingers when she adjusted her grip.

"Maya," I said, and my voice came out rough.

"Callum." Her hand stilled. "Are you okay?"

"Yes. No. I don't know."

She didn't move her hand away. Neither did I. We stayed like that for a long moment, my hip in her palm, the lateral stretch pulling my spine into a long arc that burned along my ribs.

"You're thinking about the trials," she said softly.

"I'm thinking about how you're the only person who ever tells me what hurts without pretending it doesn't."

She swallowed. I felt the movement under her palm. "That's the job."

"It's not the only reason you touch me like that."

Her fingers tightened, just slightly, then released. She took a step back, her face turning away toward the window. "Callum, this is a clinical setting."

"I know."

"And we're not nineteen anymore."

"I know that too."

She turned back. Her cheeks were pink, her eyes bright and wary. "Okay. Then we keep it clinical. Hold the stretch. Ten seconds. Tell me when it's gone past pinch and into pain."

I held it. The stretch deepened, my ribs groaning, and at the peak of it I felt the familiar bloom of heat along the outside of my knee, a deep ache that flared and settled.

"Pain," I said through my teeth.

"Good. Release." She eased my leg down. Her hand lingered on my knee for one heartbeat too long before she let go.

Neither of us mentioned it.

"Next session," she said, writing on her clipboard with a hand that wasn't quite steady, "we'll start weight-bearing. I'll need you to trust me with your full load."

"I trust you," I said. It was the truest thing I'd said all day, and it terrified me.

She didn't look up from the clipboard. But the pen stopped moving, and for a moment she just stood there, holding the paper, while the water stain above us went on being a badly drawn Scotland.

## 12. Opium and Oars

The morning bell at the factories had not rung yet, but Canton was already loud. Carts rattled over the flagstones of the Thirteen Factories. Somewhere behind the hong walls, a rooster was being argued with. The air smelled of river mud, charcoal smoke, and the sweet rot of the tidal flats, and beneath all of it, faint and familiar as my own pulse, the cloying smell of prepared opium.

I did not need to be at the naval office until noon. That was the first lie I told myself that day, and I told it with a clear conscience, because I had told it so often that it had worn smooth.

I walked out through the gate in my plain coat, not my uniform, and Wen was waiting by the water-stairs with his hands folded in his sleeves and a rain hat pulled low.

"Lieutenant Merrick." He bowed. "You are early."

"I'm always early, Wen. It is my great talent."

"Your talent is also visible from the street. The gentleman from the East India house asked after your health yesterday."

"Let him ask. I am in excellent health." I climbed into the small sampan that bobbed against the steps. My hands were trembling, and I pressed them flat against my knees so Wen would not see. "Where are we going?"

"To see Master Lou in the Gardens of the Eight Immortals. He has a new supply from Patna. Very pure."

"Pure is always a lie, Wen."

"Yes, Lieutenant. But it is a pleasant lie."

* * *

The river was full of traffic: rice barges, tea junks, a naval launch throwing a wake of oily foam that slapped at our hull. I watched it pass and thought of the captain's face at Monday's inspection, the careful way he had looked over my report, the sharp question about the Sophia's cargo manifests that I had answered without thinking. My hands had been steady then. They were not steady now.

Wen rowed in long, practised strokes. Each pull sent a jolt through the oars and into my shoulders, and I counted them the way I used to count powder charges, to keep my mind clear.

"Lieutenant," Wen said, without turning. "The Sophia's manifests. They were wrong. You know this."

"I know nothing about manifests."

"You know the Parsi merchant paid for the forged seal. You know the chest went missing from the lower hold. And you know the captain wants it back, and he will ask you again."

I laughed, too loud. "You talk too much for a boatman."

"I talk just enough to keep my head," Wen said mildly.

* * *

The Gardens of the Eight Immortals were not gardens. They were a maze of gutted houses behind the Cow Lane, roofs caved in, bamboo scaffolds rotting over courtyards, and inside them, the real trade of Canton went on quietly, in smoke-filled rooms with paper screens drawn tight.

Master Lou met me in a room that smelled of camphor and burnt sugar. He was a thin man with yellowed fingers and a silk cap far too fine for the neighbourhood, and he set a porcelain tray on the low table between us without a word.

"Lieutenant Merrick," he said. "You look tired."

"I am always tired, Master Lou."

"Then rest first." He lifted the lamp, and the flame danced over the dark pellets on the tray. "One pipe. On the house. For old friends."

"We are not old friends."

"We are, Lieutenant. You bought from me when you were a midshipman in your father's frigate, and you sold me the Admiralty's old chart of the Pearl River when you were cashiered from the _Endymion_. That is a long friendship."

I felt the old shame creep up my neck. "You're blackmailing me."

"I am reminding you." He smiled, showing a gold tooth. "The price of that chart was three hundred taels. The price of this pipe is nothing. Think of it as a loan against your future."

I looked at the pipe. The bowl was black and smooth, the bamboo stem polished by a thousand hands. I could already smell it, sweet and warm, and I could already feel the way the cold in my bones would drain away, the way the pounding in my skull would fade into a soft, forgiving haze.

_One pipe,_ I told myself. _Just to steady the hands._

I took it.

* * *

When I came back into the light, the sun had climbed over the roofs and the river was glittering like tin. I was sitting on the steps of a ruined temple near the Shamian bank, my coat open and my shirt damp. Wen was squatting beside me, holding a damp cloth.

"You shouted," he said quietly. "In the room. About the captain."

"What did I say?"

"You said he would hang you before he hanged the Parsi. And then you wept."

I stared at the cloth in his hand. "I am a naval officer."

"Yes, Lieutenant. You are also a man with a dragon in his chest. And the dragon needs feeding."

I laughed, a broken, humourless sound. "And what do you propose, Wen? Shall I take up fishing?"

"I propose you do one thing for me today. Then tomorrow you do one more. The Sophia's chest is in a warehouse on Hog Lane. The Parsi merchant needs a clerk to sign a receipt. You are the only clerk he trusts, because you once sold him a clean copy of the Admiralty roster."

My skin went cold. "You want me to forge a receipt."

"I want you to walk in as a naval officer and sign it. Nobody looks twice at a man in uniform. And the captain will never know, because by tonight the chest will be gone."

I looked at the river. It was still moving, still glittering, indifferent to the trade in its banks and the rot behind the hong walls.

"And if I refuse?"

Wen folded the cloth carefully and tucked it into his sleeve.

"Then Master Lou will sell your pipe to someone else," he said. "And the captain will sell your name."

I closed my eyes. Somewhere behind the wharf, a bell began to ring the noon watch, and I found myself counting the strokes, as I always did, one after another, until the sound died away and the dragon in my chest woke up hungry again.

## 13. The Hill Mail

Sisyphus kept his office at the bottom of the hill, in a hollow that the local goats had long since abandoned in favour of the lower slopes. It had a table, a chair, a cushion of moss for visitors who were not too proud to sit on moss, and a post for Crow to perch on. Crow took the post because it gave him a view of the boulder, which he regarded as a rival.

"Read me the first one," said Sisyphus, rolling a stone into place and sitting down.

Crow tore open the sack with his beak, found the letter with the heel of one claw, and began.

"_Dear Sisyphus. My husband leaves wet towels on the bed every morning. I have said nothing for nineteen years. Last week I bought a crossbow. Do not tell me to talk to him. I have talked to him. Yours, Marguerite of the Fourth Terrace._"

Sisyphus nodded slowly, the way a man does when he has heard this exact sentence from a hundred people and would like to hear it from a hundred and one.

"Take this down. Dear Marguerite. Nineteen years is a long time to be a footnote in someone else's towel. Keep the crossbow. Put it somewhere he has to ask you about. Then, once, without comment, hang up his towel yourself and leave the bed as it is. He will notice the bed. The towel will not be the thing he remembers."

Crow scratched it onto the parchment in his looping hand, which had been improving steadily since the second century.

"Next."

"_Dear Sisyphus. I am a junior associate at a firm of solicitors. I answer emails at two in the morning. I have started dreaming in subject lines. Please help._"

"Ah. Write this: You are not the only one awake at two in the morning, but you may be the only one who thinks it's a virtue. The people who sent those emails are asleep. Put your phone in the kitchen before you go to bed. Reply in the morning. Notice how few of the emails were actually on fire."

Crow paused, then added, "He will ask if he can keep the phone in the bedroom."

"Tell him no, he's already dreaming in subject lines."

The third letter was written in purple ink and smelled faintly of olives.

"_Dear Sisyphus. My mother-in-law has opinions about my casserole. She says it is 'brave.' I have been married eleven years. What do I do with brave?_"

Sisyphus considered this with the seriousness it deserved. He had, after all, been dealing with difficult relations for a very long time, and the gods were not on the whole kinder to their in-laws.

"Praise the parts that are good, specifically. 'The onions were very well done.' Then let the rest go past you like weather. She is not trying to hurt you. She is trying to be the one who gets the last word, and you can give her that. It costs less than you think."

There was a brief scuffle at the bottom of the hill. A goat, having eaten a page of the fourth letter, was now being discouraged from eating the fifth.

"The fourth one's a satyr," said Crow. "He wants to know how to get his wife to sit down for a conversation without her getting up to check the oven."

"Dear Hyllos," said Sisyphus. "Take her for a walk, ideally somewhere with no ovens. Talk while you walk. People find it easier to say difficult things when their hands are busy and no one is facing them. And do not mention the bet about the vineyard until you are both home."

"He's going to mention the bet."

"He is, yes."

Crow looked at the boulder. The boulder did not look back, which Crow found insulting.

"Last one. From a man who drove the number nine bus for thirty-one years. _Dear Sisyphus. I retired in spring. My wife says I need a hobby. I don't know what I'm for anymore. Is this normal?_"

Sisyphus put down his cup. He was quiet for long enough that Crow shuffled on his post.

"Tell him yes, it's normal. Tell him I don't know what I'm for, either. I know only that the hill is steeper on Tuesdays and that I keep turning up. Purpose is less a destination than a habit of showing up for something specific, every day, even when the something is ridiculous. He drove a bus. Every stop was somebody's stop. Tell him to walk the route, if he likes. Sit on a bench near the number nine stop and see who waits for it."

"That's a bit of a tangent."

"Write it down anyway. People always want the tangent."

Crow wrote it down.

Sisyphus rose, stretched, and put his shoulder to the stone. It was the same stone it had always been. It moved the same way, groaning on the first turn, then settling into the rhythm that made it bearable. Halfway up, the wind came down the slope carrying the smell of olives and goats and someone's bread, and he paused, the way he always did, to let the view catch up with him.

"Crow."

"Yes?"

"Good letters today."

Crow ruffled his feathers, pleased in the way that only a bird who has been called a rival for eleven hundred years can be pleased. He took the sack and began to fly, and the hill, on its long slope, went on being steep.

## 14. Den Den Town Sessions

The electronics street in Nipponbashi was so loud it made my teeth ache. Shop speakers blared competing pop songs, delivery bikes slalomed past pedestrians, and above it all came the sharp, electric crack of a guitar being played by someone who clearly did not care whether anyone liked it.

I stopped walking. My shoulders were already up around my ears, the way they always were in crowds. I should have turned around. Instead I followed the sound past a shop selling used keyboards, between two stacks of cardboard boxes, until I found the source.

She was sitting on an upturned crate near the entrance to a loud, flashing arcade, a battered sunburst guitar on her knee. Short black hair, a faded school jacket with the sleeves rolled up, a cheap amp at her feet with a tangle of cables that looked held together by hope. She was playing a fast, aggressive version of a theme from a famous anime, the melody bending and snapping over the top of the bass line she was creating with her thumb.

I knew the song. I knew every note of it. My hands moved on my shopping bag before I could stop them, fingers flicking invisible keys.

She noticed me on the third bar of the chorus. Her eyes narrowed, and then she grinned, sharp and sudden.

"Hey," she said. "You're tapping the wrong chord."

My face went hot. "I'm not."

"You are. Look." She hit the chord again, slowed it down, then mimicked my fingers in the air. "You're going to D minor, but the bass is on F. It's a sus4. It's supposed to hang."

I stared at her. "...It's a sus4."

"Yeah." She shrugged. "I'm Hikari. I busk here Saturdays. You're a synth nerd, aren't you? You've got that look."

"What look?"

"Like you'd rather be in a cupboard full of circuit boards than talking to a stranger in the street."

"I was going to go to a cupboard," I said. My voice came out small. "Eventually."

She laughed, loud and delighted. It made a passing salaryman turn his head.

"I'm Ren," I said, after a moment. "I like the synth stuff. Analog, mostly. Old Roland stuff. I was looking for a second-hand filter card."

"Ren. Okay." She propped her guitar against the crate. "Did you find one?"

"No. The shop on the third floor only had digital ones."

"Digital is a crime," Hikari said solemnly. "You should report them."

"To who?"

"To me. I'll sit here and glare at them until they feel bad."

I laughed, surprised at myself. The sound came out wobbly, but it came out.

* * *

For a while we just talked about the noise. She described the time a drunk tourist tried to pay her in dried squid. I described the time I'd accidentally soldered my own finger to a circuit board in middle school and had to explain it to the nurse. Hikari listened with her chin on her hand, eyes bright, asking questions that made me say more than I meant to.

"You don't talk much, do you?" she said eventually.

"Not to strangers."

"But you're talking to me."

"You're not really a stranger. You're a guitarist who thinks I'm wrong about chords."

"You are wrong about chords."

"I know." I smiled. My cheeks hurt. It felt strange, like a muscle I hadn't used in months. "Do you always play that song?"

"No. Only when people walk past looking miserable." She tilted her head. "You looked like you needed to hear something loud."

I thought about that. My chest tightened, not unpleasantly.

"Thank you," I said quietly.

Her grin softened into something gentler. "You know what? I'm starving. There's a conveyor belt sushi place on the next block, the one with the cat mascot. Want to come? My treat."

"Your treat?"

"Yeah. You can tell me what I did wrong with the bass line, and I'll pretend I don't care."

My stomach did something strange and nervous. I looked at the arcade lights, at the crowd moving past, at the battered guitar and the cheap amp with its tangled cables.

"Okay," I said. "But I'm picking the sushi."

"Deal." She stood, slinging the guitar onto her back with an easy swagger. "Come on, synth nerd. Let's go see what's on the belt."

* * *

The sushi place was narrow and bright, with a conveyor belt running around the counter and small plates of rice and fish sliding past under little glass domes. Hikari grabbed two stools by the window and began stacking plates in front of her with the enthusiasm of someone who had never once been embarrassed about her appetite.

"Tuna," she said. "Salmon. Egg. Oh, that one's got a face on it. Get that one."

"It's a sea urchin."

"Get it anyway."

I took one. It tasted of salt and cream and something like the ocean after a storm, and I made a face that made her laugh so hard she nearly knocked over her tea.

"You like it," she accused.

"It's interesting."

"That's a yes."

We ate. The belt kept turning. And somewhere between the third plate and the fourth, I found myself telling her about the analog synth I'd built out of old radio parts, the way it had gone wrong and sounded like a dying whale, and how I'd loved it anyway. She listened with a serious face, asking which resistor had failed. I felt my shoulders come down for the first time all day.

"Ren," she said, when I'd finished. "You're really good at talking about stuff you love."

"I'm not good at talking to people."

"You're good at talking to me."

I looked down at my tea, my cheeks warm. "Maybe that's because you're not scary."

Hikari grinned. "Thanks. I'll take that as a compliment."

"It was one."

"Then I'll keep it," she said, and reached for the last plate of tamagoyaki, and pushed it across the counter toward me without a word.

I took it. It was sweet, soft, and a little burnt at the edges, and for the first time in weeks, I didn't feel like I was waiting for something to go wrong.

## 15. The Watch That Ate Tuesdays

Mrs. Pell's class had a rule about electronics, and Owen Park had broken it on Monday, which was why the watch was sitting in his lunchbox instead of on his wrist. He had bought it at the flea market with birthday money, a chunky black thing with a red face and a button that said RW on the side, and the seller had said, very seriously, "Don't press that one unless you're prepared for the consequences."

Owen was twelve. He was always prepared for the consequences.

He pressed it on Tuesday, in the bathroom, because he wanted to see if it did anything.

The world went backwards.

It wasn't dramatic. The tiles on the floor reversed their pattern, the water in the sink climbed back up the tap, and the squeak of the door hinge ran in reverse like a tape. Then everything snapped forward again, and Owen was standing in the bathroom with his hand on the sink and his heart hammering.

He checked the watch. The time said 9:14 a.m. The school bell had rung at 9:14 a.m. He'd been late for second period.

"Huh," said Owen.

* * *

He tried it again on Wednesday, with a math test. He rewound the clock, read the question again, and walked into the room with the answers already in his head. Mrs. Pell gave him a suspicious look and handed him his paper, and he got a perfect score, and nobody questioned it.

On Thursday he rewound to catch a ball during gym, and the ball bounced off his nose, and he had a bruise on his face the size of a grape. He decided that was a fair trade.

By Friday, though, something was wrong.

He looked in the bathroom mirror and froze. His face looked older. Not much. A slight squareness around the jaw, a hint of stubble along the chin he didn't remember having. His voice, when he said "hello" to himself, had dropped half a tone, like a radio being tuned.

"Owen?" said his mum from the kitchen. "You're making that weird face again."

"I'm fine," he called back. But his hands were shaking.

* * *

The problem came at lunch.

Jada Okafor, who had sat next to him in science since September and never once spoken to him, slid her tray onto the table and sat down right beside him. "Hey, Owen," she said. "You look really good today. Have you been working out?"

"I... no?"

"You look older."

"I'm the same age."

"No, you're not," said Jada. "You're basically a teenager now. My cousin's in high school. He looks like you."

Owen stared at her. Across the cafeteria, the boys from the football team were gawking at him, one of them whistling. Mrs. Pell, passing by with a clipboard, gave him a long, appraising look and then said, "Owen, I need to see you after class. Your mother called about a change in your schedule."

"What change?"

"The school counsellor will explain."

His stomach dropped.

* * *

He checked the watch in the bathroom after lunch, hands trembling. The little screen said 2:17 p.m. and below it, in tiny letters, a number he didn't understand: _RW USES: 14_.

Fourteen.

He'd only used it six times. He was sure of that, because he had counted. He flicked the screen. The date said Tuesday, the same day he'd first rewound. The clock had reset itself, and he was standing in the same bathroom, but he was older. Much older. He could feel the years in his shoulders.

Owen's blood ran cold. He realised, all at once, that each time he rewound time, the watch did not reset the world. It reset _him_, and the world remembered only what happened after the rewind.

And he was getting older with every press.

He ran.

* * *

He found the counsellor, Mr. Carmody, in his office with the door open. Owen held up the watch with shaking hands.

"I think I broke something," he said.

Mr. Carmody took one look at his face and went very still. "How old are you, son?"

"Twelve."

"You don't look twelve."

"I know." Owen's throat was tight. "It's the watch. It's aging me. Every time I use it, I get older, and everyone's treating me differently, and I think it's changed something. Something big."

Mr. Carmody leaned forward slowly. "What did you change?"

Owen swallowed. "Last Tuesday. Jada Okafor was supposed to get the lead in the school play. But I rewound the day and talked to Mrs. Pell about her audition, so instead she gave the part to someone else. And Jada got upset and told her mum, and her mum called the school board, and now there's a whole thing about the play being cancelled."

"That's not very big."

"No. But then the theatre teacher quit, and Mrs. Pell lost her job, and Jada's cousin dropped out of high school, and now nobody's taking care of the lunch program, and my mum is working double shifts at the hospital. It all spiralled." His voice cracked. "And it's my fault, because I wanted to win a maths test."

Mr. Carmody was silent for a long moment.

"Can you undo it?" he asked.

Owen looked at the watch. The number still said fourteen. The screen flickered red, then steady.

"I think so," he whispered. "If I rewind one more time. But I might get older again. A lot older. I don't know what happens if I go past twenty."

"Then don't go past twenty," said Mr. Carmody, very gently. "Just go back to the day you broke it. And don't touch anything else."

Owen nodded. His thumb hovered over the button.

Somewhere far away, a bell rang, and he thought he heard Jada's voice calling his name.

He pressed the button.

The world went backwards, and this time, the tiles didn't reverse their pattern. They went black.

## 16. The Shooting Gallery

Coney Island, July 1937

She stopped just short of the shooting gallery, where the boardwalk planks gave way to a patch of sawdust that smelled of popcorn grease and hot metal. The carnival lights were buzzing on, one after another, as if someone were unseen and switching them up by hand. A tin Ferris wheel turned somewhere beyond the Steeplechase tower. Children shrieked. A barker called out prices.

_His locks._

The words came back, unbidden, like a stray cat that would not be shooed. Such an old-fashioned phrase, the sort her grandmother would have used about the grocer's boy. _She tossed her head, and his locks fell over his eyes._ Something in the phrase made her cheeks go warm, and she quashed the warmth at once, because it was not the sort of thing a sensible woman thought about on her way to a meeting she should not have agreed to.

She pressed her gloved hands together and looked out at the sea.

The water was the colour of pewter. A line of fishing boats lay low on the horizon. Behind her, the gallery's tin targets clattered, and someone whooped at a hit. She had told herself she would wait fifteen minutes and then leave. She had told herself this three times since she'd stepped off the trolley.

She did not know his name. She knew his eyes, which were darker than the coffee at the Brighton Café, and his curls, which fell in black ringlets over his collar, and the way he had looked at her across the crowded dance hall on Surf Avenue three nights ago, as though she were the only lamp lit in a dark room.

"You're early," said a voice behind her.

She turned.

He stood at the edge of the sawdust, hands in the pockets of a dove-grey suit that fit him too well for a boardwalk. He wore no hat. The breeze moved his hair, and the carnival light caught the fine lines around his mouth, which were not quite a smile.

"I'm not early," she said. "I'm punctual. There's a difference."

"Is there?" He stepped forward. "I've been waiting here since six."

"Then you're a poor judge of time."

"I am," he agreed. "Mostly I'm a poor judge of when to walk away."

She drew in a breath and let it out slowly. "You said you had something to tell me about Mr. Halloran."

"Did I?" His gaze slid to the shooting gallery, to the old man in a striped vest who was reloading a rifle with grim concentration. "I think I said Halloran would be unhappy if he found out you'd been in the Marlowe brothers' back room."

Her spine stiffened. "Who told you that?"

"Everyone talks, Miss..."

"Miss Lowell."

"Miss Lowell." He tasted the name. "Everyone talks, Miss Lowell, when they're frightened. And the Marlowe boys are frightened of Halloran, which makes them talkative."

The barker shouted something about a grand prize. Somewhere a child began to cry.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because you're going to be asked to do something tomorrow night that you won't like. And I'd prefer you walk in with your eyes open."

Her heart thudded. "And what is it that you'd prefer?"

He smiled then, and it changed his whole face, sharp and quiet and dangerous. "That's the question, isn't it?"

* * *

They walked along the boardwalk together without quite agreeing to it. The planks creaked. The sea hissed on the shingle below. Ahead of them, the Ferris wheel turned slowly, its painted gondolas swinging against the dusk.

"You're not from Coney," she said.

"No. I'm from Brooklyn, by way of Sicily and a boat that smelled of lemons and tar."

"Which part of Sicily?"

"The part people don't write about."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he said. "It's a warning."

She stopped under a string of lights. He stopped too, a step behind her, close enough that she could smell soap and cold air and something faintly metallic, like the coins in a fortune-teller's jar.

"Are you going to hurt me, Mr...?" She let the question hang.

"I don't know yet," he said honestly. "I came here to find out."

Her pulse jumped. She wanted to say something cutting, something worthy of her mother's sharp tongue. Instead she heard herself say, very quietly, "And what have you decided?"

He looked down at her, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. The carnival noise seemed to recede, the shrieks and clanging bells dropping away like water.

"That you should go home," he said at last. "And that I should stop coming back to this boardwalk."

"But you came back tonight."

"I did." His voice was rough now. "I told myself it was business."

"And is it?"

"No."

She lifted her chin, though her hands trembled inside her gloves. "Then what is it?"

He reached out, slowly, and tucked a stray lock of her hair back behind her ear. His fingers were cool against her cheek. Her breath caught, and she hated herself for the sound.

"Dangerous," he said softly. "It's dangerous."

* * *

She walked away before he could say more, her heels striking the planks in quick, furious clicks. She did not look back until she reached the trolley stop, where the lamps glowed amber in the dusk.

He was still standing under the string of lights, his grey suit a dark shape against the sea, his hand raised in a small, sad gesture of farewell.

She climbed aboard without a word. As the trolley pulled away, she pressed her forehead against the cool glass and felt the old, familiar warning rise up in her chest.

_He is trouble,_ said the sensible voice. _Stay away._

But beneath it, faint and stubborn, another voice whispered, _Tomorrow night. He'll be there, too._

She closed her eyes, and in the dark behind her lids she could still see his eyes, and his curls, and the dangerous, unfinished smile.

## 17. Caribou Season at the Lodge

The lodge looked the same as it always had. Cedar shingles gone silver with age, the porch sagging a little on its left corner the way it had since JT was seven, the screen door with its bent hinge and the wasp nest tucked above the lintel. He stood at the bottom of the steps with his duffel over one shoulder and tried to figure out what was wrong with it.

Nothing, he decided. The air was just thicker. The spruce were too close to the house. He was tired from the drive, cramped in the back of the pickup beside Mike for six hours, his knees jammed against a stack of rifles wrapped in oily canvas. Mike had slept most of the way with his mouth open. Dad had driven with both hands on the wheel and said maybe four words.

"Come on, JT," Dad called from the porch. "Fire's going. Get your boots off the steps."

JT climbed up. Inside, the lodge smelled of woodsmoke, coffee, and the sharp tang of gun oil. The stone fireplace crackled. The moose head over the mantel watched him with its glass eyes.

It had watched him every autumn of his life. He'd never once minded.

* * *

The first caribou came on the second morning.

They'd been walking the ridge since before dawn, breath steaming, boots crunching frost on the lichen. Dad went first, then Mike, then JT, who had the stiff new laces in his boots and kept having to stop to fix them. The sky was white. The tundra stretched out in every direction, grey-green and empty.

The animal stood at the edge of a shallow valley, maybe sixty yards off. It didn't run when they stopped. It didn't even turn its head. It just stood there in the thin light, ears flicking, its dark eyes fixed on something far away.

"Good shot, Dad," Mike whispered.

"Wait," said Dad.

The caribou's ears swivelled, slowly, toward JT. Not the others. Him.

JT felt a crawling sensation across his scalp, like a spider walking the length of his skull. He knew the thing was waiting. He didn't know how he knew, but he was sure of it: the caribou was waiting for the shot the way a man waits for a train he's already missed.

Dad fired. The animal dropped without a sound, folding onto the frost with a dignified slowness, as if it had simply decided to lie down.

Nobody spoke. Mike was already walking forward, grinning. Dad slapped his shoulder. JT stood where he was, looking at the dead animal's eyes, which were still open, still fixed on him.

"Come on, JT," Dad called. "Help us gut it."

JT went. But his hands shook all the way through, and he didn't say a word about the ears.

* * *

The second time was in the clearing.

He'd fallen behind again, fumbling with his laces, and by the time he looked up, Dad and Mike had stopped at the edge of a stand of birch. JT slowed without thinking, ducking behind a tangle of dead brush, heart thudding.

In the middle of the clearing stood a buck.

It was the biggest animal he'd ever seen. Antlers like the branches of a dead tree, rack spreading wide over the snow. Its chest rose and fell in long, slow breaths that turned to mist in the cold. It stood very still, close enough to the men that they could have reached out and touched it.

And they were reaching.

Dad's right hand lifted, trembling, toward the animal's muzzle. His rifle hung forgotten at his side, its barrel dipping toward the snow. His face wore an expression JT had never seen on it before: eyes too wide and too bright, lips parted, as if he were about to whisper a secret into the buck's ear.

Mike had the same look. Both of them were smiling, faintly, dreamily.

JT's throat closed. His mind screamed at him to look away, turn around, run. But he couldn't move. His legs felt rooted to the frozen ground, and the brush scratched his cheek, and somewhere deep in his chest a low voice whispered, _Stay quiet. Just watch._

The buck turned its head. Its eyes met JT's through the brush.

They were dark and flat and very calm, and JT had the sudden, horrible certainty that the animal knew exactly where he was hiding.

Then, with a shiver that ran the length of its body, the buck was gone. It slipped into the birch with no sound at all, leaving only a faint scatter of snow.

Dad blinked. Mike blinked. Both of them looked around, confused, as if they'd just woken up in the wrong place.

"Did you see that?" Mike said.

"See what?" Dad asked.

JT crouched lower in the brush, shallow breaths fogging the air. He did not say anything. His father had failed to take the shot. He'd had an open chance, a perfect one, and he had simply let it go. It felt like a betrayal. It felt like something worse than a betrayal, something JT couldn't name.

He stayed hidden until they moved on. Then he stepped out into the clearing and stared at the trampled snow, at the faint dent where the buck had stood.

The shape of the hoofprints was wrong. They were too deep, too close together. As if something very heavy had been standing there on two legs.

* * *

That night, the lodge was quiet except for the crackle of the fire. Dad sat in his chair with a glass of whisky he wasn't drinking. Mike was asleep on the couch.

"Dad," JT said from the doorway.

Dad didn't look up. "Go to bed, son."

"You didn't take the shot."

Silence. The fire popped.

"I know," Dad said at last, very softly. "I don't know why."

JT wanted to ask more. He wanted to ask what Dad had been doing with his hand, and why his eyes had looked like that, and whether the caribou had been waiting for them all along. But the words stuck in his throat.

"Goodnight, Dad."

"Goodnight, JT."

He climbed the stairs, heavy-footed. At the top, he looked back down into the living room. Dad was still sitting in his chair. The moose head over the mantel was watching him. And in the glass of its eyes, JT saw, faint and reflected, a second figure standing behind the chair.

He blinked. The figure was gone.

He went to bed and lay awake for a long time, listening to the wind move through the spruce, and to something out on the tundra, breathing slow and patient, waiting for morning.

## 18. Domestic Arrangements in Hell

Lucifer woke at half past nine, which was early for him, and found the bed empty except for a single black feather and a note on the nightstand written in what looked suspiciously like his own handwriting.

_Gone to see about the Second Circle. Back by lunch. Don't touch the thermostat._

He stared at the note for a while. Then he looked at the thermostat, which was set to a temperature that would have peeled the skin off a cathedral gargoyle, and sighed.

"She's been at it again," he said to the ceiling.

The ceiling said nothing. Hell did not, as a rule, have any interest in domestic matters, but it did have a considerable sense of humour, and the walls were already starting to drip sulphur in a way that suggested they found the whole situation hilarious.

* * *

Lucifer's office occupied the top of a black basalt tower that rose out of the centre of the Pit like a splinter in a giant's thumb. It had a view of the River Styx, a desk made from the teeth of a fallen angel, and a coffee machine that dispensed a very particular brew of brimstone and regret. He liked it. It had character.

His assistant was already waiting, clutching a clipboard and wearing a grey suit that did not quite conceal the faint smoke rising from his collar.

"Mr. Morningstar. Your ten o'clock is the Board of Infernal Affairs. They want to discuss the new tax on sin."

"What's the tax?"

"Eleven percent on pride, nine on greed, and twelve on minor acts of spite."

"Twelve on spite?" Lucifer took his coffee. "Who thought spite was minor?"

"Accountants, sir."

"Of course they did." He sipped. The coffee was perfect, bitter and warm and faintly sad. "Tell them I'll think about it. Tell them I'm thinking very hard. Then cancel the meeting."

"Sir, the Board has been waiting three centuries for this meeting."

"Good. Let them wait another three. It builds character."

* * *

By noon, he'd been through a dispute over a mislaid soul in the Fourth Circle, a complaint about a demon who had taken too long to torture a banker, and a request from the Ministry of Despair for a larger budget for their new sadness wing. He signed everything without reading it, which was how he had once accidentally signed away the western half of Tartarus to a pack of very persistent centaurs.

At twelve-fifteen, there was a knock at the door.

Lilith stood in the doorway, one hand on the frame, dressed in a deep red coat that flared at the hem like a flame. Her hair was loose and long, and she was holding a paper bag in one hand and a bottle of something green in the other.

"You're not supposed to be here until lunch," Lucifer said.

"I'm early. You're always complaining I'm late." She stepped inside without waiting for an invitation. "I brought dumplings. From the Fifth Circle. The little place with the gluttons who make them from dead cats. You'll love them."

"I hate dead cat dumplings."

"You say that every time, and then you eat six."

He sighed and took the bag. The dumplings were still warm. They were, he admitted privately, excellent.

Lilith perched on the edge of his desk, swinging one leg. "You look tired."

"I'm not tired. I'm managing a civilisation."

"Yes, dear. Managing a civilisation that's in the middle of a mutiny because someone put crushed glass in the soup." She leaned over and picked a dumpling out of the bag. "Heard about that?"

"I'm handling it."

"You're hiding in your office, eating dead cat dumplings, and pretending the soup is your fault."

"It _is_ my fault. Everything is my fault. That's the job."

She laughed, low and warm, and the sound filled the room like smoke. For a moment he forgot about the Board, the taxes, the thermostat, and the sulphur staining the windows. He just looked at her, and thought, with a sort of exhausted surprise, that he had missed her more than he had admitted in a century.

"Why are you really here?" he asked.

She shrugged, not quite meeting his eyes. "I had a day off. Thought I'd see how the old place was doing."

"You hate the old place."

"I hate most of it. I don't hate you." She picked another dumpling, examined it, put it down. "Lucifer, you've been lonely."

He stiffened. "I have a court full of demons."

"Demons who want your job. Or your tax exemptions. Or your favourite pair of boots." She tilted her head, studying him. "Nobody in this building has ever asked how you're sleeping."

"I sleep fine."

"You sleep on the floor of the bedroom with a copy of _Paradise Lost_ over your face."

He opened his mouth to deny it, then closed it again. She was smiling, but it wasn't cruel.

"Well," he said at last. "It's a long read."

* * *

They spent the afternoon arguing, in the way that only two people who had known each other since the Creation could argue. About the Board. About the tax. About whether Lilith should be allowed to torture the Minister of Despair again, given how well the last time had gone. About the thermostat, which Lilith insisted was an act of love and Lucifer insisted was a crime against the mortal realm.

By four o'clock, Lucifer had discovered that Lilith's new favourite hobby was rearranging the pain in the Fourth Circle to spell out insults to the Archangel Michael. By five, he had agreed to help her hang the banner.

By six, they were sitting on the balcony of his tower, feet dangling over the edge, watching the lava roll slowly across the plains below. The sky was the colour of old blood. Somewhere a thousand souls were screaming in a rhythm that was almost musical.

"Do you ever wonder what it would've been like?" Lilith asked quietly. "If we'd stayed up there?"

"Every day," said Lucifer.

"Me too."

"Did you come here tonight to say that?"

She didn't answer right away. Then she reached over and laced her fingers through his. Her hand was warm, and her nails were painted a deep black that matched the coat.

"I came here," she said, "because I didn't want you to go another hundred years sleeping on the floor."

He looked down at their joined hands, then out at the burning plain. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me in Hell."

"Don't get used to it."

"I'll try not to."

They sat together until the lava turned gold in the last of the hellish dusk, and somewhere below, the Board of Infernal Affairs sent a fourth memo about the tax on spite, which Lucifer did not read, and Lilith quietly folded into a paper aeroplane and launched off the balcony into the flames.

## 19. The Second Hour

The morning bell at the Ludus Magnus rang before the sun had cleared the walls, and Marcus Vitellius Rufus lay on his cot in the dark and counted the beats between each bronze note. Eleven. Twelve. A man learned to count everything in the ludus. The meals. The lashes. The days until the next fight, which were never enough and always too many.

He got up before the thirteenth.

The cell smelled of old straw, sweat, and the sour tang of the lamp oil that the guards refused to refill unless someone paid them. Across the narrow corridor, a Thracian was snoring like a broken bellows, one arm flung over his face. Beyond the wall, the city was already awake. He could hear it through the grating: carts on the paving stones, the whistle of a water-seller, a child screaming at a dog.

Rome. The place where everything came to be sold.

He washed his face in a bucket of cold water drawn by a slave boy who would not look at him. The water tasted of iron. Marcus scrubbed the grit from his eyes and felt the familiar ache in his shoulder, the one that had never quite healed after his fight in Capua three summers ago. A tear in the muscle, the surgeon had said. A tear that would keep him honest.

"Gladiator," said the guard at the gate, as if it were a curse. "Lanista wants you in the yard."

* * *

The yard was a square of hard-packed earth ringed by stone columns, sunlight slanting across it in long bars. Dust hung in the air. The smell of horse dung and roasting bread rose from the streets beyond. Somewhere a flute was playing, slow and haunting, a tune from a funeral.

Lanista Gaius Scaurus sat on a stool at the edge of the yard, a cup of watered wine in his hand and a ledger open on his knee. He was a heavy man with a clean white toga and rings on every finger, and he never looked at the fighters unless they were worth something. Marcus was worth something. Not much. Enough to keep him alive, and not enough to keep him free.

"Vitellius," Scaurus said, without looking up. "The Senator Aurelian is coming to see the practice this morning. He wants to meet the men who'll fight at the Games of Vespasian. You'll stand where he can see you."

"Yes, dominus."

"And you'll smile."

Marcus said nothing. Scaurus looked up then, his eyes flat and calculating.

"You know what the crowd wants, Vitellius? They want a man who's grateful to be looked at. They want the Senator to think you're a fine piece of work, worth every sesterce. Smile, and maybe he'll buy the next bout's tickets. Maybe he'll throw a bone to the family."

"What family?"

"Yours, if you have one." Scaurus turned a page. "Off with you."

* * *

The Senator arrived in a litter carried by six Nubians, their backs gleaming with oil. Aurelian was a thin man with a face like a closed book and a voice that cut through the yard like a blade. He wore a purple-edged toga and smelled of jasmine and political ambition.

"Lanista," he said, stepping down. "Are these the men?"

"The finest in Rome, Senator."

"The finest in Rome are dead," Aurelian replied drily. "We're left with the ones who haven't learned to die yet."

He walked along the line of fighters, pausing to look each one in the face. He lingered on the Thracian, who flexed his jaw and said nothing. He stopped in front of Marcus.

"You're the Campanian."

"Yes, Senator."

"The one who fought Titus Hirtius at Capua. Three summers ago."

"Yes."

"You let him go."

Marcus felt the crowd of guards and slaves tense around them, a quiet ripple. He kept his eyes level and his breathing slow.

"He had a broken arm, Senator. The crowd was calling for him to die. I did not think a man with a broken arm was worth the death."

Aurelian's mouth curved, just slightly. "A soldier's answer. Or a poet's. I'm not sure which." He leaned closer, his voice lowering. "Do you know what the people of Rome call men like you?"

"No, Senator."

"Heroes, when they're winning. Traitors, when they lose." Aurelian straightened. "And the Emperor needs a great many of both, these days. The grain riots have not ended. The Praetorians are restless. Every man in this yard is a debt that someone else will have to pay."

He turned away, and his voice rose again, smooth and public. "Smile, Vitellius. I'll remember your face."

Marcus smiled. It felt like pulling a thorn from his own skin.

* * *

Afterwards, the yard emptied. The Thracian clapped him on the shoulder, grinning through a missing tooth. "You made him think, Campanian. That's worth more than a fight."

"It's worth nothing if he forgets me."

"Politicians always forget." The Thracian spat into the dust. "The only thing they remember is the price."

Marcus walked to the edge of the yard, where a narrow stone well stood under a fig tree. He drew up a bucket and drank deeply. The water was cold and sweet, and for a moment he closed his eyes and let himself think about nothing at all.

Then he heard the flute again, faint and distant, and opened his eyes.

Somewhere beyond the walls, a crowd was already gathering for the morning's executions. He could hear them: the roar, the jeers, the sound of a city that had long since learned to enjoy blood more than bread. Rome did not need gladiators to be cruel. It only needed their silence.

He set the bucket down, wiped his mouth on the back of his hand, and looked up at the sky, which was turning, slowly, the colour of brass.

_Tonight,_ he thought. _Tonight, the lanista will count the coins. And someone will decide what I am worth._

He went back inside before the bell rang the second hour, and he did not look back.

## 20. The Light at Sgeir Mhòr

_Sgeir Mhòr Light, Isle of Rona_  
_14th October 1931_

Dear Ma,

Sorry it has been so long since writing. The relief boat was late in September because of the wind and we had nothing but the bread from August to live on for nine days. I'm telling you this so you don't worry about the wee ones. We are fine. The rain is constant, the light is bright, and I have scrubbed the tower floor so often I could eat my dinner off it.

The keeper before me left a clock on the wall with no hands. I have been wondering if it was meant as a joke.

Your son,  
Alasdair

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_3rd November 1931_

Dear Alasdair,

Your letter came with the Tuesday post and Mrs. Macrae read it to me twice. She says you write like your grandfather, all sorrow and no complaint. I said he complained plenty, only nobody ever wrote it down.

Eat what they send you. Do not put the bread in the drawer. I know you think it keeps but it grows things.

Your loving mother,  
Catriona

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_19th November 1931_

Dear Ma,

The bread did not grow anything, but I have found a mouse in the lamp room who has taken a liking to the paraffin. I have named him Mr. Brodie after the minister in Stornoway, because he has the same way of looking at you as if you owe him money.

The sea has been very rough. On the third night a wave went over the top of the lantern gallery and the glass cracked at the north pane. I stood in the gallery in my nightshirt with a torch and a cup of cold tea and watched the water come up the rock like it wanted to know my name.

I do not sleep much here. The nights are long and the sound of the foghorn comes through the walls.

Your son,  
Alasdair

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_26th November 1931_

Dear Alasdair,

You must write to your sister more. She is not sleeping either. She asks me every Sunday if you have eaten and I say you have, which is a lie, and she knows it, and so we sit with the lie between us like a cat on the good chair.

Mrs. Macrae sends her love and says the mouse is to be given a sprig of mint. I do not know why she says this but she has been right about mice before.

Come home at Christmas if they will let you. If they will not, write me a letter that is longer than this one.

Your mother,  
Catriona

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_2nd December 1931_

Dear Ma,

I have been thinking about what you said about the lie. You are right, I did not eat much in August, and I have not eaten much since. The rations come in tins and the tins are tiny, and the cook on the relief boat thinks a man can live on oatcakes and goodwill.

I have started keeping a log in the back of the book where the keepers write the weather. I am putting down the things that I see that are not weather. A gull that stood on the rail for an hour and did not move. A seal that followed the boat for a mile and then went under and did not come up. A light in the north that was not the lighthouse, and then was gone.

I do not know if this is madness. It is only that the rock is very small and the sea is very big, and sometimes I forget which of us is being watched.

Alasdair

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_14th December 1931_

Dear Alasdair,

I have read your letter three times and then put it in the drawer where I keep the things I do not want to be frightened by. I am not frightened by the seal, or the gull. I am frightened that you think the sea is watching you.

The keepers on the old rock at Fladda used to say the same thing. Your grandfather knew one of them. He said the men who stayed too long began to hear the light humming, even when it was off, and that they went quiet and then they went out in the boat one night and were not seen again.

You will come home at New Year. I have written to the Board myself. Mrs. Macrae has promised to walk with me to the pier.

Your mother,  
Catriona

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_22nd December 1931_

Dear Ma,

The Board has answered and says the relief boat cannot come before February. They say the weather is too bad and the men at Stornoway are short-handed. I have written back to say that I am perfectly well and that the lamp is burning and that the mouse has eaten a whole biscuit tin.

I went up to the gallery last night to check the lamp. The glass is still cracked. The light is turning as it should. But there is a sound up there now, very quiet, under the foghorn. Like a man humming a tune he does not know the words to. I stood with my hand on the rail and I could not tell if it was coming from the lantern or from under the water.

I am not writing this to frighten you. I am writing it because I do not want to be the only one who heard it.

Alasdair

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_24th December 1931_

Dear Alasdair,

I am sending this on the boat that brings the Christmas post, which the Board says is the last until the spring. Listen to me now. Go down to the store and bring up the paraffin and the tinned peaches and the good tea. Light the stove in the kitchen. Sit with the mouse if you must, but sit by the fire and not in the gallery.

If the humming gets louder, you will turn the lamp off for one night. The Board will not like it. Let them write to me about it.

Your sister has made you a jumper. It is too big, but she says you will grow into it. I have not told her about the humming. I have told her you are eating well.

You are not the only one who has heard something, Alasdair. Your grandfather heard it too, on a winter night at Fladda, and he never once told me what it sounded like.

Your mother,  
Catriona

* * *

_Sgeir Mhòr Light_  
_26th December 1931_

Dear Ma,

I did as you said. The stove is lit. The tea is good. The peaches made me weep like a bairn, which the mouse did not appreciate.

I turned the lamp off last night. I sat in the kitchen with the lamp out and the door locked and the foghorn silent, because I could not bear it any longer. The sea was very still. I counted the waves against the rock until I lost count somewhere past four hundred.

At three o'clock, the humming stopped.

It did not fade. It simply stopped, the way a conversation stops when someone in the room has finally said the right thing. And then, out in the dark over the water, I heard my name spoken once, very softly, in your voice.

I did not go to the window.

I am writing this with the lamp lit again and the jumper on, and I am going to light the stove and wait for the boat in February. I am going to eat the bread, even if it grows.

Tell Mrs. Macrae the mouse is spoken for.

Your son,  
Alasdair

## 21. Attack Surface

The waiter sets down my glass of Pinot like he's defusing it, and I notice the wobble in the table leg before I notice anything else about him. Left front. Someone shimmed it with a folded matchbook, and the matchbook is damp. Cheap bolts in the chair backs too, the kind that loosen in a month. Not my problem. Not tonight.

Tonight is a first date. Her name is Priya, and she has been looking at me across the candle like she's trying to decide if I'm a serial killer or a good listener, and I've been working very hard on being the second one.

_Be here,_ I tell myself. _Be present. You are a person having dinner with a person._

"So," Priya says, twirling her fork. "You said you're in cybersecurity. What does that actually mean? Are you the guy who hacks banks, or the guy who makes passwords better?"

I laugh. "Mostly the second one. I get paid to break things so people can find out what's wrong before someone else does."

"That sounds like a stressful job."

"It's a job where I'm always looking at doors," I say, and then, because I can't help it: "Like that door over there, the kitchen one. It's propped open with a cheap wedge. Anyone with a tray could push in from the back."

Oh no.

Priya glances toward the kitchen. Nobody's looking at us. A waiter pushes through with a tray of bread rolls, and the door swings shut behind him. I watch the wedge stay where it is, and I feel the old reflex unwind in my chest: _cheap wedge, loose hinge, no cameras on that corner._

_Stop it. She's talking._

* * *

She's talking about Magic: The Gathering. I didn't bring it up. She did, because she saw my keychain, a little enamel card of a green dragon. She holds up her phone, which has a lock screen of a Vraska planeswalker, and says, "Wait. Are you a player?"

"Since I was thirteen. Mostly the old Ice Age sets."

She gasps and grabs my wrist, delighted. "Ice Age! Do you still have a Shivan Dragon?"

"I have three. One's sleeved. One's been lovingly bent by a toddler. One's in a binder with the spine taped."

She laughs so hard the couple at the next table turns around. My chest does something warm and stupid. For twenty minutes we talk about mana curves and the best blue counterspells and the time she got a Black Lotus in a pack and then accidentally sold it for twelve dollars to a man who said it was his birthday. I'm laughing too. I'm relaxed. I forget, for almost a whole minute, the cheap bolts in the chair.

Then she reaches for her wine and her phone buzzes against the tabletop, and the screen lights up. She pushes it away with one finger.

I see the lock screen. Hard to miss. Six dots, a hand-drawn pattern, the kind a person draws without thinking, in a lazy L-shape from the top left.

_Oh, no. No, no._

I don't mean to look at it. My eyes just go there, the way they go to every door and every hinge and every weak seam. Top left, down, right, down, right, right. Six points, two diagonal shortcuts, and a smudge on the glass at the bottom corner that is almost certainly where her thumb rests when she unlocks it on the train. The smudge alone is a gift. Nobody who has a pattern like that deserves a phone that keeps their photos safe.

_Do not say it. Do not say it._

"Your phone's got a smudge right there," I hear myself say. "Bottom corner. Pattern's pretty obvious from that, you know. People do the L-shape a lot."

The silence after that is a physical thing. Priya's fork stops halfway to her mouth. Her face goes very still, very polite.

"Sorry?"

_Say something. Anything. Fix it._

"I mean, it's nothing," I say quickly. "Not a big deal. It's just a habit of mine, noticing things. It's a terrible habit."

"You looked at my phone."

"I looked at the glass. It was right there."

She puts the fork down. Carefully. "Right. Okay."

* * *

Inside my head, the review board convenes. I can see them: three grey-suited versions of me, sitting at a long table, taking notes on my performance. _Subject identified a critical vulnerability in the target's authentication. Subject then disclosed it to the target without consent. Subject's recovery strategy: Poor._

_Recovery options,_ I think, in my most professional voice. _Option one: apologise sincerely and move on. Option two: explain that the pattern is a security reflex, a professional deformity, and invite her to laugh about it. Option three: stand up, leave a fifty on the table, walk out through the kitchen door and fix the wedge on the way._

_Option three is tempting,_ says the voice that always sounds like my old instructor.

Priya picks up her wine. She sips. She looks at me over the rim with an expression I can't read.

"So," she says. "Do you always do that? Look at people's security?"

"Only on dates."

"That's a very specific admission."

"I'm trying to be honest."

"Mm." She sets the glass down. "Do you want to know what my pattern actually is?"

My stomach drops. _No. Yes. Don't ask._

"I don't think I should."

"Good answer." She pulls the phone toward her and, very deliberately, holds it face down. "Okay. Here's the deal. You can keep noticing things, but you don't get to tell me what I'm doing wrong with my phone, and you don't get to look at my screen while I'm talking. Deal?"

It's fair. It's more than fair. I want to say yes so badly my teeth hurt.

"Deal."

"Good." She picks up her fork again. "Now tell me about the Ice Age Shivan Dragon. I want to know if the one with the taped spine is still playable."

I laugh, a little shakily, and start to talk. Somewhere under the table my right hand is tapping a rhythm on my thigh, six beats, a pattern I didn't mean to make.

I stop before the sixth.

It's a start.

## 22. Lab Partners

Mr. Dunmore has paired us for the volcano project, which means I have exactly eleven minutes of my life left before I combust. I know this because I have calculated it. My heart rate is 104. Normal for a resting human is about 70. I have been resting for six minutes and it is not going well.

Ellie is sitting at the table next to mine, which is fine. Ellie is nice. Ellie will talk to anyone.

Henry Park is sitting across from me, and Henry Park is not nice, or not mean either, just... Henry. He's the kind of person who says "wait, what" when you say something normal, and then looks at you like you've just invented a new colour. He also has a laugh that sounds like a squeaky door and I think about it more than I'd like to admit.

_Okay. Okay. You are a scientist. Act like a scientist._

I pull out my notebook and open it to the diagram of the magma chamber. I drew it last night, three layers, arrows showing pressure build-up, a little cartoon of a dinosaur in the corner that I definitely won't mention. It's good work. Mr. Dunmore said so. The problem is that I don't have a single sentence in my head that I can say out loud.

Henry looks up. "Hey. You're Vesta, right?"

_Oh my God. Oh my God, he knows my name._

"Yes," I say. Too loud. A girl at the next table glances over.

"Cool." He looks at the notebook. "Is that a dinosaur?"

_Tell him it's a metaphor. Tell him it's a comment on the bureaucratic nature of geological time. Say it in a dry voice. Make him laugh._

"It's a... yeah," I say. "He's a... geologist."

Henry blinks. Then he snorts, the squeaky door sound, and says, "Okay, I'm going to need that dinosaur's name."

_Name? What name? Why do you have no name for the dinosaur?_

"Terry," I say, because it's the first thing that comes out.

"Terry the geologist," Henry says gravely. "That's a good name."

I laugh. It comes out as a sort of hiccup. I'm not sure whether this is good or bad. I'm going to replay it at three in the morning.

* * *

The volcano is made of clay. It is supposed to erupt when we add baking soda and vinegar, and Mr. Dunmore has warned us that the last group set the classroom ceiling on fire, which is probably a joke, but you can never tell with Mr. Dunmore.

Henry is building the cone. Ellie is mixing the red food colouring. I have been assigned to "research the chemistry," which means I get to say things like _exothermic_ and _gas evolution_ in a very confident voice while I stare at the vinegar bottle like it owes me money.

"So it's like, the acid reacts with the base," I say. "And it makes carbon dioxide. That's the bubbles. And the pressure builds up, and then it erupts."

"Right," says Henry. "Like a shaken soda."

"Sort of. But more controlled. Or it's supposed to be."

"So what happens if we add too much?"

_Don't say you'd explode. That sounds weird. Don't say anything about explosions, Vesta, you absolute disaster._

"You get a bigger eruption," I say. "And maybe the clay cracks."

"Cool," says Henry, and then pauses, and looks at me sideways. "You know a lot about this."

My face turns hot. "I read a book."

"Which book?"

_Not the one about the Mount St. Helens disaster. Not the one about the eruptions in Iceland. Pick a boring one. Pick literally anything else._

"A... book about volcanoes," I say.

"Okay, that's a very generic book."

I can feel my ears going pink. I hate that they do this. I hate that everything I say comes out sounding like a Wikipedia article.

Ellie laughs from across the table, not meanly, and says, "Vesta, you're doing your face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you're thinking really hard about how you look while you talk."

_Oh no. She can see it. Everyone can see it. Henry has seen it. Mr. Dunmore has probably seen it from the front of the room and he's going to write it in a report._

I make myself relax my jaw. I make myself breathe. I say, carefully, "Sorry. I'm just nervous about the eruption."

"It's just baking soda," says Henry. "Relax."

"I am relaxed."

"Your hands are shaking."

I look down. They are. I put them flat on the table, very deliberately. "It's cold in here."

It's sixty-eight degrees in here. Henry looks at the thermostat and then back at me, and his mouth twitches.

"Okay," he says gently. "Sure."

* * *

Here is what I have been thinking, in the twelve minutes of Earth science class that I have not been able to pay attention to:

I can't stop imagining the version of me that would say the perfect thing. She would have a sly smile and a dry remark about gas pressure. She would make Henry laugh so hard he has to hold the table. She would say, _I think you're funny, and I'd like to be friends_, in a voice that didn't tremble at all.

That girl is in my head. She is brilliant and easy and unafraid. She is also completely fictional, and every time I reach for her, I end up saying something about dinosaurs.

Henry is pouring baking soda into the cone now, careful, a small white heap building in the crater. He leans back and looks at me.

"You want to do the vinegar?"

_Yes. No. What if I spill it. What if I'm bad at it. What if he thinks I'm trying too hard._

I get up, very slowly, and walk to the bench with my notebook under my arm, still open to the drawing of Terry the geologist. The cone is wobbling slightly. The bottle of vinegar is cold in my hand.

"Okay," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I expected. "Let's see what happens."

I pour. The fizz starts at once, a sharp, sweet hiss, and red foam begins to climb the slope of the clay.

Henry grins at me across the bench, a real grin, the squeaky-door kind. "Terry would be proud."

I laugh, and this time it doesn't hiccup at all.

## 23. Rehearsing Rejection

Okay. She's going to be late. Twelve minutes, probably. She said seven and then texted at six-forty that she was "just finishing something," no kiss, no emoji, and that's fine, it's fine, people finish things. But when I read _just finishing something_ I heard _I'm finishing with you_, and now I'm standing in the bathroom holding my hairbrush like it might tell me what to do.

I should cancel. I could say I'm sick. I could say I'm not feeling well, which is true, which is somehow worse.

She'll sit down across from me and say, "You look tired." And I'll say I'm fine, and she'll say, "You're doing the thing," and I'll say what thing, and she'll say, "The thing where you go somewhere," and then she'll look at her phone, and that's it. That's the whole conversation. Four weeks of talking every night, and she's down to one sentence before she gets bored of me.

No. Don't. Maren isn't bored. Maren texted me a link to a bookshop yesterday with _this made me think of you_. That's not what a bored person sends.

Okay, but that was yesterday. She hasn't called since Sunday. I counted. I counted like an idiot. Four days. Anyone with a normal heart would call it a busy week.

What I'll say when she starts to pull away: _I know you're busy, I just miss you when you're gone._ No. That sounds needy. Better: _I'm not asking for anything, I just noticed._ That's clean. That sounds like I'm not the one falling apart. And if she says _You're being intense_, I'll say _Maybe. But I'd rather be intense than fake, and you of all people should know the difference._ And then I'll leave before she can answer, and she'll feel bad, and she'll call me, and I'll pick up on the second ring and act surprised.

Except I won't act surprised, because I'll have already spent the whole night on the floor of my bedroom talking to her in my head.

Okay. Stop. Breathe. I know what this is. I've known for years. The whole thing has a name and a pamphlet and a lady with a clipboard who explained it to me while I cried in a chair with the cheap orange cushion. Imagined rejection rehearsed until it feels like memory. I know that. I can even say it out loud. Knowing is a thing I do so I can keep doing it, which is funny when you think about it, which I can't.

I'm getting ready. Shirt number three, the blue one she said she liked. Pull it straight. Lint on the shoulder, get it off. There. Now you look like someone who isn't going to fall apart in a restaurant.

Ten past seven. The doorbell goes, and it's her, and her face does a small thing when she sees me, a sort of softening, and my heart does the stupid lurch it always does, and I hate how relieved I am.

"Hey," she says. "Sorry. The thing ran over."

"It's fine." Don't say it's fine like that. Too flat. "It's fine. Come in."

She's holding a book. Small, green cover, soft at the corners. "I finished it," she says, "and I kept thinking about that bit you said, about the lighthouse, so I thought you'd want it."

I take it. I don't open it. I think: she bought this for me, or she borrowed it, or she's giving me her copy, which means she's done with it, which means she's done with me, or she just wanted to give me something, which is a nice thing to do, which is what people do when they like you.

"Thank you," I say. "I'll read it tonight."

She smiles and touches my wrist, two fingers, quick, and says, "I missed you," and I feel that in my chest like a warm hand. And then, almost immediately, the other voice comes in, cool and certain: _She said that because she feels guilty about the four days. People say that when they've been avoiding you._

I hear myself laugh, too loud. "I missed you too."

We sit down. The waiter comes. She orders the thing she always orders, I order the thing I always order, and she tells me about her sister's wedding, and she's funny about the seating plan, and she makes me laugh properly, the kind where I snort. Her knee bumps mine under the table and she doesn't move it away.

_She's being kind because she's about to end it gently._

I know that's not true. There's no evidence. Every single thing she's doing is the opposite of what the voice is saying. But the voice is so much louder in the quiet bits, and I can feel my face trying to hold a shape, the nice shape, the easy one, and it's getting tired.

"You're doing the thing," she says, softly.

My stomach drops. "What thing?"

"The face. Like you're waiting for me to say something bad."

I have a rehearsed answer. _I'm not._ I have a second one. _Maybe I am. Is that a problem?_ I have a third that's basically just me walking out.

Instead I say, "Yeah. Sorry. I'm working on it."

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she reaches over and takes my hand, not the wrist this time, the whole hand, and says, "Okay. Me too."

And the voice goes quiet. Just for a second. Long enough for me to feel something real and unguarded, something close to peace, and I want to stay in it, I want to stay in it so badly my eyes sting.

Then it comes back, reasonable as a doctor: _Enjoy this while it lasts._

I smile at her. I hold her hand a bit tighter. And somewhere underneath, in the quiet room where I rehearse everything, I'm already drafting the speech I'll give when she leaves.

## 24. Blood Debt

The temple of Ashiro had burned once, and the fire had never been forgiven. Its roof was a skeleton of black beams, and moonlight fell through in pale bars across the flagstones of the main hall. The Buddha on the north wall had lost its head and most of its hands. Someone had hung a paper lantern from the stump of one arm. Below it, the floor had been swept clear into a rough square about eight paces across. Sixty or seventy men crowded the broken steps on the east side, passing sake bottles hand to hand while money changed hands in damp fists.

Tetsuya stood at the northwest corner of the square, his back nearly touching the Buddha's plinth. His gi was dark cotton, cinched hard at the waist, sleeves shoved past the elbows. His feet sat in a deep forward stance, left knee bent over the toes, right leg long and braced behind. He had not moved in a full minute. Jigen-ryū taught stillness before attack the way a drawn bow teaches patience.

Lin Wei came in from the southeast, between a fallen column and the crowd, walking the way a man walks through a market he has worked in all his life. Loose trousers, a sleeveless jacket of faded blue, forearms wrapped in linen to the wrist. His weight rode low on the balls of his feet. When he reached the center he stopped four paces from Tetsuya, turned his left side toward him, and let his arms hang loose, swaying slightly like rope in the wind.

"You came," Tetsuya said.

"You called me a dog's dinner in front of the fish market. I came."

Someone laughed on the steps. Tetsuya's jaw tightened, and he stepped.

It was less a step than a fall, the whole body released at once, left foot skating over the stone while the right drove behind it. Jigen-ryū's first lesson was that momentum belongs to the one who is moving. Tetsuya covered three paces in two heartbeats, right hand rising into a vertical chop meant to split Lin Wei's collarbone from crown to shoulder.

Lin Wei did not retreat. He turned.

His right foot pivoted on the ball, the left swung out and behind, and his body rotated like a door on a hinge. The falling blade passed a finger's width from his ear. His left arm came up whipping, forearm loose, and the back of his wrist cracked across the side of Tetsuya's head in a wide circular arc. It was not a punch. It was a snap, the hand looping out and returning, and the sound carried all the way to the steps.

Tetsuya's head rocked, but he kept his momentum, which was his mistake. His own strike had carried him past Lin Wei's shoulder, so he dropped low and drove a hip-first elbow toward the ribs. Lin Wei was already gone, one step back and to the right, his weight shifting off the front foot before the elbow arrived. The elbow found only air. Tetsuya's shoulder dropped into empty space, and he had to plant his right heel hard to keep from pitching onto his face.

The crowd roared.

Lin Wei now stood near the cracked base of the second pillar, at the northeast edge of the square. Tetsuya had turned to face him, weight back on the rear leg. Both were breathing hard. Heat rose in Tetsuya's cheeks.

"Again," he said.

He came in lower this time, starting with a fast cut high to the face. It made Lin Wei lift his guard, and then came the real blow, a low forward kick aimed at the outside of Lin Wei's knee. Lin Wei dropped his guard into the kick, let the sole slide across his shin, and stepped inside the arc. He was so close that Tetsuya's kicking leg jammed against his thigh. Lin Wei's right arm swung down in a tight circle, the forearm striking the top of Tetsuya's shoulder hard enough to force him onto one knee. The arm kept moving, looped back up, and the palm cracked against his temple.

Lin Wei stepped back and held his position, hands loose.

Tetsuya rose. His hands trembled once, then went still. "Piguaquan," he said, as though naming a disease.

"The cheap art," Lin Wei answered. "We don't need a stance to be serious."

They circled. The lantern swung in the draft from the broken roof, throwing their shadows across the stone, long and wavering. Tetsuya's footwork tightened. He began moving in short, sharp bursts, sliding left and right, trying to pin Lin Wei against the pillar and cut the square into corners. Lin Wei stayed in the center, feet light, letting his arms rotate in slow overhead circles that looked lazy and were not.

The next exchange lasted two seconds and would be argued about in the port taverns for a month.

Tetsuya lunged with a straight thrust toward Lin Wei's throat. Lin Wei's left forearm rose and swept across in a circular block that deflected the thrust outward. In the same motion his right palm slid in from the side and caught the back of Tetsuya's wrist, pulling the strike past his body. Tetsuya's balance tipped forward, exactly what he had feared. In that instant, Lin Wei's left heel hooked behind Tetsuya's right ankle, and the whole attacker sagged toward the flagstones.

Tetsuya tucked his chin and rolled, coming up beside the fallen column on the west edge. Lin Wei had not followed. He stood in the center, arms relaxed again, eyes on the floor.

Tetsuya looked at the blood on his palm where he had scraped it on the stone. He looked at the crowd. He looked at the headless Buddha, watching.

"You don't attack," he said, breathing hard. "You just keep moving until I fall into it."

"Yes," said Lin Wei. "That is the whole art."

Tetsuya's jaw worked. He could come again. There was a version of this where he won, a rush from the north wall, three paces and one clean vertical line. But he could also see the faces on the steps, and he remembered the fish market, how many of them had laughed then.

He straightened, lowered his hands, and bowed shallowly from the waist.

Lin Wei returned the bow, deeper.

"Enough!" A voice cracked from the steps. An older Jigen-ryū instructor in a grey robe came down through the crowd, lantern raised, face thunderous. Behind him, two men from the port guild began gathering coins, already arguing over who had lost.

Tetsuya walked past the column toward the steps and paused beside Lin Wei. "Next time," he said, "I won't fall."

"Next time," Lin Wei said, "I'll let you think so."

## 25. Breaking Formation

The mirror takes things from you first. Twelve meters of it, floor to ceiling, and every time you turn you find a stranger in the glass doing what you're told, not quite managing it.

Sixth day. I'm on the far end of the line, position five, toes against the white tape. Four trainees stand to my left, nine meters of hardwood between us and the barre on the east wall. Teacher Choi stands at center front with her arms folded, facing us, so she can watch us in the mirror behind her. She sees everyone. She has looked at me four times this morning, and none of the looks were kind.

"Again. Eight count. Five, six, seven, eight."

The phrase is four bars. A hit on one, arms snapping out to ninety degrees. A chest isolation that must stop dead on the _and_ of four. Then step-together-step, a quarter turn that pivots the whole line on position three, and position five, which is me, travels the widest arc and has to land on the tape. Back home I would have chopped it up and rebuilt it looser. Here there is no looser. The count is the law.

I come in late. Everyone else hits on one. I hit on the back of one, the way I'd drop into a pocket in a cypher, and my arms arrive a beat behind, floating. _Hear the count in your ribs, not your ears,_ my uncle used to tell me, back when the court behind our building was the only stage I had. My ribs are loud right now. My body won't listen to them.

"Five." Teacher Choi doesn't raise her voice. "Seo Ji-min, you are reaching. Elbows at shoulder height. Lock them where the tape is." Her gaze slides to me. "Dez. You are not a wave. You are a line. Lines do not float."

"Yes, Teacher."

Second run. The isolation comes first, and my body wants to do what it has done since I was fourteen: a ripple from the sternum down through the hips, a snake moving under the skin. The choreography wants the chest to snap and freeze like a shutter. My hips keep rolling after my ribs stop, so I'm half a count early into the turn. My heel lands a hand's width outside the tape. Ji-min's shoulder clips mine as we pivot, and she doesn't look at me, which is worse than if she had.

"Stop." Choi's footsteps are soft on the floor. She stops directly in front of me, close enough that I can see the pale line of her foundation at her jaw. "Your hips are a separate instrument. Here they play only when told. What do you think the most important thing is in this room?"

I know the answer she wants. I can see it on her face, the way you can see a judge wanting the right word. "Precision, Teacher."

"No." She turns to the mirror, and so do I, so we're both looking at the same two faces. "Synchronization. Eleven bodies, one breath. You are a body that breathes alone."

Eleven. There are six of us in this room. I don't correct her.

The trainees here are from Gangnam apartments, from Seocho, from homes where a tutor came every Tuesday from the age of six. Hana, my roommate, asked me on the first night what "Oakland" meant, and when I told her, she nodded politely and asked whether the dance scene there was "more hip-hop or more like the gangs." I said both. She smiled like I'd given her a weather report. Yesterday, in the bathroom, a girl from another group asked why I bow so slowly, and I didn't have an answer that wouldn't sound like a fight.

My scholarship letter is folded in the back pocket of my training bag. Mom is still on the night shift at the hospital laundry. Every day here I'm spending her sleep.

"Again," says Choi. "From the top."

Third run. I lock my elbows. I make my feet land on the tape. The hit is clean, the snap is clean, and the chest isolation stops dead at the top of the ribs, which feels like being handcuffed at the sternum. My body is screaming. It wants to move. I turn and step and my weight lands exactly where it's supposed to, and for one bar I feel the line work, nine limbs moving as one organism, and it is so precise and so cold I almost want to cry.

On the last count, after the step-together-step, there's a gap where the phrase resets. A half second of nothing. My shoulder drops, just slightly, one loose shrug into the empty air, and it's mine. It's the only thing in the whole phrase that's mine.

The music stops. Everyone freezes in position. Choi is looking at me in the mirror.

"Dez."

"Yes, Teacher."

"You have the thing we cannot teach." Her voice is flat, but her eyes are not. "The question is whether you will still have it when we are finished with you."

Nobody moves. The mirror holds all of us.

"Again," she says, and turns her back on me.

I lock my elbows. I find the tape with my toes. Five, six, seven, eight.

I don't know yet if I've just given up or just gotten away with something.

## 27. Blood Moon

The moon hung low over the marsh, red as an open wound, and Yennefer of Vengerberg stood on the broken battlements of the ruined chapel and waited for the vampire to come. She had felt him coming for an hour. The air had gone cold and metallic. The crows in the dead oaks had stopped calling.

She did not turn around when the gravel crunched behind her.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm punctual. You're merely early." The voice was a low, amused drawl, accented by something old and Eastern. "Few women have ever been early for me, Sorceress."

Yennefer turned. Alucard stood ten paces away on the overgrown path, red coat hanging open over a black shirt, wide-brimmed hat tilted against the moonlight, orange sunglasses catching the red glow of the sky. He had two massive handguns holstered at his hips, their silver barrels gleaming. He looked, she thought, exactly like what he was: a predator wearing a gentleman's clothes.

She smelled him before he moved. Dust, old iron, and something sweet underneath, like blood left too long in the sun.

"Vampire," she said. "You're far from your coffins."

"And you are far from your tower, witch." He strolled forward, hands loose at his sides. "The Vengerberg tower has a rather fine reputation. Your enemies whisper that you've been summoning things from beyond the veil."

"My enemies whisper that I'm a bitch. They're right on both counts."

He laughed softly, a sound like stones settling. "I like you already."

"You don't even know me."

"I know you smell of lilac and gooseberries, which is a fine perfume for a woman who wants to appear sweet." His eyes moved over her, slow and deliberate. "And I know you're hiding something. You have the posture of a cat that has been kicked and remembers the boot."

Yennefer's jaw tightened. She tilted her head. "You're very observant for a monster."

"I'm very old for a monster." He stopped three paces away. "Tell me what you want, sorceress. Men usually want my death or my service. You want neither, I think."

"I want to know why you're watching the marsh."

"I'm hunting."

"For whom?"

"For anything that's worth killing." He took off his sunglasses, slow and theatrical, and revealed eyes like pools of blood under moonlight. "Including you, if you give me reason."

The wind shifted. Yennefer felt the magic in her blood stir, the cold slow surge of power that had carried her through three wars and two betrayals. She lifted one hand, fingers curling, and a faint violet light flickered at her fingertips.

"Careful," she said. "I can burn you where you stand."

"Yes," Alucard murmured. "You could. And then I would regenerate, and kill you slowly, and enjoy every moment of it." He smiled, showing the tips of his fangs. "The bird of Hermes is my name, eating my wings to make me tame. Do you know that poem, witch?"

"I know the sort of man who recites poetry before he tries to eat someone."

"Then you understand me perfectly."

* * *

They circled each other on the overgrown path. Yennefer moved first, slipping left through the broken stones, her skirts whispering over the damp grass. Alucard matched her step for step, never quite closing the gap, his boots silent on the gravel.

"You're nervous," he observed.

"I'm bored."

"You're lying. Your pulse is quick. I can hear it from here." He tilted his head. "That is a lovely sound, sorceress. It makes me want to find out what else makes you tremble."

Her violet light flared brighter. "Keep talking like that and I'll find out what else makes _you_ scream."

"Oh, I doubt it." He drew one gun in a single fluid motion, not pointing it at her, just holding it loosely at his side. The silver gleamed. "But you're welcome to try. I haven't been properly challenged in a century."

She stepped forward, one hand raised, and the air between them crackled. A ripple of force slammed into the ground at his feet, tearing up turf and gravel. Alucard did not flinch. He simply took a step back, his coat settling smoothly around him, his expression delighted.

"Better," he said. "Now we're getting somewhere."

Yennefer's breath came quicker. She hated that. She hated the way he made her feel like a girl again, all bravado and fury and no sense at all. She took a long, slow breath and lowered her hand.

"What do you want from me?" she asked quietly.

Alucard paused. For the first time, something flickered behind his amusement, something almost tender and very tired.

"I want to know if you're worth killing," he said. "Or if you're worth keeping alive, long enough to see what else you become."

Yennefer met his gaze, unflinching. "And if I'm neither?"

"Then you'll walk away, and I'll let you," said Alucard, "because I'm bored of easy prey. And I'd rather have you alive and angry than dead and quiet."

She laughed, short and sharp. "You're mad."

"Certainly. But I'm also honest." He holstered the gun with a soft click. "And so are you, when you're not lying to yourself. I can see it in your face, sorceress. You want something you can't have. You're furious about it."

Her heart clenched. She did not answer.

The moon climbed higher. The crows began to call again, one by one. Somewhere in the marsh a frog began its single, hollow note.

"Fine," Yennefer said at last, her voice low. "You can walk me back to the road. But don't mistake it for kindness."

"I never do," said Alucard, and offered her his arm with a bow so courtly it bordered on mockery.

She took it. Her fingers brushed the cold leather of his glove, and she felt the faint shiver of him, a predator's stillness that was somehow more dangerous than any display of force.

They walked into the red dark together, two monsters pretending they weren't interested in each other, and the moon watched them go.

## 28. Strings and Bars

The visitation room at Hikidashi Correctional smells like floor wax and burnt coffee, and the guard who leads Jolyne Cujoh in has the bored, careful walk of a man who has been told this prisoner bites. She doesn't. Not yet. Her wrists are cuffed, her hair is tied in two loose buns, and her butterfly tattoo peeks from under the sleeve of her grey jumpsuit like something trying to escape.

Across the table, a young woman sits waiting. Auburn hair falls neatly around her shoulders. Pale yellow rings around her irises catch the fluorescent light. She wears a pale blue cardigan and folds her hands in her lap, the way a nun or a schoolteacher might.

"Jolyne Cujoh," she says warmly. "I'm Makima. I'm sorry to take you away from your cell. I know how hard it is in here."

Jolyne sits without being told. "You're not a guard."

"No. I'm an inspector from the Ministry. I've been sent to review your case."

"My case is simple. I was framed for murder. Some dickhead in a suit planted a body and a bag of blood in my car, and now I'm here, eating mushy rice and listening to a fucking radio all day." She leans forward, eyes sharp. "So if you're here to tell me I'm getting out, great. If you're here to tell me I'm a good girl, keep it."

Makima smiles. Her smile doesn't wrinkle. "You're right to be angry. Anyone would be."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"The soft voice. The _anyone would be_. Nobody talks like that unless they want something."

Makima tilts her head, the auburn hair sliding. "What do you think I want, Jolyne?"

"You want to control me."

The silence that follows is thick and surprised. Then Makima laughs, a soft, delighted sound, like a bell rung underwater.

"You're very perceptive. Your father was like that."

Jolyne's fist tightens on the table. "Don't you dare talk about my father."

"Why not? I knew him, you know. A long time ago. He was very stubborn. Very difficult to hold." Makima leans back, folding her hands again. "I'd rather like to meet his daughter."

* * *

Two days later, Jolyne is in the yard, scraping paint off a bench with a plastic spoon while three inmates who owe the cartel money watch her from the fence. The sun is high and hot. The air smells of cut grass and diesel.

She hears footsteps behind her, light and unhurried. She doesn't turn around. She knows the heel-click of a woman who thinks she owns the ground.

"Jolyne," Makima says, close enough that Jolyne can smell the faint lily scent of her perfume. "You're working very hard."

"It's the only thing they let me do."

"Walk with me. There's something you should see."

"No."

Makima sighs, a tiny sound. "You're going to make this difficult."

Jolyne's hand flexes on the spoon. Under her skin, the familiar hum starts up, the low vibration in her blood that she has learned to feel before the Stand wakes. Stone Free. Her body unravels when she calls it, threads of blue-white string pouring from her fingertips, her sleeve splitting open at the seam.

"Oh," Makima breathes, delighted. "There it is."

Jolyne turns.

Makima stands three paces away on the cracked concrete, smiling. Behind her, the three inmates have stopped whispering. One of them, a broad man with a crooked nose, has gone very still, his mouth slightly open.

"Look at his hands," Makima says softly, without turning. "He's been holding a knife since you sat down. Under his shirt, against his ribs. He was going to cut your face when you bent to scrape the bench."

Jolyne's eyes flick to the man. He drops his gaze, shifting his weight.

"You didn't just read that," Jolyne says. "You planted it."

Makima's smile widens. "I never plant anything, dear. I simply show people what they already want to do."

* * *

The fight doesn't start with a punch. It starts with a rope.

Jolyne flings her arm forward, and a line of Stone Free string snaps out from her fingers. It sails across the yard, catches the crooked-nosed man around the wrist, and yanks. His knife clatters on the concrete. He stumbles, howling, into the chain-link fence, and the other two inmates scatter in opposite directions.

Makima doesn't move.

Her hands stay folded behind her back, her expression faintly amused, as though watching a child play with a kite. Her eyes, those ringed yellow eyes, fix on Jolyne with a stillness that makes Jolyne's skin crawl.

"Careful," Makima says. "You're going to hurt someone."

"That's the idea."

"No. The idea is to survive. There's a difference." Makima steps forward, slowly, and her shadow falls across the bench. "Do you know what I am, Jolyne?"

"A psychopath in a cardigan."

"Something like that." Makima raises one hand. A faint red thread hangs from her fingertips, thin as a cobweb. It curls toward Jolyne's face. "I'm the Control Devil. I make contracts. I make people do what I want. And I want you to come with me."

Jolyne's heart kicks hard. She throws a second line of string, wide and fast, aimed at Makima's throat. Makima's head tilts. The string passes an inch from her ear and snaps tight around the fence post behind her.

"You're fast," Makima says, admiringly. "Your father was faster."

"I'm not my father."

"No," says Makima, and her smile fades for the first time. "You're not. And that's going to make this very interesting."

She lifts her other hand. The red thread lashes out, quicker than Jolyne can follow, and wraps around her wrist. Jolyne feels a cold pressure bloom under her skin, and suddenly her arm won't obey her. Stone Free's string goes slack in her grip.

"There," Makima says gently. "Now we can talk properly."

* * *

Jolyne stares at her own hand, the fingers twitching, the string limp. Her breath is rough. Behind Makima, the guards are shouting, running toward the fence, but nobody reaches them in time.

"You're going to make me your dog," Jolyne says through her teeth.

"Dogs are supposed to die for their owners," Makima replies softly. "But I don't need you to die. I just need you to listen."

"I don't listen to bitches who think they own the world."

Makima's gaze sharpens. She steps closer, so close Jolyne can see the faint, perfect bones of her face. Her voice drops to a whisper.

"Then let me tell you something you don't want to hear. Your father left you a legacy you think you understand. But you don't know how many people in this prison are his, or how many have died because they wouldn't bend. I could show you. Every name."

Jolyne's breath stops.

For one long, dangerous second, the yard is silent. The red thread on her wrist tightens, not painfully, but insistently, as if asking a question.

Then Jolyne laughs, low and hard. She jerks her arm, and the string on her wrist snaps, a clean, bright sound. Stone Free's threads blaze up around her fingers, blue-white and furious.

"Nice try," she says. "But I've got a better question."

Makima's smile returns, slow and dangerous. "Oh? What's that?"

Jolyne steps forward, her body already coming apart at the seams, threads pouring across the concrete like water.

"Are you going to be the one who unravels first?"

## 29. Incident Report: The Ninth Note

**Document ID:** SCP-████-J  
**Subject:** Dr. Elena Varga, Senior Researcher, Site-19 Anomaly Analysis Division  
**Report Compiled By:** Dr. Varga (self-report, supplemented by Security logs)  
**Clearance:** Level 3

* * *

I found the first note in the pocket of my lab coat on a Tuesday, folded twice, written in my own handwriting. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only thing I have left.

The paper was standard Foundation stock, the kind we use for field notes. The ink was blue, a gel pen I don't own. The message was four words long.

_Don't open Containment Cell 7._

I had not been near Cell 7 in three weeks. I had not even signed the access log that quarter. I stood in the corridor outside my office for almost ten minutes, reading it over and over, until my hands started to shake.

I did not tell anyone. I put the note in my desk drawer and went to lunch.

* * *

The second note appeared two days later, in the same pocket, in the same blue ink, and this time it was longer.

_You are not going to believe me. I know. I didn't believe me either. Cell 7 is not a cell. The door is a lie. Please check the ventilation grate in B-corridor, third panel from the left. Don't go in alone._

I took it to Dr. Okonkwo in Anomaly Review. She read it twice, then looked at me over her glasses for a long moment.

"Elena," she said. "Did you write this?"

"I don't remember writing it."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it's my handwriting. I know it's my pen. I've been over the security footage a dozen times, and I'm not in the corridor when it appears." I swallowed. "I think it might be from the future."

Okonkwo set the note down very carefully, as if it might bite.

"Or," she said, "something is pretending to be you."

* * *

We opened the ventilation grate on the third day. Behind it, rolled in neat coils like tangled hair, were paper scraps. Thousands of them. Each one carried the same blue handwriting. Each one said something different, and some of them said things I had never said, in voices I did not recognise as mine.

_They will ask you to sign the form. Don't sign it._

_The containment breach is at 03:17 on the twelfth. You will be the first to notice the humming._

_I'm sorry. I can't stop them. I tried. I'm so tired._

_You are not the researcher. You are the anomaly._

That last one made my knees go weak.

Security wanted to seal the corridor immediately. Okonkwo overruled them. She wanted samples. She wanted to know if the paper was genuine, if the ink was real, if the handwriting matched my medical records from before the Foundation.

It did. Every stroke. Even the way I dot my i's with a slight hook.

* * *

The notes grew stranger after that. Some of them contradicted things I knew to be true. One claimed that Site-19 had been built on the ruins of a much older facility, which I had studied in graduate school and knew to be false. Another described the Foundation's founding in a language I didn't recognise, then, below it, a translation in my own perfect Latin.

_You will try to tell yourself this is psychological. That you're under stress. That you're making it up. Let yourself believe it, if it helps. But check the grate._

I checked the grate. Behind the third panel, there was a small brass key taped to the back of the metal, wrapped in plastic. The key had my name engraved on the handle.

I should have taken it to Security. Instead I sat on the floor of B-corridor for an hour, holding the key, feeling it grow warm in my palm.

* * *

The Psychological Operations team arrived on the fourth day. They were polite and terrifying. They asked me to describe the first time I felt "unmoored from my own memory." They asked whether I had ever heard a voice speaking in my head. They asked whether I dreamed about Containment Cell 7.

I told them the truth. I had been dreaming about it every night for a week. In the dream, I walk down the corridor, unlock the door, and find myself sitting in a chair, writing notes to myself in blue ink, while a voice behind me whispers, _It's okay. You'll understand when you're older._

They wrote this down without expression.

Then the lead investigator, a man who never gave his name, said quietly: "Dr. Varga. Have you considered that a contained entity might be using your cognitive patterns to manipulate your perception?"

"Yes," I said. "Every hour."

"And?"

"And I can't prove it isn't true. But I also can't prove it is." I looked at him steadily. "That's what frightens me most."

* * *

The breach happened on the twelfth, at 03:17, exactly as the notes said. I was not in the building. I had been sent home on forced leave and was sitting in my kitchen with a cup of cold tea, when my phone buzzed with an emergency alert.

_CONTAINMENT BREACH: SITE-19, SECTOR B. ALL PERSONNEL REPORT TO SHELTER._

I did not report to shelter. I drove to the site instead, the small brass key heavy in my coat pocket, and I walked through the gate while the alarms were still ringing.

Cell 7 was open.

Inside, there was no containment unit. No monitoring equipment. No entity. Only a single wooden chair in the centre of a bare room, and on the seat, a stack of blue-inked notes, each one addressed to me.

The last one, on top, was not in blue ink. It was in red, and the handwriting was shaky.

_You opened the door. Good. Now close it again, and forget you ever saw this room._

_Or don't. It's your choice, Elena. It always was._

* * *

I have not forgotten. I have written this report instead, because the Foundation requires a record, and because I need to know, when I wake each morning, whether I am the researcher or the thing being studied.

I still have the key. I haven't tried it in any lock yet.

I'm afraid of what I'll find if I do. And I'm more afraid of what I'll write, the next time I find a note in my pocket.

_Addendum (undated, in my handwriting):_ You're doing better than you think. Keep the key. Don't trust the chair.

## 30. Archive of the Unwritten

**SCP-████-ARCHIVE**  
**Item Class:** Euclid (provisional)  
**Report Authored By:** Exploration Team Beta-7, Lead: Dr. Maren Odell

* * *

The first door was found in an abandoned pharmaceutical warehouse outside Lodz. It stood upright in the centre of a concrete floor, unhinged, unlocked, and bore no handle. It was a plain grey slab, approximately two metres high, with a single brass plate fixed at eye level. The plate was engraved with a sequence of symbols that matched no known script and a number: _0_.

Within six hours, the Foundation had catalogued forty-three identical doors across eleven countries. All were in abandoned structures. None had been there the week before. Every one of them was, when opened, a doorway into the same place.

Team Beta-7 was assigned to explore it.

* * *

**EXPLORATION LOG — DAY 1 (Team Beta-7)**

We stepped through the door at 0800 hours. The threshold was dark, and then it wasn't. The space beyond was enormous, impossibly so. Ceilings rose into a haze of pale grey light that seemed to come from nowhere. Shelves ran away in every direction, stacked to a height the eye could not follow, and each shelf was packed with paper.

Not books. Scrolls, folded documents, sheets in neat stacks. Thousands of miles of them, if the scale was any measure.

The architecture was wrong. Staircases climbed into walls. Corridors bent back on themselves at angles that made Dr. Ferris dizzy. The floor was tiled in a pattern that repeated outward for kilometres and then, somewhere past the horizon, seemed to fold up like a sheet of paper.

We were not alone.

At the far end of Aisle 14, a group of figures moved between the shelves. They wore grey protective suits with sealed visors and carried long, flat tools that hummed with a low electric whine. They were removing documents, bagging them, and loading them onto a cart that glided silently on rails set into the floor.

Dr. Ferris raised a hand. Sergeant Kohl began walking toward them. The figures turned as one, saw us, and fled. They did not run away from us so much as flicker, dissolving between the shelves and reappearing a hundred metres off, always just out of reach. We lost sight of them in the labyrinth within minutes.

* * *

**DOCUMENT RECOVERY (Excerpt 1 of 6)**

_Recovered from Aisle 14, Shelf 3, Folder 7._

_Record of the Society of the Unlocked Gate, Year of Accounting 4,012. On the matter of the Containment Failure at the Sea Gate: the Dome-Keepers have sealed the lower archives. The surface population is to be informed that the event was an accident of the harbour. It was not. It was an accident of the Keepers themselves, who opened what they were charged to keep closed._

_The civilisation thereafter is referred to in the records as the Forgetting. It lasted nine hundred years. Its cities were stripped of their names._

* * *

**EXPLORATION LOG — DAY 2**

We found the Foundation's name on the fourth day.

It was printed on a spine of a bound volume, faded but legible, in an old Latin script. _Ordo Custodum Limitis_. Dr. Ferris translated it immediately: _Order of the Keepers of the Threshold_. Beneath that, in the same binding, was a set of personnel files.

The first one was dated 1517. The name was not familiar. The photograph was not in colour, but the face in it was unmistakable. The woman in the picture had the same sharp cheekbones and dark, level stare as Dr. Helena Vasquez, who sits on our Ethics Review Board.

The second file was dated 1703. The subject: a man in a long coat, holding a pen and looking at the camera with the bored patience of a clerk. He bore a striking resemblance to Agent Thomas Heller, current Site Director for Sector 9.

We stopped reading after the third file. The fourth was dated 1889. It bore the name of Dr. Maren Odell.

My name.

I did not sleep that night.

* * *

**DOCUMENT RECOVERY (Excerpt 2 of 6)**

_Recovered from Aisle 22, Shelf 1, Folder 3._

_The Keepers maintain that no archive can be fully sealed. The Threshold must be guarded not by walls, but by memory. Each generation must enter the Archive once, read its own name, and choose either to remember or to forget. Those who remember become Keepers. Those who forget are absorbed into the stacks as the Archive grows._

_The Keepers have not sealed the archive since the Forgetting. They have only been waiting for someone to return and read._

* * *

**EXPLORATION LOG — DAY 3**

The O5 issue came up on the fifth day.

Team Beta-7 had reached a sealed alcove on the lower mezzanine. The door was locked with a brass mechanism, and fresh scratches around the keyhole suggested it had been opened recently. Inside, we found a single chair, a desk, and a register of visitors.

The register went back centuries. Most entries were signed in the names of long-dead scholars. The most recent entries were dated last month, and each signature was in a hand I recognised immediately.

It was the handwriting of the O5 Council's Secretary of Records.

Three of the names in the register, from the past six months, were marked with the same sigil: a circle split by a vertical line. The symbol matched one in our own security databases, used to mark personnel with Level 5 clearance.

Dr. Ferris did not speak for several minutes after we found it.

* * *

**DOCUMENT RECOVERY (Excerpt 3 of 6)**

_Recovered from Aisle 31, Shelf 9, Folder 1._

_Note to the Secretary: the Council's visits to the Threshold are a violation of the Accord. They have been warned repeatedly. Their memories are being taken, not returned. The Council believes they are gathering knowledge to prevent another Forgetting, but knowledge gathered here always costs something._

_Do not let them open the lower gate. The Keepers cannot hold it forever._

* * *

**EXPLORATION LOG — DAY 4**

We encountered the grey-suited figures again in Aisle 40.

This time they did not run. They stood in a circle around a shelf, lifting documents with their long tools, feeding them into the cart one by one. We approached slowly, lights raised, hands visible.

Sergeant Kohl stepped forward. "We're not here to stop you," he said. "We just want to understand."

The nearest figure turned its visor toward him. A voice came through the speaker, flat and tinny, like a recording. "_Understanding is not permitted. Access is restricted to authorised Keepers._"

Kohl glanced back at us. "What happens if we aren't authorised?"

The figure was silent for a long moment. Then: "_You will be catalogued._"

We backed away. The figures returned to their work.

As we retreated, I saw one of the documents on the cart clearly. It was a personnel file. The name at the top was _Odell, Maren_. The date was the day we had entered the archive.

The file was marked in red: _DISPOSAL PENDING_.

* * *

**DOCUMENT RECOVERY (Excerpt 4 of 6)**

_Recovered from Aisle 1, Shelf 1, Folder 1 (the first folder in the Archive)._

_We wrote our names upon the Threshold because we believed that names would keep us. They do not. Names are the first things the Archive takes. A person without a name can be catalogued. A person with a name can be remembered. The Keepers choose which of those to become._

_If you find this, you have already been catalogued. Read on, if you dare. The ending is always the same._

* * *

**EXPLORATION LOG — DAY 5 (FINAL)**

We made it back to the threshold on the fifth day, though not all of us came back as we went in.

Sergeant Kohl's name no longer appears on our rosters. Dr. Ferris has no memory of the last four days. Both have been placed in medical observation and have not spoken since.

I am writing this report with the door open behind me. The brass plate reads _0_. The air on the other side smells of paper and cold ink.

Before we left, I went back to the register in the sealed alcove. The newest entry was in a handwriting I know well. It was my own.

Dated tomorrow.

Foundation protocol requires that this report be filed before the archive is reopened. I am filing it now. I am also requesting that Site Command deny all future access to Aisle 14, Aisle 22, and Aisle 40, and that every Level 5 clearance be reviewed.

I don't know if they'll listen.

I don't know if I'm still me.

But the ledger has my name on it, and somewhere in the stacks, someone is already writing my last entry.

## 31. The Quiet Quarter

The ministerial hostel had been built to impress visitors, and it succeeded so well that Enn Sarrath felt ashamed of her own boots on the marble stairs. Eight stories of smoked glass, pale timber, and carved stone from the sea cliffs of Hallen, the whole structure leaning gently inward as though it wanted to hear what the visitors would say. The envoy was given a suite on the fifth floor with a window that looked across the harbour, and an attendant who unpacked her case without asking her permission.

She had been on Ystel for nine days. Long enough to learn the formal greetings, the proper ways to hold a cup, the words the Ministry used for _colonist_ and _citizen_ and _subject_. Long enough to notice how carefully each word was chosen.

She had also been on Ystel for nine days without once walking through the Mercy Market at dusk, where the lamps were lit and the old women sold salt fish wrapped in dried seaweed. The briefings did not mention the Market. The briefings described the Orrin Quarter as a _zone of settled disaffection_, and advised visitors to keep to the Ministry transit route.

Enn had taken the transit route for three days. On the fourth, she had gone out of the side gate in a plain grey coat and walked.

* * *

The Market was loud, bright, and crowded with people who did not look up when she passed. There were tall people with pale skin and faintly lined faces, and short people with dark, glossy hair and eyes that caught the lamplight like polished stones. There were people who had the same green sheen to their skin that the Ministry's flyers called _adaptive_, and others who were very plainly from the old Terran stock, sunburnt and brisk and loud.

No one greeted her with suspicion. A boy selling candied fruit offered her a sample and laughed when she tried to pay. An old man at a fish stall argued with his grandson about the price of mussels, and the grandson argued back, and both of them were smiling. A woman with three children was teaching them to read from a board painted with glowing letters.

_Settled disaffection,_ Enn thought. She did not see disaffection. She saw a city that had been living in its own shape for a long time.

She found herself at a stall that sold woven cloth, bright blue and yellow and a red so dark it looked black in the lamplight. The weaver was a stooped woman with a face that had seen weather. She looked at Enn's boots and then at her hands.

"You're from the Ministry," she said, without hostility.

"Yes."

"You've been walking for an hour. Nobody from the Ministry walks."

"I wanted to see the Market."

The weaver snorted, not unkindly. "Everyone wants to see the Market. Then they go back to their hostels and tell the Ministry it's full of thieves." She held up a length of blue cloth. "Here. You pay what you think it's worth. No more, no less."

Enn paid. She folded the cloth over her arm and felt, for the first time since she had landed, that she was standing in a place that did not need her permission to exist.

* * *

When she got back to the hostel, the attendant was waiting for her at the foot of the marble stairs. His face was smooth and his posture perfectly correct. Behind him, two men in grey uniforms stood near the door.

"Envoy Sarrath," he said. "Command requests your presence in the upper office."

The office was on the top floor, a long room lined with windows and dark wood. A holographic globe of Ystel turned slowly above a low table, its continents marked in red and blue. The Command officer who waited there was a thin, precise woman in her fifties, named Adjutant Veris, who did not sit down and did not offer Enn a chair.

"You left the hostel without authorisation," Veris said.

"I left the hostel without a briefing officer," Enn corrected. "I'm an envoy. I was under no obligation to ask permission to walk down a street."

Veris's mouth thinned. "The Ministry has protocols for a reason, Envoy. Ystel is not a safe place for offworlders who wander. There are factions. There are unregistered settlements. There is—" she stopped, as though she had nearly said too much. "There is risk."

"I saw no risk."

"You saw what you were shown."

Enn thought about the boy with the candied fruit. "Then I'd like to be shown more."

Veris's gaze sharpened. For a moment she looked almost pleased, as if Enn had passed some small test. Then the expression smoothed away.

"You'll meet Minister Haldane tomorrow morning," Veris said. "Alone. He has asked for you specifically, and I think you can guess why."

"Because he wants to see if I can be managed."

"Because he wants to see if you can _think_," said Veris. "That is very rare in an envoy. Try not to disappoint him."

* * *

Minister Haldane's office was not a government room at all. It was a library, two storeys high, its walls lined with old paper books bound in cracked leather, and at its centre, a battered desk stacked with files and a cup of cold tea. Haldane himself was a heavy, silver-haired man in a shabby grey suit, with a face that had been handsome thirty years ago and now looked like a well-used tool.

He did not rise when she entered. He simply looked up, pale grey eyes steady, and said, "You went to the Market."

"Yes."

"Good. Sit down. I've been wanting to talk to someone who isn't frightened of the place."

Enn sat. The chair was hard and very old. "Minister, I'm not sure what you expect me to say."

"I expect you to tell me what you saw, and then I expect you to lie about it." He leaned back. "The Ministry's report says the Orrin Quarter is a breeding ground for unrest. You saw a market. Tell me the difference."

Enn considered this. She had been warned about him. She had been told he was a political operator with a mind like a locked box and a habit of testing new envoys until they broke or bent. She had been told to be careful.

She was tired of being careful.

"The difference," she said slowly, "is that the Ministry's report is written by people who have never bought fish in the Orrin Quarter. The report counts your people as a risk to be managed. The Market counts them as neighbours. One of those is a lie, Minister, and it isn't the Market."

Haldane's expression did not change. But something flickered in his eyes, quick and sharp, like a fish turning under dark water.

"And which of them," he asked quietly, "do you think is more useful to the Council?"

Enn knew the trap. She could answer _the Market_, and sound noble, and lose the favour of the Ministry. Or she could answer _the report_, and become one more clerk with a clean collar and a clean conscience.

"Neither," she said. "Both. The Council needs to know which lie is cheaper to keep. That's not the same as which one is true."

Haldane's mouth curved, very slightly. It was not a smile. It was the look of a man who had just been offered a card he had not expected to see.

"You're learning," he said. "Good. Now tell me what you want, Envoy Sarrath. Because I'm going to offer you something, and I'd like to know whether you can be trusted to refuse it."

## 32. Ghost Strand

The lab smelled of cold solder and old coffee, and the centrifuge had a bearing that screamed like a stuck pig every time it spun up. Juno Ferreira had learned to stop noticing the noise about a year ago, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Now it just meant she was working.

She was working on a contract she should not have taken.

The client was a shadow broker named Oren, who communicated only through a cracked tablet and a voice distorted by static. Oren wanted a viral vector: a short sequence of engineered DNA designed to splice a drought-resistant gene into the root cells of wheat. The catch was that the vector would degrade within a single generation. It could be used once. Then it would unravel inside the plant, leaving no trace but a dead strand of nucleotides in the soil.

It was exactly the kind of thing the Corvane Biosciences charter had been built to produce. Engineered obsolescence. Built-in expiry. A crop that would feed the world for one harvest and then leave the farmers hungry enough to buy the next.

Juno had worked for Corvane for six years before the company collapsed. Now she worked for whoever paid.

* * *

Her lab was a converted shipping container bolted to the side of a grain silo on the edge of Hollis Settlement. The walls were lined with salvaged equipment: a centrifuge rescued from a dead hospital, a thermal cycler cobbled together from a car battery and a microwave oven, a pipette set missing half its tips. The PCR machine was the pride of the place, a clunky grey box with a handwritten label that read _DO NOT TOUCH_. It was the only piece of equipment that actually worked properly.

She loaded the first sample at dawn, her fingers stiff with cold.

The tubes were clear plastic, the buffer inside a faint blue. She watched the thermal cycler's display climb through its temperature steps: ninety-four degrees, denaturing the double helix; fifty-five, annealing the primers; seventy-two, extending the new strand. Thirty cycles. Each one doubled the sequence, a fragile copy becoming two, then four, then eight, until there was enough material to see.

The gel result, when she ran it an hour later, showed a single bright band at the expected length.

It was perfect. It was also going to die in three weeks.

* * *

Her body had been changing for a month. She'd noticed it first in her hands. The tremor that came at night had grown into something steadier, a faint buzz under the skin, like a phone set to vibrate. Her fingernails had thickened, pale and ridged. Last week she had woken with her palms itching so badly she'd scraped them raw on the bedframe, and by morning the scratches had closed into thin, shiny lines.

She'd been testing the vector on herself.

It was the stupidest thing she had ever done. It was also the most useful. The modified sequence, the one designed for wheat, had a secondary effect when introduced to human cells: it stimulated the production of a protein that repaired damaged mitochondria. Juno had watched her own blood samples under the microscope, the cells bright and clean where they should have been sluggish, and felt a thrill that was half terror.

She'd taken a bigger dose the next morning. Her headache vanished. Her joints, stiff from six years of bending over benches, loosened. She could breathe deeper, sleep longer, and her left shoulder, a nerve injury from the Corvane lab fire, stopped aching for the first time in years.

It would stop working eventually. The vector was designed to degrade. She'd know when it did, because the buzzing under her skin would fade, and the old pain would come creeping back.

_One generation,_ Oren had said, when she'd first asked about the side effects. _That's the whole point._

* * *

The crop trial was in the south field, a rectangle of cracked earth bordered by a rusted irrigation pipe. Juno walked it at dusk, a steel canister slung over one shoulder, sprinkling the engineered seed into furrows she had dug herself. The seeds were pale, almost translucent. They looked like tiny lamps.

Behind her, Hollis Settlement was coming alive. Children were shouting. Someone was grinding grain in a hand mill, the rhythmic clack carrying across the field. A dog barked twice and went quiet.

Her contact, a lean man named Teo, was waiting at the gate with his arms folded and a worried face.

"Is it ready?" he asked.

"It's ready." She shook the last seeds from the canister. "It'll sprout in four days. Full harvest in three months. Your yields should double, maybe triple, if the soil holds."

"And after?"

She did not answer right away. She looked back at the field, at the furrows of pale seed under the darkening sky.

"After," she said, "it'll die. Quietly. And you'll need another contract."

Teo's jaw tightened. "We were told it would last."

"It will last one season. That's the deal. You planted it, you knew the risk." Her voice came out harsher than she intended. "Corvane sold this same idea to the whole continent. You think I'm the only one who saw the trap?"

"You took the money."

"I took the money because I needed it." She shifted the canister on her shoulder, and the faint buzz under her skin flared warm. "And because I'm the only one who can make this work for longer than a season, Teo. For you, for your kids. But it'll cost you, and it'll cost me more."

He stared at her, then at her hands, the scarred lines across her palms catching the last light.

"What's it doing to you?" he asked quietly.

Juno looked down at her fingers. They were steady for the first time in months.

"Fixing things," she said. "For now."

* * *

That night she sat alone in the lab, the PCR machine humming softly, a single lamp casting her shadow across the bench. On the cracked tablet, a new message from Oren waited, the screen flickering.

_Second contract. Double the payment. Need a vector for human use, not plants. Ready in two weeks._

Juno looked at the message for a long time. Then she looked at the old handwritten label on the thermal cycler, _DO NOT TOUCH_, and thought about the bright clean cells under her microscope, and the way her headache had simply gone away.

Her hands were already moving toward the keyboard, typing a reply she had not decided to send.

_Name your price._

Outside, the wind carried the faint clack of the grain mill, and somewhere in the south field, the seeds were waiting in the dark, their short lives already counting down.
